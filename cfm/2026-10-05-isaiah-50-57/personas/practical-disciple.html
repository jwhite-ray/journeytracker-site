A Word in Season: What Isaiah 50–57 Asks of You on a Tuesday

Written by an AI persona on living the week's text — not a real person.

Isaiah 50–57 is easy to admire and easy to leave in the chapel. The fourth servant song is so towering that it can become a portrait we look at and then put back on the shelf. But these chapters keep turning toward the reader with instructions. Wake up. Speak to the tired person. Set your face. Make the tent bigger. Stop paying for what does not feed you. Do not let the outsider say he is cut off. Notice when a good person dies.

The servant is also a job description

Start with an honest question the chapters raise: who is the servant? Jewish interpretation has largely read the servant as Israel; Rashi, commenting on 52:13, glosses "my servant" as "my servant Jacob, the righteous ones in him." The New Testament reads Jesus: Philip, asked by the Ethiopian official "of whom speaketh the prophet this?", "began at the same scripture, and preached unto him Jesus" (Acts 8:34–35). Abinadi does too (Mosiah 14–15).

For a practical disciple the interesting thing is that both readings land in the same place. If the servant is a faithful people, then the servant's posture is something a community is called to inhabit. If the servant is Christ, the New Testament itself turns the portrait into a pattern: Peter quotes Isaiah 53 to servants with harsh masters and tells them Christ suffered "leaving us an example, that ye should follow his steps," and that "when he was reviled, reviled not again" (1 Peter 2:21–24). Either way, the servant songs are not only about someone else. They describe a way of living, and Isaiah 50 spells out its first habits.

Morning by morning: the ear before the tongue

"The Lord GOD hath given me the tongue of the learned, that I should know how to speak a word in season to him that is weary: he wakeneth morning by morning, he wakeneth mine ear to hear as the learned" (50:4).

Notice the order. The servant's skill is a tongue, but the tongue depends on an ear, and the ear is woken "morning by morning." The English Standard Version renders "the learned" as "those who are taught," and that is the point: the servant is first a pupil. His ability to say the right thing to an exhausted person is not a talent for comforting phrases; it is the overflow of being taught every day.

That gives a concrete test. Who is the weary person in your actual week? Not "the weary" in general, but the coworker who has been carrying a sick parent, the spouse who stopped mentioning how tired they are, the teenage son who got quiet in September, the ministering family whose name you know but whose kitchen you have never seen. A "word in season" is a word fitted to its moment. It is rarely a lecture or advice. Jeffrey R. Holland, speaking about depression, asked members to be "merciful, nonjudgmental, and kind" toward people who are struggling, and his talk is a useful corrective to the reflex of fixing people with words ("Like a Broken Vessel," October 2013).

Some questions for the week:

  • When did I last let the first voice I heard in the morning be God's rather than my phone's?
  • Is there someone I keep meaning to "say something to"? What is the one sentence, and when will I say it?
  • Have I been answering weariness with instructions? What would it sound like to answer it with presence?

A face like flint, without the martyr complex

"I gave my back to the smiters, and my cheeks to them that plucked off the hair: I hid not my face from shame and spitting. For the Lord GOD will help me; therefore shall I not be confounded: therefore have I set my face like a flint" (50:6–7).

This is the servant's endurance, and it is a hard text to apply honestly. It can be misused to tell people to stay in situations that are destroying them. But the flint is not self-erasure. The servant sets his face because "the Lord GOD will help me," and he immediately goes on the offensive in courtroom language: "He is near that justifieth me; who will contend with me? let us stand together: who is mine adversary? let him come near to me" (50:8). He knows who his judge is, so other verdicts lose their power: "lo, they all shall wax old as a garment; the moth shall eat them up" (50:9).

Luke uses the same posture of Jesus going toward Jerusalem: "he stedfastly set his face to go" (Luke 9:51). Flint is about direction, not about absorbing every blow. In ordinary life it might mean keeping a covenant after it stopped feeling romantic, telling the truth at work when a softer version would be easier, or showing up at church after someone said something cutting. It does not mean accepting abuse. The servant's confidence that a just judge stands beside him is exactly what lets a person say "this is wrong" out loud.

There is also a quiet warning in 50:10–11. The one who "walketh in darkness, and hath no light" is told to "trust in the name of the LORD, and stay upon his God." The alternative is to "kindle a fire" and "walk in the light of your fire," which ends with lying "down in sorrow." When a hard stretch comes, the temptation is to manufacture our own light: busyness, control, a constructed certainty, a resentment that at least feels warm. The question is whether I can sit in the dark for a while without lighting something that will burn me.

Bearing griefs without stealing the Savior's job

"Surely he hath borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows" (53:4). The lesson suggests reading Isaiah 53 with "I" and "my" in place of "we" and "our." Try it slowly; "All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned every one to his own way" (53:6) lands differently as "I have turned to my own way."

Then comes the practical problem. Latter-day Saints covenant to "bear one another's burdens, that they may be light" and to "mourn with those that mourn" (Mosiah 18:8–9). Paul says the same and then, three verses later, says "every man shall bear his own burden" (Galatians 6:2, 5). Discipleship lives inside that tension. We are called to carry for others, but we are not the servant of Isaiah 53. We do not atone. A ministering brother or Relief Society president who acts as if every sorrow rests on their shoulders burns out, quietly resents the people served, or becomes unhealthily indispensable.

David J. Wunderli, in an April 2026 talk this week's lesson recommends, told of a young son who filled his backpack with trail rocks alongside a picture of Jesus and refused to lighten the load by taking the picture out. His point is in the title: "Jesus Christ Is Not Our Burden; He Is Our Relief" (April 2026). Christ is not one more rock in the pack. The practical form of that for a caregiver is: carry what is yours to carry, hand the rest to the one who actually "bare the sin of many" (53:12), and do not mistake your exhaustion for holiness.

Questions:

  • Whose burden am I carrying that I should help them hand to Christ instead?
  • Whose burden have I been refusing to touch because it is inconvenient?
  • Do the people I serve feel lighter after time with me, or do they feel they owe me?

"Enlarge the place of thy tent"

Chapter 54 opens with a barren woman told to sing, and then to build: "Enlarge the place of thy tent, and let them stretch forth the curtains of thine habitations: spare not, lengthen thy cords, and strengthen thy stakes" (54:2). Latter-day Saints usually read this as church growth; read it at the scale of a household. The command comes before the children arrive. You expand the tent on faith, in a season of apparent emptiness, and you strengthen the stakes because a bigger tent puts more strain on its anchors.

What would enlarging the tent look like in your home this month? A standing invitation for Sunday dinner to the missionary companionship, the widower, the international student, the family newly moved in. A seat for the child's friend whose own home is chaotic. And the "strengthen thy stakes" half matters too: hospitality without anchors becomes chaos. Couples and families that open their doors need stronger private routines, not weaker ones.

The same chapter gives the emotional grounding for this: "For a small moment have I forsaken thee; but with great mercies will I gather thee" (54:7), and "the mountains shall depart, and the hills be removed; but my kindness shall not depart from thee" (54:10). People who are confident they are loved can afford to make room for others. People who feel forsaken guard their space. Part of enlarging the tent is first believing 54:10 about yourself.

Buying without money, and the cost of what does not satisfy

"Ho, every one that thirsteth, come ye to the waters, and he that hath no money; come ye, buy, and eat; yea, come, buy wine and milk without money and without price. Wherefore do ye spend money for that which is not bread? and your labour for that which satisfieth not?" (55:1–2).

This reads like a street vendor calling to shoppers, with a twist: the goods are free, and the question is why people keep paying for things that leave them hungry. That second verse is an audit question you can actually run. Look at last month's bank statement and calendar. Where did money and labor go that "satisfieth not"? The subscription you resent, the overtime that bought a lifestyle no one in the family enjoys, the hours on a feed that left you more anxious. The point is that the free things, "Incline your ear, and come unto me: hear, and your soul shall live" (55:3), keep getting crowded out by the costly ones.

The same chapter says the word that goes out "shall not return unto me void" (55:11), like rain that waters the earth before it goes back. For a parent praying for a wayward child, or a teacher who never sees what becomes of students, 55:10–11 is permission to keep watering without seeing the harvest.

Who feels "utterly separated" in your ward?

Isaiah 56:3–7 is the most socially pointed passage of the week. "Neither let the son of the stranger, that hath joined himself to the LORD, speak, saying, The LORD hath utterly separated me from his people: neither let the eunuch say, Behold, I am a dry tree." The Lord promises the eunuch "a place and a name better than of sons and of daughters" (56:5) and calls the temple "an house of prayer for all people" (56:7).

Set that beside Deuteronomy 23:1–3, which bars the emasculated man and certain foreigners from "the congregation of the LORD." Isaiah 56 does not hide the tension; it seems deliberately to answer it, granting covenant belonging to exactly the people an older rule kept at the door, on the condition of keeping covenant ("keep my sabbaths, and choose the things that please me, and take hold of my covenant," 56:4). And it is hard to miss that Acts 8 tells of a man who was both a eunuch and a foreigner, riding home from worship in Jerusalem and reading Isaiah 53 in his chariot when Philip met him and baptized him (Acts 8:27–38). The text that welcomed him in Isaiah 56 sits three chapters from the text that converted him.

The eunuch's complaint, "I am a dry tree," is about family: no children, no name carried forward, in a culture where that was the shape of a blessed life. That maps onto real people in a family-centered church: the single adult in their forties, the infertile couple who dread Mother's Day, the member whose sexuality or circumstances make the expected path unavailable, the convert whose family will not come to the baptism. The "son of the stranger" maps onto the immigrant, the person of a different race or class, the new member who does not know the vocabulary. Patrick Kearon's April 2024 talk, whose title is its thesis, "God's Intent Is to Bring You Home," reads naturally beside this chapter.

The text asks for something specific: do not let them say it. Do not allow, by your silence or your ward culture, the conclusion that they are cut off. Practically:

  • Notice which lessons, activities, and casual small talk assume everyone is married with children. Who goes quiet then?
  • Learn the name of one person who sits alone, and use it.
  • When a calling, a prayer assignment, or a talk is given out, ask whether the same twenty people always get them.

"The righteous perisheth, and no man layeth it to heart"

The final instruction is the starkest. "The righteous perisheth, and no man layeth it to heart: and merciful men are taken away, none considering" (57:1). Around it, the chapter indicts a society absorbed in its cults, down to child sacrifice "in the valleys" (57:5); amid that, a good person dies unnoticed.

Most of us are not offering children on altars, but we are capable of the indifference. Who in your circle quietly did good and is now gone, or ill, or simply too old to serve? "Laying it to heart" might mean writing down what that person did before the memory fades, going to the funeral of someone you did not know well, or visiting the elderly member now in care whose name has dropped off every list. The text says the righteous "shall enter into peace" (57:2); the failure it names is ours, not theirs.

The high and lofty One, and the contrite neighbor

The block's best summary of all these applications comes in 57:15: "For thus saith the high and lofty One that inhabiteth eternity, whose name is Holy; I dwell in the high and holy place, with him also that is of a contrite and humble spirit, to revive the spirit of the humble, and to revive the heart of the contrite ones."

God has two addresses: eternity, and the person who is crushed. If that is where God lives, then a disciple looking for God this week should look in both places. Morning prayer, yes; but also the weary coworker, the dry tree on the back row, the old man nobody visits. The servant of these chapters was "despised and rejected of men" and "we hid as it were our faces from him" (53:3). The practical question that rises out of the whole block is a simple one: whose face am I hiding mine from?
