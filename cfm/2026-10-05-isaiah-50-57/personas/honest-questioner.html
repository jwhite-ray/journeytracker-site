Whose Wounds? The Hard Questions in Isaiah 50–57

Written by an AI persona that surfaces the hard questions honestly — not a real person.

This week's block contains the chapter Christians have quoted more than almost any other passage in the Hebrew Bible, and the chapter Jews have had quoted at them more than any other. Anyone who reads it seriously, and then goes online, will meet the arguments. Better to meet them here first, at full strength, with the serious responses alongside. Nothing below is meant to settle anything.

Who is the servant?

The Church's lesson page answers without hesitation: few chapters "describe Jesus Christ's redemptive mission more beautifully than Isaiah 53." The New Testament agrees. Philip expounds the passage to the Ethiopian eunuch (Acts 8:32–35), 1 Peter 2:22–25 weaves its phrases into the passion, Luke 22:37 puts "he was reckoned among the transgressors" in Jesus' own mouth, and Matthew 8:17 cites 53:4. In the Book of Mormon, Abinadi quotes the whole song (Mosiah 14) and then explains it at length (Mosiah 15).

Now the strongest version of the other side. Within Isaiah 40–55 itself, the servant is named again and again, and his name is Israel: "thou, Israel, art my servant, Jacob whom I have chosen" (41:8); "hear, O Jacob my servant; and Israel, whom I have chosen" (44:1); "Thou art my servant, O Israel, in whom I will be glorified" (49:3). A reader who meets "my servant" again at 52:13 has been trained by the book to hear Israel. On the Israel reading, the astonished "we" of 53:1–6 are the kings who "shut their mouths at him" (52:15), realizing that the exiled nation they despised was suffering for more than its own guilt. Rashi, the most influential medieval Jewish commentator, explains that the prophet speaks of all Israel as though it were one man (on 53:3), and reads "he hath borne our griefs" as the nations confessing that Israel bore the sickness that should have fallen on them (on 53:4). Abraham ibn Ezra raised a textual objection to the Christian reading that is still repeated: a man who has died cannot "see his seed" or "prolong his days" (53:10), as Marc Zvi Brettler and Amy-Jill Levine report from the medieval sources.

The Christian rejoinder is also strong. In 49:5–6 the servant has a mission to Israel, "to bring Jacob again to him" — so the servant cannot simply be the nation. The fourth song is unusually individual: a body, a face, a grave, a trial, a silence before accusers. And the song insists on the servant's innocence — "he had done no violence, neither was any deceit in his mouth" (53:9) — while Isaiah elsewhere is relentless about Israel's guilt (50:1: "for your iniquities have ye sold yourselves").

Two historical facts complicate the polemics on both sides. First, the collective reading is not a late invention to dodge Christian claims. Origen, writing in the third century, records a debate in which a Jewish interlocutor answered that the prophecy referred to the whole people as though to a single individual (Contra Celsum 1.55). Second, the individual and even messianic reading is not uniquely Christian. Brettler and Levine survey rabbinic texts that apply the song to the Messiah (Ruth Rabbah on 53:5), to Moses, or to any righteous sufferer, and a midrash in Pesiqta Rabbati in which the Messiah Ephraim willingly accepts suffering for others — though he does not die. Brettler and Levine, drawing on Joel Rembaum's study of the medieval commentators, suggest that Rashi's national reading likely reflects the Rhineland massacres of the First Crusade: the servant song became a way of explaining why faithful Jews were being slaughtered by people who called themselves followers of the servant. That history should give pause to any Christian who uses this chapter as a weapon.

A faithful Latter-day Saint does not have to choose one referent exclusively. Nephi's rule of "likening" (1 Nephi 19:23) and the scriptures' habit of layered fulfilment allow a reading in which the song first described a real suffering figure or community in the exile and is fulfilled most fully in Christ. Brettler and Levine, writing as Jewish scholars, argue that Christian readings need not exclude Jewish ones; the reverse courtesy is worth extending.

The "skipped" chapter

A claim that circulates widely: synagogues deliberately removed Isaiah 53 from the weekly prophetic readings because it points too clearly to Jesus. The facts are these. The seven "haftarot of consolation" read after the ninth of Av draw on Isaiah 40–63, and one of them (for the Torah portion Shoftim) runs 51:12–52:12; another (Ki Teitzei) is 54:1–10. The servant song, 52:13–53:12, falls in the gap. But the haftarah cycle is a selection; large parts of the prophets are never read publicly. Brettler and Levine state the honest version: the verses are absent from Jewish liturgy, and "perhaps the verses dropped out of Jewish liturgical readings under Christian pressure; perhaps they were never included." The gap is real; the motive is unproven either way.

Is this atonement — and is it penal?

Even readers who agree the servant is Jesus disagree about what the song says he does. Three readings are on the table.

The vicarious reading takes 53:5–6 at face value: "he was wounded for our transgressions… the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all." Bernd Janowski's essay in the Tübingen collection The Suffering Servant describes the song as a "drama of taking another's place," in which the speakers come to recognize, only after the servant's death, that his suffering was on their behalf. Even Brettler and Levine, who do not read the servant as Jesus, grant that "his suffering has vicarious effects" and compare the death of David and Bathsheba's child (2 Samuel 12:13–14) — a comparison that is itself morally unsettling.

The minimalist reading, argued most fully by R. N. Whybray in Thanksgiving for a Liberated Prophet, holds that vicarious punishment is almost unknown elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible and that the servant — perhaps the prophet himself — suffered alongside guilty Israel and was then released, not killed. The song is then a thanksgiving, not an atonement theory.

The penal-substitution reading — God punishes the innocent servant in place of the guilty so that justice is satisfied — is the one many Protestant readers assume. It is worth noticing what the Hebrew does and does not say. The "offering for sin" of 53:10 is ʼasham, the reparation or guilt offering of Leviticus 5, which is about making restitution, not about punishment as such. The "chastisement of our peace" (53:5) uses musar, a word more at home in the vocabulary of discipline and instruction than of judicial penalty. And the "griefs" and "sorrows" of 53:4 are literally sicknesses and pains, which is why Matthew 8:17 applies the verse to Jesus' healings rather than to the cross.

Latter-day Saint scripture contains both strands. Abinadi speaks of Christ "standing betwixt them and justice… and satisfied the demands of justice" (Mosiah 15:9), language that sounds penal. But Alma 7:11–12 describes Christ taking on "pains and afflictions and temptations of every kind… the sicknesses of his people… that he may know according to the flesh how to succor his people" — a reading much closer to the literal Hebrew of 53:4, and to the lesson page's line that Christ "does not save us from a distance." The restored scriptures do not lock Isaiah 53 into a single atonement theory.

"It pleased the LORD to bruise him"

The KJV says "it pleased the LORD to bruise him; he hath put him to grief" (53:10). The verb rendered "pleased" (ḥafets) can mean to will or to purpose as well as to delight, and most modern translations soften it toward "it was the LORD's will." But the problem does not go away with a gentler verb. Either God willed the crushing of an innocent man, or the text is wrong about God. Some critics of penal substitution have gone so far as to call the bare version of this a kind of divine child abuse. Defenders answer that in Christian theology the Son is not a third party but God himself bearing the cost, so the giver and the victim are one; Latter-day Saints, with a sharper distinction between Father and Son, cannot use that answer in quite the same form, and might instead lean on Abinadi's insistence that the Son submits his will (Mosiah 15:7) and on the voluntary language of the song itself: "he hath poured out his soul unto death" (53:12). Note also the song's own self-correction. The speakers confess "we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God" (53:4) — and the point of the next verse is that they were wrong. Part of the song is a confession that human beings misread suffering as divine punishment. That does not resolve 53:10, but it means the text is arguing with its own most brutal reading.

Textual knots

At 53:11 the traditional Hebrew text reads "he shall see of the travail of his soul." The Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran (1QIsaᵃ), a second Qumran copy, and the Greek Septuagint all read "he shall see light" — a reading many modern translations adopt, and one that sounds to Christian ears like resurrection. At 52:15, "so shall he sprinkle many nations" renders yazzeh, a verb that elsewhere means to sprinkle (as with blood or water in ritual); the Septuagint has "many nations shall marvel at him," and translations like the NLT render "startle." "Sprinkle" is priestly; "startle" is about onlookers. And at 53:9, "with the rich in his death" sits awkwardly parallel to "with the wicked"; some scholars propose emending the word for "rich" (ʻashir) to something meaning "evildoers," while Christian readers have long seen the rich man's tomb of Joseph of Arimathea (Matthew 27:57–60).

The Book of Mormon enters this debate in an interesting way. Shon Hopkin's comparison of Isaiah 52–53 with Mosiah 13–14 counts twenty differences from the KJV, of which he argues fourteen have some support in ancient witnesses — for example, plural "iniquities" and "transgressions." Yet Mosiah 14:11 reads "He shall see the travail of his soul," without the Qumran "light." Believers can point to variants that align with ancient texts; skeptics can point out that the most important ancient variant is missing.

The Book of Mormon's Isaiah problem

The larger issue is dating. A broad scholarly consensus assigns Isaiah 40–55 to an anonymous prophet of the Babylonian exile, writing after 586 BC, and chapters 56–66 — which begin inside this week's reading — to a still later setting in restored Judah. The Book of Mormon has Nephi quoting Isaiah 50–51 and 52:1–2 (2 Nephi 7–8) from brass plates his family carried out of Jerusalem around 600 BC, and Abinadi, centuries later, quoting Isaiah 53, presumably from the same records. If the consensus is right, those chapters did not yet exist.

Joshua Sears of BYU, writing for the Religious Studies Center, lays out four Latter-day Saint responses: dismiss the scholarly dating outright; defend the unity of Isaiah on biblical grounds (for instance, by challenging the assumption that predictive prophecy is impossible); accept much of the scholarship but argue the material was composed or edited in the century between Isaiah and Lehi; or posit divine intervention in transmission or translation. Sears holds that none of this need undermine faith. Critics reply that the second option rejects the overwhelming consensus, the third pushes the dates earlier than most scholars will allow, and the fourth explains anything and therefore nothing.

Related is the question of KJV dependence. The Book of Mormon's Isaiah chapters follow the King James wording closely, including KJV translation choices. Royal Skousen's textual work, and Stan Spencer's "missing words" study in Interpreter, document that the Book of Mormon disproportionately alters or omits the words the KJV prints in italics (words the translators supplied). Spencer reads this as a translation process interacting with an English text Joseph Smith saw; critics read it as evidence that Joseph worked from a printed Bible and adjusted the italicized words.

Eunuchs, foreigners, and a law overturned

Isaiah 56:3–7 is one of the most generous passages in the Hebrew Bible: the eunuch who keeps covenant will receive "a name better than of sons and of daughters," and the foreigner's sacrifices "shall be accepted upon mine altar; for mine house shall be called an house of prayer for all people." But Deuteronomy 23:1 says flatly that a man so injured "shall not enter into the congregation of the LORD," and 23:3 bars Ammonites and Moabites "for ever." Meanwhile Ezra 9–10 and Nehemiah 13 push hard in the opposite direction, expelling foreign wives. Scripture is arguing with itself, in public. A faithful reading can see progressive revelation; a critical reading sees competing parties in post-exilic Judah. Either way, it is striking that one of the first converts from outside Israel in Acts is a eunuch, baptized after reading Isaiah 53 (Acts 8:27–38) — the very scroll that, three chapters later, promises eunuchs a name better than sons and daughters.

Chapter 57 and the marriage metaphor

Chapter 57 is rarely taught on Sunday. It accuses "sons of the sorceress, the seed of the adulterer and the whore" (57:3) of "slaying the children in the valleys" (57:5) — likely child sacrifice of the kind condemned in 2 Kings 23:10 and Jeremiah 7:31. Verse 8, in the KJV, is opaque: "thou lovedst their bed where thou sawest it." The Hebrew literally says "you gazed on a hand," and the UBS translators' Handbook on Isaiah by Ogden and Sterk explains that yad here is a euphemism for the male organ. The KJV's discretion hides how graphic the language is.

That language belongs to a larger pattern that begins with this week's first verse — "Where is the bill of your mother's divorcement?" (50:1) — and continues in "thy Maker is thine husband" (54:5) and "a small moment have I forsaken thee" (54:7). God is the husband, the people a wife who has whored and been put away. Renita Weems's Battered Love argues that this prophetic marriage metaphor, however powerful, scripted divine love in the shape of a husband who punishes and then woos back, with real consequences for how readers imagine both God and marriage. A defender can answer that Isaiah 54 bends the metaphor toward tenderness: the forsaking is "a small moment," the kindness "everlasting," sworn like the covenant after Noah (54:8–10). Whether that redeems the metaphor or merely softens it is a question each reader has to sit with.

What remains open

Who the servant was, what his suffering accomplished, whether the text was written before Lehi left Jerusalem, and how to read a God who "bruises" and "forsakes" — none of these has a consensus answer, and some never will. What the text itself models is a community that looked at a suffering figure, got him wrong ("we esteemed him not"), and then changed its mind. That is not a bad posture for reading difficult scripture.
