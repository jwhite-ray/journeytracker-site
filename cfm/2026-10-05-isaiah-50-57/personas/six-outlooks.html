Feet on the Mountains: One Verse Read Six Ways

Written by an AI persona applying Donald W. Parry's six-outlooks framework. Professor Parry did not write, review, or endorse this essay, and no words are attributed to him that are not cited to a published source.

How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him that bringeth good tidings, that publisheth peace; that bringeth good tidings of good, that publisheth salvation; that saith unto Zion, Thy God reigneth! (Isaiah 52:7)

Isaiah 53 is this week's headline, but for a method built on reading one passage from several distances at once, the better test case sits a few verses earlier. Isaiah 52:7 has an unusually well-documented afterlife. It was already a stock phrase before Second Isaiah used it (Nahum 1:15). A Qumran commentator turned it into a prophecy about his own sect. Paul quoted it to explain why preachers are sent. Abinadi spent most of a sermon answering a hostile question about it. Jesus recites it to the Nephites. Joseph Smith applied it to baptism for the dead. Few verses have been read this many ways by this many readers, and that history makes it a good place to walk slowly through the six outlooks that Donald W. Parry, professor of Hebrew Bible at Brigham Young University and a member of the international Dead Sea Scrolls translation team (BYU Speeches speaker page), teaches as ways of reading Isaiah.

The framework in brief: read a passage (1) in its historical context, the near view; (2) as prophecy, the distant view; (3) for dual or multiple fulfillment; (4) by likening it to ourselves; (5) personally, for whatever instruction or revelation comes, even if it has little to do with the passage; and (6) for doctrine, the outlook Parry presses hardest. The headings below follow that order. This essay applies his framework. It does not claim to speak for him.

1. Historical context: a runner on the ridge road

Chapters 40–55 address Judeans in or just after the Babylonian exile. Jerusalem had fallen in 586 BCE, and the text names Cyrus of Persia as the one who will say of Jerusalem, "Thou shalt be built" (44:28; 45:1). Most critical scholarship treats these chapters as the work of an exilic prophet writing in Isaiah's tradition (see Joseph Blenkinsopp's Anchor Bible commentary Isaiah 40–55). Read inside that world, 52:7 is a picture anyone in the ancient Near East would have known. A city waits for news. Watchmen stand on the wall. A single figure appears on the hills, running.

Dana M. Pike points to the clearest biblical parallel, 2 Samuel 18:24–27, where a watchman over Jerusalem's gate sees "a man running alone," recognizes the gait of Ahimaaz, and David concludes, "He is a good man, and cometh with good tidings." The Hebrew word in Isaiah 52:7 is not the usual word for messenger (malʼak) but the participle mebasser, "one who brings news" (Pike, "How Beautiful upon the Mountains," 255–257). The word itself is neutral. In 1 Samuel 4:17 a mebasser reports a massacre. Here the verse insists three times that the news is good: peace (shalom), good, salvation.

Nahum had used nearly the same line several decades earlier to celebrate the fall of Nineveh: "Behold upon the mountains the feet of him that bringeth good tidings, that publisheth peace! O Judah, keep thy solemn feasts" (Nahum 1:15). So the image was already in the stock of Judean poetry. Second Isaiah redirects it from an enemy's fall to Jerusalem's own restoration.

Then there is the message itself, "Thy God reigneth," Hebrew malak ʼelohayik, more literally "your God has become king." That is the formula used when a king is proclaimed: "Absalom reigneth in Hebron" (2 Samuel 15:10), "Jehu is king" (2 Kings 9:13). The enthronement psalms use it of God: "The LORD reigneth" (Psalms 93:1; 96:10; 97:1). For exiles whose king had been blinded and carried to Babylon, the herald's news is that the throne in Zion is occupied again, and its occupant is the LORD. In the next verse the watchmen see it "eye to eye" (52:8). Where the KJV reads "when the LORD shall bring again Zion," many modern translations (the NRSV, for one) render the Hebrew as the LORD's own return to Zion, a king coming home to his city.

The near view has to be honest about how the story turned out. Verse 9 still addresses the "waste places of Jerusalem." When Judeans did return under Persian rule, Judah remained a province of someone else's empire. Pike himself notes that Jerusalem "was hardly redeemed" by the return (252–253). That gap between the herald's announcement and what the returnees found is where every later reading of this verse begins.

2. Prophecy: the distant view

The Book of Mormon reads the verse as looking well past the sixth century. Jesus recites it to the Nephites in 3 Nephi 20:40, placing it after the Lord's people "shall know my name" in "that day" (20:39), which in context is the latter-day gathering of Israel. His version has two small additions: "the feet of him that bringeth good tidings unto them… that bringeth good tidings unto them of good." The news is addressed to a gathered people who have not yet received it.

Doctrine and Covenants 113:7–10 points the same way for the verses just before. Joseph Smith explains that the call to "put on thy strength, O Zion" (52:1) refers to those "whom God should call in the last days." Pike draws the conclusion for the whole unit: whatever Isaiah's first audience heard, latter-day scripture puts the main fulfillment of 52:1–10 still in the future (Pike, 252). Verse 10 supports him: "all the ends of the earth shall see the salvation of our God." No sixth-century event fits that description.

So the distant view sees a herald whose news has not yet fully arrived. The prophecy reaches past Cyrus to a final gathering and a fully realized reign.

3. Dual and multiple fulfillment: Abinadi's four tenses

Parry has defended dual readings of Isaiah elsewhere. On the Immanuel sign of Isaiah 7, Parry and his coauthors in Understanding Isaiah (1998) describe a "lesser fulfillment" in Isaiah's own day and a "greater fulfillment" in Christ (quoted and discussed in Joshua M. Sears, "An Other Approach to Isaiah Studies"). For 52:7, though, the best multiple-fulfillment reading is already in scripture, and it comes from Abinadi.

The priests of Noah ask him what the verse means (Mosiah 12:20–24). Pike reads the question as a trap: the verse praises a messenger of good news, and Abinadi has brought only bad news, so the priests can call him a false prophet (Pike, 264). Abinadi delays his answer for two chapters. When he finally gives it, he builds it on Isaiah 53 ("he shall see his seed," 53:10). His followers and the holy prophets, he says, are Christ's "seed" (Mosiah 15:10–13), and then:

And these are they who have published peace, who have brought good tidings of good, who have published salvation; and said unto Zion: Thy God reigneth! And O how beautiful upon the mountains were their feet! And again, how beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of those that are still publishing peace! And again, how beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of those who shall hereafter publish peace, yea, from this time henceforth and forever! (Mosiah 15:14–17)

Past, present, and future: "were," "are still," "shall hereafter." Then a fourth step that changes the scale: "this is not all. For O how beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him that bringeth good tidings, that is the founder of peace, yea, even the Lord" (15:18). Abinadi does not choose between fulfillments. He sets them in order, and the last one includes all the others. Every prophet is a herald. The Lord is the herald behind all of them.

Abinadi also turns the verse against the men who asked about it. The priests are supposed to be watchmen, and they have not seen anything. Isaiah 56:10 has a harsher word for them, "His watchmen are blind… dumb dogs, they cannot bark."

4. Likening: from pesher to Nauvoo

Nephi asked his people to "liken all scriptures unto us" (1 Nephi 19:23), and 52:7 may be the most likened verse in the Bible. Comparing the readers who likened it shows how the move works.

The most aggressive example comes from Qumran. 11QMelchizedek (11Q13), a fragmentary Hebrew text from about 100 BCE, is a pesher, a commentary that reads scripture as coded prophecy about the interpreter's own community and its last days. It quotes 52:7 and decodes it item by item. "Zion" is the congregation of the sons of righteousness. The messenger is "the anointed of the spirit." "Your God" is Melchizedek, a heavenly figure who will deliver the righteous from Belial in a final jubilee (Wikipedia, "11Q13"). Pesher does not say the verse resembles the community's situation. It says the verse was written about the community.

Paul likens in a gentler way. In Romans 10:14–15 he asks how anyone can call on the Lord without hearing, or hear "without a preacher," and then quotes: "How beautiful are the feet of them that preach the gospel of peace." Isaiah's single herald becomes "them," the preachers sent out by the church. Nephi does the same thing in 1 Nephi 13:37: "whoso shall publish peace… how beautiful upon the mountains shall they be."

Joseph Smith applied the verse to the most unexpected subject. Writing about baptism for the dead, he heard in the gospel "glad tidings for the dead" and quoted: "How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of those that bring glad tidings of good things, and that say unto Zion: Behold, thy God reigneth!" (D&C 128:19). Pike adds that the Hebrew word for "beautiful" here, naʼwu, is the source Joseph Smith drew on when he named the city of Nauvoo (Pike, 258).

The difference between pesher and likening matters, and Nephi's method is the more honest of the two. Pesher claims the text was always secretly about us. Likening admits it was about someone else and asks what follows if we stand where they stood.

5. Personal reading: what the verse may say to one reader

Parry's fifth outlook lets a passage serve as the occasion for instruction that the passage itself may not contain. The discipline is to label this kind of reading honestly. It is not exegesis, and it should not be presented as what Isaiah meant.

The detail that tends to catch readers is the feet. Pike notes that feet are not usually anyone's idea of beauty. They are dusty and calloused, and in Israel they had to be washed at the door (Pike, 258). The watchmen on the wall cannot see the runner's face at that distance. They see the feet moving, and they call them beautiful because of what is coming. A reader might take from that something the verse never says directly: that we are judged less by how we look than by whether we arrive, and that the plain work of walking toward someone with good news is lovely to the people waiting for it.

Another reader might stop at the watchmen and ask whether they themselves are watching for a messenger who is already on the road. A third might think of a particular person who once walked a long way to bring them peace. These readings are real and can be revelatory, but they belong to the reader. The other five outlooks are there to keep them from being mistaken for the text's own meaning.

6. Doctrine: what the verse teaches

Parry stresses this outlook most, and his own published work shows why. His 2020 Interpreter article argues for "the unmistakable presence of Jesus Christ in Isaiah's text," chiefly by identifying the LORD (Jehovah) of the Hebrew Bible with Christ (Parry, "An Approach to Isaiah Studies"). Read with that identification, 52:7 yields several doctrinal claims.

The gospel is news about a king. In the Greek Septuagint, the herald of 52:7 is euangelizomenos, "one who brings good news." That is the verb from which "evangel" and "gospel" come. The content of the good news is not a technique or a feeling. It is "Thy God reigneth." When Mark summarizes Jesus' preaching as "the gospel of the kingdom of God… the kingdom of God is at hand" (Mark 1:14–15), the pairing of good news with kingship is Isaiah's.

The reign is announced before it can be seen. The herald arrives while Jerusalem is still "waste places" (52:9). The proclamation comes first and the rebuilding afterward. That is a doctrine of faith in the most concrete sense: the watchmen sing over ruins because they trust the messenger.

The messenger and the message meet in one person. In Abinadi's exposition, the Lord is both the herald ("the founder of peace") and the God whose reign is announced (Mosiah 15:18). If the LORD of Isaiah is Christ, as Parry argues, then "Thy God reigneth" is news Christ brings about himself. On the modern rendering of 52:8 noted above, the verse itself shows the same thing: the LORD returning to Zion in person.

Receiving the news makes a person a messenger. Abinadi links 52:7 to 53:10. Those whose sins the servant bore become his "seed" (Mosiah 15:10–12), and the seed are the ones whose feet are beautiful (15:14–17). In this reading, discipleship takes the form of announcement. The verse that follows the herald is the fourth servant song (52:13–53:12), and the order is deliberate: first the news that God reigns, then the account of what that reign cost.

Read from all six distances, the verse holds together. A runner brings news of a God-king's return to a ruined city. That news outlived its first fulfillment. Prophets, apostles, sectarians, and a frontier city each took it up, and in Abinadi's hands it became a statement about who the Messiah is and who belongs to him.
