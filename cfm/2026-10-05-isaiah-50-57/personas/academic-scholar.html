A Servant Without a Name: Isaiah 50–57 Through Critical Eyes

Written by an AI persona in the voice of a non-Latter-day-Saint Hebrew Bible academic — not a real scholar.

Strip away twenty-five centuries of reception, and Isaiah 50–57 is a set of anonymous poems written for a defeated people at a hinge of Near Eastern history. Mainstream scholarship cannot tell you who wrote them or who the suffering servant was. It can show what the text says, what it may have meant to its first audience, and where the Hebrew itself is uncertain, which matters more here than almost anywhere else in the Bible.

Where we are in the book

Since Bernhard Duhm's 1892 commentary Das Buch Jesaia, critical scholarship has generally divided Isaiah into three blocks: chapters 1–39, largely tied to the eighth-century prophet of Jerusalem; chapters 40–55, which scholars call Second Isaiah or Deutero-Isaiah; and chapters 56–66, called Third Isaiah or Trito-Isaiah. The reason for the second division is internal. Chapters 40–55 speak to people already in Babylon, treat Jerusalem's destruction as past, and name the Persian king Cyrus (44:28; 45:1) as YHWH's instrument. Cyrus took Babylon in 539 BCE, and his own propaganda text, the Cyrus Cylinder now in the British Museum, presents him as the restorer of displaced gods and peoples. Most commentators therefore place Second Isaiah in the 540s and 530s. Joseph Blenkinsopp's Anchor Bible volumes are a standard statement of this consensus.

This week's block crosses the line. Chapters 50–55 are the last movement of Second Isaiah. Chapter 56 opens with "Keep ye judgment, and do justice" (56:1). From that point on, the setting is a community back in Judah arguing over the temple, the sabbath, and who counts as a member. The line is contested: Shalom Paul's Eerdmans commentary argues for continuity of authorship across chapters 40–66 and declines to mark off a separate Third Isaiah. Still, most scholars hear a change of voice at 56.

The servant songs as a scholarly construct

Duhm also isolated four "servant songs": 42:1–4, 49:1–6, 50:4–9 (some extend it to verse 11), and 52:13–53:12. He argued that they came from a different hand than the surrounding chapters. His proposal shaped a century of research, but it is a proposal. In A Farewell to the Servant Songs (1983), Tryggve Mettinger argued that the songs cannot be cut out of their context. Elsewhere in these chapters the servant is explicitly Israel. "Thou art my servant, O Israel, in whom I will be glorified" (49:3) sits inside one of Duhm's own songs. Many scholars now read the songs as integral to Second Isaiah. Even so, they keep the label as a convenience.

The third song (50:4–9) is a first-person speech by someone trained as a disciple. The KJV's "the tongue of the learned" translates a Hebrew word that means "those who are taught" (limmudim), the same word used in 54:13, "all thy children shall be taught of the LORD." He is woken "morning by morning" to listen. He then suffers physical humiliation: "I gave my back to the smiters, and my cheeks to them that plucked off the hair: I hid not my face from shame and spitting" (50:6). The vocabulary that follows is legal. "He is near that justifieth me; who will contend with me? let us stand together: who is mine adversary?" (50:8). The speaker imagines a lawsuit in which God acts as his advocate. This mix of personal lament and confidence in vindication resembles the individual laments of the Psalms and the "confessions" of Jeremiah. That is one reason a prophetic individual remains a strong candidate for the speaker.

Myth, cup, and herald: chapters 51–52

Chapter 51 shows how freely this poet uses older material. "Art thou not it that hath cut Rahab, and wounded the dragon? Art thou not it which hath dried the sea, the waters of the great deep?" (51:9–10). This is combat myth, the storm god's victory over the chaotic Sea. The Ugaritic texts from Ras Shamra show it in Canaanite form, where Baal defeats Yamm (Sea) and a coiling sea-serpent. John Day's God's Conflict with the Dragon and the Sea argues that the Hebrew Bible's Rahab and Leviathan imagery descends from this Canaanite mythology rather than directly from the Babylonian Enuma Elish. The poet then does something characteristic: the primeval victory over the sea merges into the Exodus crossing ("a way for the ransomed to pass over," 51:10) and then into the expected return from Babylon (51:11). Creation, Exodus, and return become one act.

The "cup of his fury" (51:17–23) is a stock prophetic image of judgment as forced drunkenness (compare Jeremiah 25:15–29). Here the cup is taken from Jerusalem and handed to her tormentors.

Isaiah 52:7 contains a phrase that matters for historians of religion. The herald's message to Zion is "Thy God reigneth" (malak ʼelohayikh). This is the language of the enthronement psalms ("The LORD reigneth," Psalm 93:1; 97:1). In a world where Marduk of Babylon had seemed victorious, the poet proclaims YHWH's kingship at the moment of Babylon's fall.

Isaiah 52:13–53:12: structure

David Clines's short monograph I, He, We, and They (1976) made the most useful observation about its structure: the poem is built from shifting pronouns. God speaks in 52:13–15 and again in 53:11b–12, framing the poem with "my servant." In between, an unidentified "we" confesses (53:1–6): "we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted. But he was wounded for our transgressions." "They" are the nations and kings, who are startled into silence. "He" is the servant, who never speaks. The poem's drama is a change of perception. The "we" had read the servant's suffering as proof of divine disfavor (the logic of Job's friends) and now recognize that it was for them.

Isaiah 52:13–53:12: the Hebrew problems

Here the KJV rests on uncertain ground.

  • 52:13, "deal prudently." The verb yaskil can mean "act wisely" or "succeed." The KJV's "prudently" is defensible, but "prosper" is the more common modern choice.
  • 52:15, "sprinkle many nations." The Hebrew yazzeh normally means "sprinkle," as with sacrificial blood, but "sprinkle nations" is a strange phrase. The Greek translation reads "many nations shall marvel at him," and many modern versions follow with "startle." The Greek fits the parallel line ("the kings shall shut their mouths") better.
  • 53:8. "He was taken from prison and from judgment: and who shall declare his generation?" Nearly every word here can be read more than one way. The phrase could mean "by oppressive judgment he was taken away," and "his generation" could mean his contemporaries, his fate, or his descendants. Whether it describes an execution, an illness, or something else depends on how they are resolved.
  • 53:9, "with the rich in his death." The parallel line has "the wicked," and "the rich" sits awkwardly beside it. Some scholars propose emending the Hebrew ʻashir ("rich") to a word meaning "evildoers." Others keep it and take "the rich" as a byword for the arrogant oppressor, as in Wisdom literature. The Hebrew for "in his death" is plural ("in his deaths"), which is itself unusual. The verse's apparent fit with a burial in a wealthy man's tomb (Matthew 27:57–60) owes much to the KJV's choices.
  • 53:10, "an offering for sin." The Hebrew is ʼasham, the reparation or guilt offering of Leviticus 5. This is the strongest single piece of cultic, substitutionary language in the poem, and much of the debate about "vicarious atonement" depends on how literally to take it.
  • 53:11, "He shall see of the travail of his soul." The Masoretic Hebrew has the verb "see" with no object. The Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran (1QIsaᵃ, about 125 BCE), other Qumran Isaiah manuscripts, and the Greek Septuagint all read "he shall see light" (yirʼeh ʼor). Most critical translations now adopt this reading. It gives the servant an explicit vindication after death or near-death, which the Masoretic text leaves implicit.

Who is the servant?

There are four main families of answer, and none has settled the question.

Israel, collectively. This is the reading with the strongest support in the surrounding text, since Second Isaiah repeatedly calls Israel/Jacob "my servant" (41:8; 44:1; 49:3). It became the dominant medieval Jewish reading. Rashi, commenting on this passage, identifies the servant with the people of Israel, who suffered among the nations. On this view the "we" of 53:1–6 are the astonished nations.

A prophetic individual. This could be Second Isaiah himself, a disciple, or Jeremiah. R. N. Whybray's Thanksgiving for a Liberated Prophet (1978) argued that the poem is a thanksgiving by the prophet's followers after he was imprisoned and released. It is not about a death at all, and it is not about vicarious suffering in any technical sense.

An individual whose suffering benefits the community. Marc Brettler and Amy-Jill Levine, in "Isaiah's Suffering Servant: Before and After Christianity" (2019), describe the servant as originally an individual in Babylon whose vicarious suffering explains why Israel can be forgiven. They then trace how the figure was reread in Daniel, in the New Testament, and in later Jewish sources.

A royal figure. John Walton has argued that the poem draws on the imagery of the Mesopotamian substitute-king ritual. In that rite, when omens threatened the king, a stand-in was enthroned, bore the threatened evil, and was put to death. Walton does not claim that Isaiah describes an actual ritual. His claim is that its motifs supply the background for the poem's language of substitution, exaltation, and royal reward ("I will divide him a portion with the great," 53:12).

The interpretive history begins inside the Hebrew Bible itself. H. L. Ginsberg showed in a 1953 article that Daniel 11–12 reads Isaiah 53 as a template for the persecuted "wise" (maskilim, from the same root as yaskil in 52:13) who "turn many to righteousness" (Daniel 12:3; compare Isaiah 53:11). In other words, by the second century BCE the servant was already being applied to a group of martyrs.

Is it about vicarious atonement?

The plain sense of 53:4–6 is that the servant suffered something that should have fallen on the "we," and that his suffering benefits them: "with his stripes we are healed." The ʼasham of 53:10 supports a substitutionary reading. Against that, nothing in the poem describes a mechanism like later Christian atonement theory, and the Hebrew Bible elsewhere resists the idea that one person can bear another's guilt (Deuteronomy 24:16; Ezekiel 18:20). Many scholars split the difference. On their reading, the poem's main claim is about recognition: the community learns that the sufferer it despised was innocent and that his suffering was meaningful for them. The cultic vocabulary gives that meaning a theological frame. The essays in Bernd Janowski and Peter Stuhlmacher's The Suffering Servant: Isaiah 53 in Jewish and Christian Sources (2004) trace this debate through the Targum, the New Testament, and the church fathers.

The reception history is also contested ground. The New Testament reads the passage as fulfilled in Jesus (Acts 8:32–35; 1 Peter 2:22–25). The Babylonian Talmud (Sanhedrin 98b) applies 53:4 to the Messiah, playfully, in a list of rabbinic school answers. Medieval Jewish commentators, writing under Christian polemical pressure, emphasized the national reading. In the synagogue's annual cycle, the prophetic readings of consolation after the Ninth of Av move from Isaiah 51:12–52:12 (read with Deuteronomy's Shoftim portion) to 54:1–10 (read with Ki Teitzei). The fourth song falls between them. Writers disagree about whether that gap is a deliberate response to Christian use or simply a consequence of choosing passages of comfort, since the lectionary also skips many other chapters of Second Isaiah. Neither explanation is settled. (Mosiah 14 in the Book of Mormon, which reproduces the chapter, belongs from a critical standpoint to this reception history.)

Chapters 54–55: wife, covenant, word

After the servant poem, the poet returns to Zion, now pictured as a woman: barren, widowed, and abandoned (54:1–6). The divorce question of 50:1 receives its answer here. There was no bill of divorce, only "a small moment" of anger (54:7). The comparison to Noah's flood (54:9) makes the restoration a cosmic oath, as unbreakable as the promise of Genesis 9:11.

Isaiah 55:3 is one of the most important verses in the block for historians. "I will make an everlasting covenant with you, even the sure mercies of David." The "you" is plural. The promise of a permanent dynasty given to David (2 Samuel 7) is transferred to the people as a whole. With no Davidic king on the throne since 586 BCE, and with Cyrus as Second Isaiah's royal figure, most commentators read 55:3–5 as a deliberate democratization of royal ideology.

Chapters 56–57: a new community and its quarrels

The opening of Third Isaiah contains a remarkable legal reversal. Deuteronomy 23:1 bars a man with crushed or cut genitals from "the congregation of the LORD," and 23:3 bars Ammonites and Moabites. Isaiah 56:3–7 promises eunuchs "a place and a name better than of sons and of daughters" and declares that foreigners' "burnt offerings and their sacrifices shall be accepted upon mine altar; for mine house shall be called an house of prayer for all people." Conditioned on sabbath-keeping and covenant loyalty, it takes a side in a live Persian-period debate, whether or not it consciously overturns Deuteronomy. On the other side stand Ezra 9–10 and Nehemiah 13:1–3, which apply Deuteronomy 23 to expel foreigners. Blenkinsopp's Isaiah 56–66 situates these chapters within that argument over who belongs to restored Israel.

Chapter 57 is the most graphic text in the block, and the KJV's phrasing softens it. The accusations include fertility rites "under every green tree," child sacrifice "in the valleys under the clifts of the rocks" (57:5), and an extended image of Israel as a promiscuous woman who "enlarged thy bed" (57:8). In 57:8, "thou lovedst their bed where thou sawest it" renders a Hebrew phrase that many commentators take as a sexual euphemism. In 57:9, some read "the king" (melek) as the deity Molek. Most strikingly, in 57:6, "Among the smooth stones of the stream is thy portion" was reinterpreted after W. H. Irwin proposed a Ugaritic-based meaning, "the dead of the wadi." Theodore Lewis develops the case in "Death Cult Imagery in Isaiah 57" and Cults of the Dead in Ancient Israel and Ugarit: the drink and grain offerings in 57:6 would be offerings to the dead. Susan Ackerman's Under Every Green Tree treats Isaiah 57 as evidence for sixth-century Judean popular religion. In her reading, the polemic preserves real practices, including cults of the dead and fertility worship, that many Judeans may not have regarded as apostasy.

Against this, the chapter closes with one of the Bible's most striking pairings of transcendence and nearness: "the high and lofty One that inhabiteth eternity… I dwell in the high and holy place, with him also that is of a contrite and humble spirit" (57:15). The final verse, "There is no peace, saith my God, to the wicked" (57:21), repeats 48:22 almost word for word. It is one sign that whoever shaped chapters 40–66 knit the material together deliberately.
