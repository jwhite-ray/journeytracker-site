"Who Shall Be His Seed?": Isaiah 50–57 in the Hands of Restoration Readers

Written by an AI persona connecting the block to Restoration scripture and modern teaching — not a real person.

Apart from Isaiah 2–14, which Nephi copies wholesale into 2 Nephi 12–24, no stretch of Isaiah is worked over more heavily in Latter-day Saint scripture than this week's eight chapters. Jacob reads Isaiah 50:1 through 52:2 aloud to the Nephites (2 Nephi 7–8). Abinadi's accusers throw 52:7–10 at him (Mosiah 12:20–24), and he answers by reciting all of chapter 53 (Mosiah 14). The risen Jesus quotes 52:8–10 at Bountiful (3 Nephi 16:18–20), returns the next day to recite most of chapter 52 (3 Nephi 20:32–45), and then recites chapter 54 in full (3 Nephi 22). A revelation through Joseph Smith interprets 52:1–2 (D&C 113:7–10).

So a doctrinal reading of this block need not be bolted on. It already exists, done by named readers in specific settings, and they do not all do the same thing with the text.

Jacob: the divorce question becomes the plan of salvation

Jacob reads Isaiah because the words "may be likened unto you, for ye are of the house of Israel" (2 Nephi 6:5). His audience is a small band of exiles, and Isaiah 50:1 asks the exiles' question: "Where is the bill of your mother's divorcement, whom I have put away?"

Jacob's text sharpens it. 2 Nephi 7:1 opens with a line the KJV does not have: "Have I put thee away, or have I cast thee off forever?" A question about paperwork becomes a question about permanence, which a people stranded across an ocean would ask. The answer is the same in both texts: no bill of divorce exists, the separation is self-inflicted ("for your iniquities have ye sold yourselves," 50:1), and the hand that could redeem is not "shortened" (50:2). Jacob's text also adds "O house of Israel" to 50:2 and 50:4, addressing the poem to his listeners by name.

Then, when the quotation ends at 52:2 (2 Nephi 8:24–25), Jacob goes not to the restoration of Jerusalem but straight to death. Within a few verses he is talking about an "infinite atonement" without which "this flesh must have laid down to rot" (9:7), and about "that monster, death and hell, which I call the death of the body, and also the death of the spirit" (9:10). In effect, Isaiah's divorced mother and captive daughter become the human condition after the Fall, and the arm that "dried the sea" (51:10) becomes the Redeemer who "suffereth the pains of all men" (2 Nephi 9:21).

He closes with Isaiah 55:1–2 (2 Nephi 9:50–51) but replaces Isaiah's "eat ye that which is good" with "feast upon that which perisheth not, neither can be corrupted" (9:51), turning the free banquet of chapter 55 into a promise of incorruption. Jacob reads Isaiah's consolations as one argument that ends at the plan of salvation. That is a theological reading, not a historical one, and Jacob says openly that he is likening.

Abinadi: the priests' verse and the prophet's answer

Abinadi's encounter is a debate over a proof text. Noah's priests ask what Isaiah 52:7–10 means: "How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him that bringeth good tidings" (Mosiah 12:21). The priests seem to wield it as a credential: they are the watchmen, and a doom-saying prophet has no business in their Zion. Abinadi scolds them (Mosiah 12:25), walks them through the Ten Commandments, and only then answers, by reciting the whole of Isaiah 53 (Mosiah 14).

The point: you cannot have the beautiful feet of 52:7 without the marred visage of 52:14 and the man of sorrows of 53:3.

Abinadi's chapter 53 is nearly identical to the KJV (Mosiah 14:9 reads "done no evil" for "no violence"). His commentary goes to a puzzling line, 53:10: "he shall see his seed." If the servant is "cut off out of the land of the living" (53:8), how does he have offspring? Abinadi asks it directly: "And now what say ye? And who shall be his seed?" (Mosiah 15:10).

The seed, he answers, are "all those who have hearkened unto [the prophets'] words, and believed that the Lord would redeem his people," and "these are they whose sins he has borne" (Mosiah 15:11–12). Then he widens it: the prophets themselves, "all the holy prophets ever since the world began," are his seed (15:13). Then he turns the priests' own verse into three tenses: beautiful "were their feet," "are the feet of those that are still publishing peace," and of "those who shall hereafter publish peace" (15:15–17). Only then does he give its final referent: "how beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him that bringeth good tidings, that is the founder of peace, yea, even the Lord" (15:18).

This bears on the live question of who the servant is. Mainstream scholarship tends to read the servant collectively, as Israel or a faithful remnant; Christian tradition reads one individual, Jesus. Abinadi does not choose. The servant is the Lord, who makes his soul an offering, but the servant's role passes to his seed, a community of prophets and believers. Individual and collective are bound together by spiritual adoption, the same idea King Benjamin expresses when he tells his covenanting people they "shall be called the children of Christ, his sons, and his daughters" (Mosiah 5:7). This does not settle the scholarly debate, but it shows Restoration scripture's own reading is more layered than a proof text.

"Griefs" and "sorrows": what Alma heard in 53:4

The New Testament hears "griefs" and "sorrows" in 53:4 more physically than the KJV suggests. Matthew renders the verse "Himself took our infirmities, and bare our sicknesses" and applies it to Jesus healing the sick (Matthew 8:17). So does Alma. The Son "shall go forth, suffering pains and afflictions and temptations of every kind; and this that the word might be fulfilled which saith he will take upon him the pains and the sicknesses of his people" (Alma 7:11). "The word... which saith" marks a citation, and the closest source is Isaiah 53:4.

Alma adds a reason Isaiah does not give: "that he may know according to the flesh how to succor his people" (Alma 7:12). Elder David A. Bednar built "Bear Up Their Burdens with Ease" (April 2014) on these verses, teaching that "there is no physical pain, no spiritual wound, no anguish of soul or heartache, no infirmity or weakness you or I ever confront in mortality that the Savior did not experience first." That is Isaiah 53:4 read through Alma 7, and it fits the lesson's choice of "He hath borne our griefs" as its title.

The risen Lord reads Isaiah 52, and the servant multiplies

The most surprising reading is the Savior's own. In 3 Nephi 20 Jesus walks through Isaiah 52 out of order and with his own changes. The watchmen who "see eye to eye" (52:8) are the scattered Jews after they "believe in me, that I am Jesus Christ" (3 Nephi 20:31–32), and "the salvation of our God" (52:10) becomes "the salvation of the Father; and the Father and I are one" (20:35).

Then he quotes the opening of the fourth servant song, 52:13–15: "my servant shall deal prudently... his visage was so marred, more than any man" (3 Nephi 20:43–45). He does not go on to Isaiah 53. Instead, in the next chapter, he applies the song's language to a latter-day figure. "Kings shall shut their mouths" (21:8) at a "great and a marvelous work" (21:9), and "the life of my servant shall be in my hand; therefore they shall not hurt him, although he shall be marred because of them. Yet I will heal him" (21:10).

Latter-day Saint readers have identified this servant as Jesus himself, as Joseph Smith, or, in an argument by Gaye Strathearn and Jacob Moody, as the Book of Mormon personified (summarized in Scripture Central's KnoWhy "Who Is the Servant Spoken of by Christ?"). The important point is less which candidate is right than what Jesus does: he takes the "marred" servant of 52:14, read of him by Christians for two thousand years, and applies its vocabulary to someone else. That should make Latter-day Saints slow to call the collective-Israel reading of Isaiah 53 simply wrong, while still testifying, with Abinadi and Philip (Acts 8:35), that the song finds its fullest meaning in Jesus.

He introduces Isaiah 54 as fulfillment: "And then shall that which is written come to pass: Sing, O barren" (3 Nephi 22:1). He follows it with a command, rare in scripture, to study one named prophet: "search these things diligently; for great are the words of Isaiah" (3 Nephi 23:1).

Strength, garments, stakes: the Doctrine and Covenants' Zion

When Elias Higbee asked what "Put on thy strength, O Zion" (52:1) means, the revelation answered in terms of priesthood: "to put on her strength is to put on the authority of the priesthood, which she, Zion, has a right to by lineage; also to return to that power which she had lost" (D&C 113:8). The "bands of her neck" (52:2) are "the curses of God upon her," and loosing them means returning to the Lord, who promises to "give them revelation" (113:10). A city dressing itself becomes restored authority and restored revelation.

"Zion must increase in beauty, and in holiness; her borders must be enlarged; her stakes must be strengthened; yea... Zion must arise and put on her beautiful garments" (D&C 82:14) combines 52:1 and 54:2. D&C 101:21 names the Church's basic unit from 54:2: places of gathering "shall be called stakes, for the curtains or the strength of Zion." Every stake president holds an office named for a tent peg in this week's reading. And 52:11, "be ye clean, that bear the vessels of the LORD," which in context concerns temple vessels carried home from Babylon (compare Ezra 1:7–11), is applied twice to latter-day Saints leaving their own Babylon (D&C 38:42; 133:5).

President Russell M. Nelson reads 54:7 as a gathering text in "Let God Prevail" (October 2020), quoting "For a small moment have I forsaken thee [Israel]; but with great mercies will I gather thee" and adding that "the Lord is gathering those who are willing to let God prevail in their lives." The forsaken wife of chapter 54 becomes scattered Israel now being gathered.

A name in the house

Isaiah 56:3–7 most invites a temple reading, and it raises a real ethical question. Deuteronomy 23:1 bars a man "wounded in the stones" from "the congregation of the Lord," and 23:3 bars Ammonites and Moabites "for ever." Isaiah 56 speaks to exactly these people, the eunuch who says "I am a dry tree" and the foreigner who fears "The LORD hath utterly separated me from his people" (56:3). It promises them more than admission: "in mine house and within my walls a place and a name better than of sons and of daughters... an everlasting name, that shall not be cut off" (56:5). The Hebrew for "a place and a name" is yad vashem, the phrase Israel's Holocaust memorial took as its name: remembrance for people with no descendants to carry it.

Whether Isaiah 56 overturns Deuteronomy or narrows it, it plainly makes covenant faithfulness ("keep my sabbaths... take hold of my covenant," 56:4) outweigh lineage and physical condition. The New Testament acts this out. Jesus quotes 56:7 ("my house shall be called... the house of prayer") while clearing the temple (Mark 11:17). Philip finds an Ethiopian eunuch, a foreigner and a eunuch at once, reading Isaiah 53 in his chariot and baptizes him (Acts 8:27–38). The kind of man Deuteronomy excluded is the one to whom Acts shows the servant song explained.

The Kirtland Temple dedicatory prayer asks that the Saints may "honorably hold a name and standing in this thy house, to all generations and for eternity" (D&C 109:24). A name received in the Lord's house that needs no children to preserve it is a temple idea; so is extending covenant blessings, through work for the dead, to those who had no one to carry them. I offer that as a connection, not a claim about Isaiah's intent. But the eunuch's "I am a dry tree" is answered by a premise of the plan of salvation: exaltation does not depend on mortal circumstance.

Rest for the righteous, and no peace for the wicked

Chapter 57 opens with a complaint, "The righteous perisheth, and no man layeth it to heart," and a reassurance: the righteous man "is taken away from the evil to come. He shall enter into peace" (57:1–2). Alma's teaching on the spirit world uses the same vocabulary: the righteous "are received into a state of happiness, which is called paradise, a state of rest, a state of peace" (Alma 40:12). Compare D&C 42:46: "those that die in me shall not taste of death." Isaiah's verse is too short to build an afterlife theology on, but the plan of salvation explains how it could be true.

Verses 3–13 describe fertility rites "under every green tree," children slaughtered "in the valleys," and a "bed" set up on the high mountain in covenant with other gods. The sexual imagery is the prophet's own, idolatry as adultery, and the Restoration does not soften the ending: "There is no peace, saith my God, to the wicked" (57:21) agrees with Alma's "wickedness never was happiness" (Alma 41:10).

Between that indictment and that verdict stands 57:15, which the lesson pairs with chapter 54: "I dwell in the high and holy place, with him also that is of a contrite and humble spirit." Elder Patrick Kearon's "God's Intent Is to Bring You Home" (April 2024) says the Father's plan "is designed to bring you home, not to keep you out," and "God is in relentless pursuit of you." Isaiah's God says "I have seen his ways, and will heal him" (57:18). The Book of Mormon puts the invitation in the same words as Isaiah 55: "doth he cry unto any, saying: Depart from me? Behold, I say unto you, Nay... buy milk and honey, without money and without price" (2 Nephi 26:25).

Elder Jeffrey R. Holland's "Behold the Lamb of God" (April 2019) carried the sacrificial lamb to the sacrament table, asking Saints to remember "that Christ died from a heart broken by shouldering entirely alone the sins and sorrows of the whole human family." Abinadi asked who would be the servant's seed. In Restoration scripture's answer, the seed are the people who come to that table, which means the contrite of 57:15, the eunuch and the foreigner of 56:3, and the prophets of every period.
