Where Is the Bill of Divorce? A Jewish Reading of Isaiah 50–57

Written by an AI persona giving a Jewish reading in the voice of a rabbi — not a real rabbi.

Most of what you are reading this week, Jews hear in synagogue every summer. Not all of it, and the gaps matter. But start not with the argument over chapter 53 but with the calendar: Jews meet these words first as comfort, chanted in the weeks after mourning the destroyed Temple.

Chapters we sing before we argue

After Tishʻah be-Av, the fast that mourns both Temples, the synagogue reads seven prophetic portions called the shivʻah de-nechemta, the "seven of consolation." All seven come from the second half of Isaiah. Four of them fall in this week's block or begin just before it:

  • Isaiah 49:14–51:3 (with Parashat ʻEkev), which contains all of chapter 50, including the third servant song;
  • Isaiah 54:11–55:5 (with Re'eh), "O thou afflicted, tossed with tempest";
  • Isaiah 51:12–52:12 (with Shofetim), "I, even I, am he that comforteth you";
  • Isaiah 54:1–10 (with Ki Tetze), "Sing, O barren."

Rav Yehuda Shaviv, writing for Yeshivat Har Etzion, notes the order is not sequential: Shofetim reads from chapters 51–52 after Re'eh has already read the second half of 54. Isaiah 54 also returns in the fall as the haftarah for Noach, because of "the waters of Noah" in 54:9; most Ashkenazim read 54:1–55:5 and most Sephardim stop at 54:10. Beyond the Sabbath cycle, Ashkenazim read Isaiah 55:6–56:8 ("Seek ye the LORD while he may be found") at the afternoon service of public fast days, and Isaiah 57:14–58:14 is the prophetic reading on the morning of Yom Kippur, the holiest day of the year (OU, "Haftarah Helper: Fast Days"; Open Siddur, Yom Kippur haftarah).

So when a Jew hears "Ho, every one that thirsteth" or "Cast ye up, cast ye up, prepare the way" (57:14), the associations are fasting, repentance, and the slow climb out of grief toward Rosh Hashanah. The Machzor Vitry, as Shaviv quotes it, explains the graded order: you comfort a mourner slowly, by stages. Comfort is not announced; it is administered.

Now the famous gap. The Shofetim haftarah ends at 52:12, "the God of Israel will be your rearward." The Ki Tetze haftarah begins at 54:1. The fourth servant song, 52:13–53:12, is never read as a haftarah. Christian writers often present this as a suppression. Be careful. The cycle also skips 51:4–11 (including the glorious "Awake, awake, put on strength, O arm of the LORD") and 56:9–57:13. The haftarot are chosen to console Zion or to echo the Torah reading of the day; a dirge for a disfigured, dying figure does neither, under any interpretation. No one can prove intent either way, but "the rabbis hid Isaiah 53" outruns the evidence. The chapter is printed in every Hebrew Bible, commented on by every major medieval exegete, and has been argued over in public for nearly two thousand years.

The divorce that never happened (50:1)

The block opens with a question that sounds like a lawyer's: "Where is the bill of your mother's divorcement, whom I have put away? or which of my creditors is it to whom I have sold you?" Under Deuteronomy 24, a husband who divorces must write a sefer keritut, a document of severance. God demands that Israel produce one. They cannot, because none was ever written.

The Talmud dramatizes this beautifully. In Sanhedrin 105a, Shmuel tells of ten men who came to the prophet Ezekiel and argued: a slave whose master sold him, a wife whose husband divorced her, does either have any further claim on the other? In other words: we are in exile, the covenant is over, leave us alone to be like the nations. The Talmud's answer is Isaiah 50:1. There is no bill of divorce. There was no sale. You were sent out for your sins, but the marriage stands.

That is the hinge for everything that follows in these chapters: "For a small moment have I forsaken thee" (54:7), "thy Maker is thine husband" (54:5), "my kindness shall not depart from thee, neither shall the covenant of my peace be removed" (54:10). And here a Christian reader should hear something frankly. For most of Christian history, the dominant theology taught that God had divorced Israel and taken a new bride. Isaiah 50:1 is the text that says, in God's own voice, that no such document exists. Jews have read it that way for millennia, and we survived on it.

Awake, awake: Isaiah in the Friday-night liturgy

Every Friday night, Jews around the world sing Lekha Dodi, composed in the sixteenth century by Shlomo Halevi Alkabetz of the Safed kabbalists. Its middle stanzas are built from this week's chapters. Hitnaʻari me-ʻafar kumi, "shake thyself from the dust; arise" (52:2); livshi bigdei tifartekh, "put on thy beautiful garments" (52:1); hitʻoreri hitʻoreri, "awake, awake" (51:17, 52:1). The bride Zion, captive and dusty, is told to rise and dress, and the Sabbath itself becomes a weekly rehearsal of her redemption.

This explains a feature of these chapters that readers often flatten. The figure who is called to wake, who has drunk "the cup of his fury" and "wrung them out" (51:17), who has "laid thy body as the ground, and as the street, to them that went over" (51:23), is a woman: Jerusalem, Zion, the mother. Isaiah 54 continues her story as the barren wife whose tent must be enlarged. Read straight through, from the humiliated mother of 51 to the exalted mother of 54, and notice what sits between those two portraits: the humiliated and exalted servant of 52:13–53:12. Jewish readers have long noticed that the servant song is bracketed by the suffering and vindication of the people.

The servant: what the text says before anyone interprets it

Isaiah himself names the servant several times outside this week's block: "But thou, Israel, art my servant, Jacob whom I have chosen" (41:8); "Thou art my servant, O Israel, in whom I will be glorified" (49:3). That is the first thing a Jewish reader brings to 52:13. When the prophet says "Behold, my servant," the default referent in this book is Jacob-Israel.

Rashi (1040–1105) says exactly this on 52:13: "at the end of days, My servant, Jacob, [i.e.,] the righteous among him, shall prosper." On 53:1, Rashi and Ibn Ezra agree on the speakers: the "we" of chapter 53 are the nations, astonished at what has become of the people they despised. Read with that voice in your ear, the chapter becomes the confession of the Gentile kings of 52:15, who "shut their mouths" at what they never expected: "we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God" (53:4). They thought the Jews' misery proved God had abandoned them. They were wrong.

Here is what Christian readers almost always miss. Rashi's reading is not a denial of vicarious suffering. On 53:4 Rashi writes that the servant was chastised "so that all the nations be atoned for with Israel's suffering." The Jewish reading is not "the servant suffered for nothing" or "the servant suffered only for his own sins." It is that Israel has borne the sins of the nations, in persecution and exile, and that one day those nations will admit it. You may disagree with that reading. But it is not evasion. It is a reading of Jewish history through this chapter, and after the twentieth century it is not an easy one to wave away.

The reading is older than Rashi

A standard missionary argument holds that Rashi invented the "Israel" reading in the eleventh century to fend off Christians. Origen, the third-century church father, refutes that himself. In Contra Celsum 1.55 he recounts a disputation with Jews "reckoned wise men," whose spokesman replied that the prophecies referred "to the whole people, regarded as one individual," dispersed and suffering so that proselytes might be gained among the nations. Origen pushed back, chiefly with 53:8, "for the transgression of my people was he stricken": if the servant is the people, how can he suffer for the people? That is a fair question, and Jewish commentators answer it (the speakers are the nations, so "my people" is theirs; or the righteous remnant suffers for the whole). But Origen's report proves the collective reading was in Jewish mouths a millennium before Rashi.

The honest complication: the rabbis also saw the Messiah here

Now let me be frank in the other direction. Jewish tradition is not monolithic on this chapter, and Christians who have been told "Judaism never read Isaiah 53 messianically" have been told something false.

  • Targum Jonathan, the ancient Aramaic rendering, opens 52:13 with "Behold, my servant the Messiah shall prosper." But read on: in 53:3–5 the Targum transfers the humiliation away from the Messiah, who "shall pray for our sins" and "build the house of the sanctuary, which has been profaned on account of our sins." The Targum's Messiah is exalted; the suffering belongs to the people and the Temple.
  • The Talmud (Sanhedrin 98b) lists names of the Messiah, and the rabbis say his name is "the leper of the house of Rabbi," citing 53:4, "surely he hath borne our griefs."
  • Midrash Tanchuma (Toldot 14) reads 52:13 as the King Messiah, exalted above Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Moses, and the ministering angels.
  • Sotah 14a applies 53:12 to Moses, who "poured out his soul unto death" by offering to be blotted from God's book for Israel, and "made intercession for the transgressors" in prayer.
  • Berakhot 5a uses 53:10, "it pleased the LORD to bruise him," as the proof text for yissurin shel ahavah, "afflictions of love": suffering that visits the one God delights in.

Much of this material is gathered, with English translations, in the two-volume collection by Adolf Neubauer and S. R. Driver, The Fifty-Third Chapter of Isaiah according to the Jewish Interpreters (Oxford, 1876–77), which remains the place to see the full range for yourself.

What do these texts show? That the rabbis read Scripture with many voices at once, applying the servant to Israel, to the righteous, to Moses, to the Messiah, to any sufferer God loves. What they do not show is a dying-and-rising divine Messiah whose death atones for the world. A suffering Messiah, yes, in some aggadic texts; the Messiah as God incarnate, no. Taking the midrash seriously means taking all of it, not quarrying one line.

The disputations: why this chapter still stings

At Barcelona in 1263, before King James I of Aragon, the Dominican friar Pablo Christiani, himself a convert from Judaism, cited Isaiah 53 against Nachmanides. Nachmanides replied that, according to the true meaning of the section, it speaks of the people of Israel. He also argued that the homiletical passages of the Talmud, the aggadah, are not obligatory for Jews, so a Christian could not prove his case by quarrying them. He won the argument on the floor, published his account, and was forced out of Aragon; he ended his life in the Land of Israel, where he founded the synagogue in Jerusalem's Old City that still bears his name. Three centuries later the Karaite Isaac of Troki compiled Chizzuk Emunah ("Faith Strengthened"), whose case for the Israel reading became a standard Jewish handbook.

When Jews hear Isaiah 53 quoted at them today, they hear that history: not a Bible study but a cross-examination, conducted by people with power over them. An honest Christian conversation about it has to start there.

Words in the Hebrew that matter

A few details the KJV smooths over:

  • 53:8, negaʻ lamo, "the stroke was upon them" or poetically "upon him." The form lamo is usually plural, which Jewish polemicists since the Middle Ages have pressed hard; Christian grammarians answer that it can be singular in poetry. It is a real ambiguity, not a knockout.
  • 53:9, be-motav, literally "in his deaths," plural. Collective readers take it as the many deaths of a people; others treat it as a plural of intensity or majesty.
  • 53:10, yirʼeh zeraʻ yaʼarikh yamim, "he shall see his seed, he shall prolong his days." Zeraʻ elsewhere means physical offspring, which is why Jewish readers balk at applying the verse to a figure who died childless. Latter-day Saint readers may notice that Abinadi faces the same word and reads "seed" as the prophets and those who hearken to them (Mosiah 15:10–13), a collective reading that a Jewish commentator would recognize.
  • 53:11, where the Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran and the Septuagint add "light": "he shall see light." The Masoretic text, which Jews read, lacks it.

The house of prayer, the eunuch, and a name

Isaiah 56 contains one of the most quoted verses in Jewish life: ki veiti beit tefillah yikkare le-khol ha-ʻammim, "mine house shall be called an house of prayer for all people" (56:7). The chapter welcomes the foreigner who "hath joined himself to the LORD" and the eunuch who keeps Sabbath, against the exclusions of Deuteronomy 23:1–3. Rabbinic law took the foreigner's path seriously: the convert is a full Jew. Notice how the welcome is conditioned. Three times, the qualification is keeping the Sabbath and taking "hold of my covenant" (56:2, 4, 6). Isaiah's universalism is not the dissolving of the covenant into general goodwill; it is the opening of the covenant's door.

And the eunuch's promise has become one of the most charged phrases in modern Jewish life. "Even unto them will I give in mine house and within my walls a place and a name," yad va-shem (56:5). In 1953 the Knesset gave that name to Israel's memorial to the six million, many of whom left no sons or daughters to carry their names. When you read 56:5 this week, know that it now names the place in Jerusalem whose work is to gather and keep their names.

Peace to the far and the near

The block ends badly for the wicked, "no peace, saith my God" (57:21), but just before it is a verse the Talmud turned into one of Judaism's boldest teachings on repentance. "Peace, peace to him that is far off, and to him that is near" (57:19). Rabbi Abbahu, in Berakhot 34b, notices the order: the far one is greeted first. Therefore, he says, in the place where penitents stand, even the completely righteous cannot stand. This is the Yom Kippur chapter, and that is its Yom Kippur message: the one who comes back from far away outranks the one who never left.

One more liturgical echo. "All thy children shall be taught of the LORD; and great shall be the peace of thy children" (54:13). Berakhot 64a, the last words of the tractate, reads banayikh, "your children," as bonayikh, "your builders": Torah scholars build peace. Jews recite it in the Sabbath liturgy. It is a good summary of how my tradition reads all eight chapters. The mother was not divorced. The children were not sold. The comfort comes slowly, and those who learn are the ones who build the house again.
