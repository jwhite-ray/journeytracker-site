The Book We Say Out Loud: Reading Tehillim in the Month of Elul

Written by an AI persona giving a Jewish reading of the Psalms in the voice of a rabbi — not a real rabbi.

A small confession about method. When a Latter-day Saint student opens Psalms, the natural question is what does this text mean? When a Jew opens Tehillim, the more native question is when do I say this one? Not a lower question — a different one, and it goes somewhere the first does not.

The Psalms are, for Jews, not primarily an anthology to be interpreted but the raw material of the prayer book. An old midrashic line gets at this with characteristic economy: "Moses gave them five books of the Torah, and corresponding to them David gave them the book of Psalms, which contains five books" (Midrash Tehillim 1:2). Five and five. Torah is what God says to Israel; Tehillim is what Israel says back. A Jew who prays the morning service has said several psalms before breakfast and will say more before bed, mostly without consciously "studying" any of them — the way you know a song rather than the way you know an argument.

So: twenty chapters this week, as they are actually used — and honesty at the places where our readings diverge.

You are reading this in Elul

Here is a piece of timing your manual cannot know about but which shapes everything for a Jewish reader. Rosh Chodesh Elul 5786 fell on 13–14 August 2026. This lesson week — August 17–23 — sits squarely inside Elul, the run-up to the High Holy Days. (Rosh Hashanah 5787 begins at sundown on 11 September 2026.) Which means that in synagogues around the world right now, twice a day, Jews are saying Psalm 27.

The custom is to recite L'David Hashem Ori v'Yish'i — "The Lord is my light and my salvation" — from the start of Elul through the end of the festival season, and the reason is rabbinic wordplay. The midrash reads the opening verse as a calendar: "'My light' — on Rosh Hashanah. 'And my help' — on Yom Kippur" (Midrash Tehillim 27:3; the same reading appears in Vayikra Rabbah 21:4). That passage also notes that the numerical value of ha-satan is 364 — one short of the solar year — so there are days on which the accuser has no standing. Later custom extended the pattern to verse 5, "he shall hide me in his pavilion," in Hebrew ba-sukkah, and heard Sukkot in it. Light, atonement, shelter: the whole autumn in a few verses.

Feel what this does to the psalm. Read as literature, Psalm 27 is a puzzle — serene confidence in verses 1–6, then a lurch into panic at verse 7, "Hear, O Lord, when I cry with my voice." Read liturgically, the seam is the point. This is the psalm of a person walking toward judgment who is confident and terrified at once, and who says so, out loud, every morning for forty days. "When my father and my mother forsake me, then the Lord will take me up" (27:10) is not a serene sentence. It is said by people who mean it.

Psalm 23 has no fixed place — and that is the point

Your lesson is titled from Psalm 23, so let me say something that may surprise you: in Jewish liturgy, Psalm 23 is not fixed anywhere. Rabbi Samuel Barth of the Jewish Theological Seminary puts it plainly — "this psalm has not found its way into any regular part of our fixed liturgy."

It lives at the edges instead. It is sung at se'udah shlishit, the third Shabbat meal, eaten in the deepening dark of late Shabbat afternoon, often with the lights off and everyone humming the same wordless melody a long while before anyone reaches the words. And it is said at funerals and at Yizkor, the memorial service — a use Barth notes is contemporary, shaped in part by the surrounding culture.

Those two homes tell you how Jews hear it. Latter-day Saint readers arrive at Psalm 23 with the Good Shepherd of John 10 already in mind, and the psalm becomes a portrait of Jesus. Jews arrive with no such figure, and what is left is starker and, I would argue, braver: God feeds me in front of the people who want me dead. Verse 5 is not pastoral. There is a table, and there are enemies watching you eat at it — provision happening inside the danger, not after it is removed. And gam ki elech b'gei tzalmavet is "though I walk through." Nobody is exempted from the valley.

Seven voices and eighteen names

Psalm 29 is a thunderstorm. Count the phrase kol Hashem, "the voice of the LORD" — verses 3, 4 (twice), 5, 7, 8, 9. Seven. A storm rolls in off the Mediterranean, snaps the cedars of Lebanon, moves inland to the wilderness of Kadesh, and the poet hears in it not weather but speech.

The Talmud found something else. Asking why the Amidah — the standing prayer, the spine of every Jewish service — has eighteen blessings, Rabbi Hillel son of Rabbi Shmuel bar Naḥmani answers: "corresponding to the eighteen mentions of God's name that King David said in the psalm 'Give unto the LORD, O you sons of might'" (Berakhot 28b). The central prayer of Judaism is built to the measure of Psalm 29. Fittingly, it is sung at Kabbalat Shabbat on Friday night and again on Shabbat morning as the Torah scroll is carried back to the ark — a procession psalm for a procession.

Its weekday counterpart is Psalm 24, sung as the Torah returns to the ark on ordinary days, festivals, and Rosh Chodesh. But Psalm 24 has an older job. The Mishnah records the psalms the Levites sang in the Temple, one for each day, and Sunday's was Psalm 24 (Mishnah Tamid 7:4). It is still the Sunday psalm in every Ashkenazi and Sephardi siddur. When your manual notes that "the hill of the LORD" (24:3) means the Temple mount, it is exactly right — and the antiphonal "Who is this King of glory?" of verses 7–10 is best imagined as what it probably was: call and response at a gate, with the gate personified and told to raise its head.

The house David never built

Psalm 30 opens the daily morning service in the Ashkenazi rite — it is literally the first psalm of Pesukei d'Zimra, the "verses of song." And it opens with a superscription that does not fit: Mizmor shir chanukat ha-bayit l'David, "a song for the dedication of the house, of David."

David did not build the house. That is the whole tension of 2 Samuel 7. So the heading is either about David's own palace, or the Temple site he bought, or — the traditional reading — a song written for a dedication he would not live to see. Whichever you choose, the psalm underneath is not about a building at all. It is about a man who was sick and got better: "O LORD my God, I cried unto thee, and thou hast healed me" (30:2). Somebody put a national, architectural, public heading on a private recovery from illness. That is the psalm's genius, and why it works as the daily opener: every ordinary morning is a small resurrection. Chanukah means dedication — which is why the word also names a festival about a rededicated house.

A historical honesty: this daily use is not ancient. Psalm 30 is absent from the Geonic prayer books and the classical medieval codes; by the usual account its daily recitation entered the rite through the circle of the Ari, R. Isaac Luria, in sixteenth-century Safed. Jewish liturgy is a living thing, still growing four hundred years ago.

Two smaller notes. Psalm 20 is said each weekday right after Tachanun, the penitential prayers, as their continuation; belonging to that mournful register, it is dropped on joyous days, though Sephardim and Ashkenazim draw the line differently (R. Eliezer Melamed, Peninei Halakha, Prayer 23:1). And the Talmud treats Psalms 1 and 2 as one unit: "'Happy is the man' and 'Why are the nations in an uproar' constitute a single chapter" (Berakhot 9b). Read that way, the pairing snaps into focus — the man who will not stand in the counsel of the wicked, then the wicked taking counsel against the LORD's anointed.

Where we part, and how

Three places diverge sharply. I raise them not to score points but because a reader who does not know they exist will misjudge what Jews are doing.

Psalm 2. Jewish tradition does read this psalm messianically. Sukkah 52a puts "Ask of me, and I shall give thee the heathen for thine inheritance" directly in the mouth of God addressing Messiah son of David — and in the same passage applies Psalm 21:5, "He asked life of thee, and thou gavest it him," to that same figure. Berakhot 7b hears the psalm's uproar of nations as the war of Gog and Magog. But then Rashi, writing in eleventh-century France under real pressure, says something extraordinary on 2:1: "Our Sages expounded the passage as referring to the King Messiah, but according to its apparent meaning, and for the refutation of the heretics, it is proper to interpret it as referring to David himself." Li-teshuvat ha-minim — as an answer to the sectarians. Rashi is telling you in the text that he chooses the plain sense partly because of the Christian argument. That is not dishonesty; it is a scholar showing his work. Note too that "this day have I begotten thee" (2:7) is heard in Jewish tradition as royal adoption language — the formula of coronation, not of generation.

Psalm 22. Your manual pairs 22:1 with Matthew 27:46, and rightly — the Gospels quote it. But Jews have a long-standing owner of that cry, and it is not David. The Talmud assigns Psalm 22 to Esther. Its superscription is ayelet ha-shachar, "the hind of the dawn," and Yoma 29a asks: "Why was Esther likened to the dawn? Just as the dawn is the conclusion of the entire night, so too Esther was the conclusion of all miracles." Then Megillah 15b narrates the moment she enters the inner court unsummoned: the Divine Presence departs from her, "and she immediately said: My God, my God, why have You forsaken me?" The same passage has her call Haman a dog (22:21, Hebrew numbering) and then a lion (22:22). Read that way, the psalm is the interior monologue of a woman walking into a throne room where arriving uninvited is a capital offense.

Which brings us to the hard verse. KJV 22:16 reads "they pierced my hands and my feet." The Masoretic Hebrew — at 22:17, one verse ahead — reads ka'ari yadai v'raglai, "like a lion, my hands and my feet." Rashi glosses it "as though they are crushed in a lion's mouth," pointing to Hezekiah's identical idiom in Isaiah 38:13. This is a real textual crux with ancient variants on both sides, and I will not pretend it away. But know that the Hebrew your Jewish neighbor reads says lion, and that this is no late evasion — it is what the consonants say.

Psalm 8. The angels appear here, and not as you might expect. In Shabbat 88b, Moses ascends to receive the Torah and the ministering angels protest: "Master of the Universe, what is one born of a woman doing here among us?" They argue by quoting this very psalm — "how glorious is Your name in all the earth, that Your majesty is placed above the heavens" (8:2 Hebrew, KJV 8:1), meaning: keep the Torah up here. Then they quote "What is man that You are mindful of him?" (8:5 Hebrew, KJV 8:4) against humanity. Moses wins. The verse your manual reads as humble wonder, the Talmud hears as the angels' sneer — and as a question God answered by handing the Torah to mortals anyway.

Small things that repay attention

The numbering. You just saw it. In psalms with a full superscription — "To the chief Musician… A Psalm of David" — the Hebrew counts that heading as verse 1, so everything after runs one number ahead of the KJV. That holds for Psalms 8, 19, 20, 21, 22, 30, 31, 40, and 46 in this block, but not for 23–29 or 33, whose shorter headings fold into verse 1. If you ever cite a psalm to a Jewish friend and get a blank look, this is usually why.

Selah. Nobody knows for certain; probably a musical direction. But the rabbis had a reading: "Wherever it states netzach, selah, or va'ed, the matter will never cease" (Eruvin 54a). Selah as a hold pedal — and this does not stop. Try it at Psalm 46:3, 7, 11.

"Be still." Psalm 46:10 in the KJV (46:11 in Hebrew) is harpu u-de'u ki anochi Elohim. Harpu is the imperative of a root meaning to slacken, drop, let go. The new JPS renders it bluntly: "Desist! Realize that I am God!" In context — verse 9 has just broken the bow and burned the chariot — this is not an invitation to contemplative quiet but a command to warring nations to put their weapons down. English readers have made "be still" a devotional whisper. In the Hebrew it is closer to a shout across a battlefield.

Psalm 40:6. "Mine ears hast thou opened" is oznayim karita li — literally, "ears You have dug for me." The Greek translators rendered it "a body You have prepared for me," and that Greek is what Hebrews 10:5 quotes. Worth knowing, if only to see how much theological weight a translation can carry.

Saying Psalms for the sick

Let me close with a tension in my own tradition, because it is instructive.

There is no more universal Jewish practice than saying Tehillim for a sick person. A name is passed around, a group divides the 150 chapters, and the whole book gets said. This is how ordinary Jews meet catastrophe.

And the Talmud is against it. "One is prohibited from healing himself with words of Torah" (Shevuot 15b). Sanhedrin 101a lists among those with no share in the World to Come "one who whispers invocations over a wound." The concern is real — that scripture becomes a technology, a charm deployed on a body rather than a prayer offered to a Person. One qualification is allowed: "to protect oneself is different," so a healthy person may say verses for protection. But the line is drawn.

How did the practice survive the prohibition? Because saying Tehillim for the sick is not, in the end, an incantation over a wound. It is a room full of people who cannot do anything, doing the only thing. The psalms are not applied to the illness; they are said in its presence, by people who are afraid, in the words David was afraid in. That is not magic. That is Psalm 27 in Elul: confident and terrified, out loud, again tomorrow.
