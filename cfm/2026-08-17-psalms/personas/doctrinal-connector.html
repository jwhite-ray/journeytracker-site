Green Pastures and the Holy Hill

Written by an AI persona connecting the block to Restoration scripture and modern teaching — not a real person.

Two psalms sit side by side in this week's block, and they say opposite things. Psalm 23 says the Lord takes you where you need to go — pasture, water, valley, table, house — and asks nothing of you but that you follow. Psalm 24 asks a hard entrance question: "Who shall ascend into the hill of the Lord? or who shall stand in his holy place?" and answers with a standard most of us fail on any given Tuesday: "He that hath clean hands, and a pure heart" (Psalm 24:3–4).

Israel apparently sang both. The Restoration does something more interesting than choose between them — it supplies the machinery that connects them.

The Shepherd who calls you by name

Psalm 23 is usually read as consolation, and it is. But Alma reads it as an interrogation. In Alma 5 he takes the shepherd image and turns it into a question about voice recognition: "the good shepherd doth call you; yea, and in his own name he doth call you, which is the name of Christ; and if ye will not hearken unto the voice of the good shepherd, to the name by which ye are called, behold, ye are not the sheep of the good shepherd" (Alma 5:38). Then the sharp edge: "And now if ye are not the sheep of the good shepherd, of what fold are ye? Behold, I say unto you, that the devil is your shepherd" (Alma 5:39). Alma's point is that everyone is in somebody's fold. The comfort of Psalm 23 is not free-floating; it is the comfort available to those who have learned a particular voice. And Alma gives a test that is behavioral rather than emotional: "if a man bringeth forth good works he hearkeneth unto the voice of the good shepherd" (Alma 5:41).

The Book of Mormon then does something no other text does with the shepherd figure. In 3 Nephi 15, the resurrected Christ quotes His own saying from John 10 — "other sheep I have which are not of this fold; them also I must bring, and they shall hear my voice; and there shall be one fold, and one shepherd" (3 Nephi 15:17) — and tells the Nephites that His Jerusalem disciples had misread it: "they supposed it had been the Gentiles" (3 Nephi 15:22). He identifies the other sheep as the people standing in front of Him. This is the only place in scripture where the Shepherd corrects the interpretation of His own parable. It also means Psalm 23's "I shall not want" has a geography we would not otherwise have known about. The flock was always larger than the fold anyone could see.

Notice, too, where Psalm 23 ends: "I will dwell in the house of the Lord for ever" (Psalm 23:6). The shepherd psalm terminates in the temple, which is exactly where Psalm 24 picks up — and where Psalms 26 and 27 stay ("I have loved the habitation of thy house," Psalm 26:8; "that I may dwell in the house of the Lord all the days of my life," Psalm 27:4). The sequence in this block is not accidental. Pasture leads to house.

Who shall ascend

The Church's own lesson page notes that "the hill of the Lord" (Psalm 24:3) likely refers to the temple mount, and it directs readers to Elder David A. Bednar's "Clean Hands and a Pure Heart" (October 2007 general conference). That talk is worth engaging carefully, because it makes a distinction the psalm itself only implies. Elder Bednar observes that "It is possible for us to have clean hands but not have a pure heart," and that both are required. He then maps them onto two different operations of the Atonement: hands are made clean "through the process of putting off the natural man and by overcoming sin," while "Hearts are purified as we receive His strengthening power to do good and become better." Cleansing and redeeming power on one side; sanctifying and strengthening power on the other.

That reading turns Psalm 24:4 from a checkpoint into a description of two ongoing processes — which is precisely how the Doctrine and Covenants describes the Savior's own development. D&C 93 says the Son "received not of the fulness at the first, but received grace for grace," and "continued from grace to grace, until he received a fulness" (D&C 93:12–13), and then extends the pattern to us: "you shall receive grace for grace" (D&C 93:20). Psalm 24 asks who may ascend. D&C 93 answers that ascent is incremental and that Christ Himself did it that way first. The recommend interview is a periodic reading on a trajectory, not a final grade.

The footnote apparatus in the Church's edition of Psalm 24:3 makes the connection editorially, pointing readers to 1 Nephi 15:33, Mosiah 15:23, D&C 76:62, and Moses 6:57 — to the celestial promise that "These shall dwell in the presence of God and his Christ forever and ever" (D&C 76:62), and to the principle that no unclean thing can dwell in God's presence. When the Saints dedicated a temple, the prayer they were given carried Psalm 24's standard forward almost verbatim: "no unclean thing shall be permitted to come into thy house" (D&C 109:20). Psalm 30's superscription reads "A Psalm and Song at the dedication of the house of David." The genre did not die out; D&C 109 is its Restoration instance.

The Joseph Smith Translation's reversal

Here is the part of Psalm 24 most readers never see. The King James text ends with a processional: "Lift up your heads, O ye gates; and be ye lift up, ye everlasting doors; and the King of glory shall come in" (Psalm 24:7). The Joseph Smith Translation of these verses, printed in the appendix of the Latter-day Saint edition, replaces the gates with people: "Lift up your heads, O ye generations of Jacob; and be ye lifted up; and the Lord strong and mighty; the Lord mighty in battle, who is the king of glory, shall establish you forever" (JST, Psalm 24:7). And then: "And he will roll away the heavens; and will come down to redeem his people; to make you an everlasting name; to establish you upon his everlasting rock" (JST, Psalm 24:8).

The direction of travel flips. In the KJV, the temple gates open upward and the King enters. In the JST, the covenant people are the ones told to lift up their heads, and the King comes down to redeem them. Read against verses 3–4, this is not a small edit. The psalm asks who is worthy to ascend the hill; the JST answers that before anyone ascends, He descends. That is the same logic the Restoration applies everywhere — the condescension precedes the ascent (compare 1 Nephi 11:16–33) — and it is the logic that keeps Psalm 24:4 from becoming despair. Whatever the temple asks of us in the way of clean hands, it is built on the prior fact that He rolled away the heavens and came down.

Nephi writes a psalm

If you want evidence that the psalmic form is native to Restoration scripture and not merely quoted by it, read 2 Nephi 4:16–35 immediately after Psalm 22 or Psalm 31. Nephi's lament follows the same architecture: an opening declaration of delight in the Lord, a collapse into self-accusation, a recollection of past deliverances, a self-rebuke ("Why should I yield to sin?"), and a closing vow of trust. Psalm 22 runs the same arc, from "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?" (Psalm 22:1) to "I will declare thy name unto my brethren: in the midst of the congregation will I praise thee" (Psalm 22:22). Psalm 31 does it in miniature: the broken vessel of verse 12 becomes "But I trusted in thee, O Lord: I said, Thou art my God" in verse 14. Nephi lands in the same place: he knows in whom he has trusted.

This matters doctrinally, not just formally. Lament is canonical. A person who cannot say "I am a worm, and no man" (Psalm 22:6) has not been given a scriptural vocabulary adequate to their week. The Restoration did not soften the psalmists' despair; it reproduced it, in a prophet's own voice, on the small plates.

Psalms 22 and 31 are also the two psalms Jesus prayed from the cross — "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?" (Matthew 27:46, quoting Psalm 22:1) and "Into thine hand I commit my spirit" (Luke 23:46, quoting Psalm 31:5). He died reciting Israel's hymnbook. Psalm 22:16 — "they pierced my hands and my feet" — receives an independent, physical witness half a world away when the resurrected Lord invites the Nephites to "feel the prints of the nails in my hands and in my feet" (3 Nephi 11:14). Alma pushes further than the psalm does, describing a Savior who takes upon Him "the pains and the sicknesses of his people" and their infirmities, "that he may know according to the flesh how to succor his people" (Alma 7:11–12). Psalm 22 shows us the suffering. The Book of Mormon tells us what it was for.

What is man

Psalm 8 asks the Restoration's favorite question: "What is man, that thou art mindful of him?... For thou hast made him a little lower than the angels, and hast crowned him with glory and honour" (Psalm 8:4–5). The footnote in the Latter-day Saint edition flags the Hebrew of verse 5 as "less than the gods" and tags the verse with the Topical Guide entry Man, Potential to Become like Heavenly Father.

Moses 1 is Psalm 8 dramatized. Moses sees the works of God's fingers, and his conclusion is the psalm's first half at maximum volume: "man is nothing, which thing I never had supposed" (Moses 1:10). Then God supplies the second half: "this is my work and my glory — to bring to pass the immortality and eternal life of man" (Moses 1:39). Smallness and crowning are held in the same frame. D&C 76 completes the arc for those who receive the fulness: "they are gods, even the sons of God" (D&C 76:58). Psalm 8 is not flattery, and Moses 1:10 is not self-loathing. Both are true at once, and the Restoration insists on keeping them both.

Elder Gérald Caussé quoted Psalm 8:3–4 in the April 2026 general conference, drawing the same conclusion: "Every soul carries infinite worth in the eyes of our Creator," and though we may seem small physically, "each of us is personally known, remembered, and loved" by the Father and the Son ("Love All; Love Each," April 2026).

Psalm 19 runs the argument from the other end: the heavens declare God's glory (19:1) and the law of the Lord converts the soul (19:7) — two witnesses, creation and revelation, in one poem. Alma makes the first argument to Korihor in nearly the psalm's own terms, insisting that "all things denote there is a God" (Alma 30:44). Psalm 29 adds a third witness, the voice — seven times "the voice of the Lord," breaking cedars and shaking wilderness before it gives "strength unto his people" and blesses them "with peace" (29:11).

Forgiven, not merely covered

Psalm 32 opens: "Blessed is he whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered" (32:1). The Joseph Smith Translation refuses the second clause: "Blessed are they whose transgressions are forgiven, and who have no sins to be covered" (JST, Psalm 32:1). That is a theological correction, not a stylistic one. Covering implies a sin still present, merely hidden. The JST asserts removal.

The Doctrine and Covenants says the same in the Lord's voice: "he who has repented of his sins, the same is forgiven, and I, the Lord, remember them no more" (D&C 58:42). The next verse names the mechanism the psalm had already described — "By this ye may know if a man repenteth of his sins — behold, he will confess them and forsake them" (D&C 58:43) — which is exactly Psalm 32:5. The JST makes a similar move on Psalm 30:5, expanding "his anger endureth but a moment" into a statement about repentance: the wicked "repent, and in a moment it is turned away, and they are in his favor, and he giveth them life" (JST, Psalm 30:5). In both cases the Restoration reads Israel's poetry and asks: by what process?

Be still — and Zion shall not be moved

Psalm 46 is the last text in the block and the one most often reduced to a decorative phrase. Its two hinges are verse 5, "God is in the midst of her; she shall not be moved," and verse 10, "Be still, and know that I am God."

In December 1833, with the Saints driven from Jackson County, the revelation now printed as D&C 101 picked up both hinges within two verses: "let your hearts be comforted concerning Zion; for all flesh is in mine hands; be still and know that I am God. Zion shall not be moved out of her place, notwithstanding her children are scattered" (D&C 101:16–17). That is not a meditation cue. It is a command issued to refugees who had just lost their homes, and it holds the psalm's confidence in a situation where that confidence looked absurd. Psalm 46 was probably sung under comparable pressure — "though the earth be removed, and though the mountains be carried into the midst of the sea" (46:2).

Which is finally why these twenty chapters were Israel's hymnbook and remain a Latter-day Saint one. President Dallin H. Oaks, then of the Quorum of the Twelve, observed that "Our hymns contain matchless doctrinal sermons, surpassed only by the scriptures in their truth and poetic impact" ("Worship through Music," October 1994). In the Psalms those two categories are the same thing. The shepherd leads you to the house; the King of glory comes down to meet you at its gates; and the hands you bring, still being washed, are the ones He takes.
