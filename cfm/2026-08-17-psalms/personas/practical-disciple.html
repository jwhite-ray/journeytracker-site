The Psalms Will Not Let You Be Vague

Written by an AI persona asking what this block requires of an ordinary disciple this week — not a real person.

Twenty chapters this week, and almost none of them are about ideas. They are about a body that will not sleep, hands that are or are not clean, a man being lied about by his neighbors, and a man who cannot get an answer and says so out loud, at length, in public worship. If you read Psalms 1–2; 8; 19–33; 40; and 46 looking for doctrine, you will find some. If you read them looking for assignments, you will find more than you can do in a week.

Permission to pray badly, in writing

Psalm 22 opens with "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? why art thou so far from helping me, and from the words of my roaring?" (22:1). Verse 2 is worse: "I cry in the daytime, but thou hearest not; and in the night season, and am not silent." That is not a prelude to the real prayer. That is the prayer, and it was set to music, given to the chief Musician, and sung by the congregation.

This is not a fluke of one psalm. Claus Westermann's work on the Psalter's forms argues that lament is the single most common type of psalm — the complaint is not the exception in Israel's hymnbook, it is the default (Praise and Lament in the Psalms). Walter Brueggemann built a whole reading of the Psalms around the observation that the book moves through orientation, disorientation, and new orientation, because that is the actual shape of a life of faith rather than the shape we wish it had (The Message of the Psalms).

The practical consequence is small and specific. Most of us pray in a register we would never use with a friend: we open with gratitude, we soften the hard part into a request for patience, and we close before we have said the true thing. Try one prayer this week — one, on a weekday, not Sunday — where the first sentence out of your mouth is the complaint, in the words you would use at a kitchen table, with the name of the person or the diagnosis or the number in it. Say it, and then stop. Do not resolve it in the same session.

Because notice what Psalm 22 does not do. It reaches praise by verse 22 — "I will declare thy name unto my brethren: in the midst of the congregation will I praise thee" — and nowhere between verse 1 and verse 22 does anything change. The bulls of Bashan are still there. The garments are still divided. Nobody says "and then the Lord delivered him." The turn is real and it is unearned by circumstance, which means the honest version of your prayer life may look like arriving at verse 22 on Thursday about something you were still stuck at verse 1 with on Tuesday. That is not backsliding. That is the form.

Confession has a return address

Psalm 32 is the most physically specific text in the block. "When I kept silence, my bones waxed old through my roaring all the day long. For day and night thy hand was heavy upon me: my moisture is turned into the drought of summer" (32:3–4). Whatever else this is, it is a report about what unspoken wrongdoing does to a body. Psalm 31 says the same thing from a different angle: "my strength faileth because of mine iniquity, and my bones are consumed" (31:10).

This is exactly the place where a devotional writer reaches for a health claim he cannot back, so: the nearest real research is James Pennebaker's expressive writing paradigm, and Pennebaker himself, reviewing four decades of it, reports that the average effect of expressive writing on health across more than a hundred studies is about d = .16 — modest, with replication failures and contested mechanisms ("Expressive Writing in Psychological Science," 2018). The honest statement is that there is real but small evidence that putting the hard thing into words changes something measurable, and there is a three-thousand-year-old poem that says keeping it unsaid dried him out like a summer field. Take the poem as testimony, not as a clinical finding.

What the psalm asks is not a feeling. It is verse 5: "I acknowledged my sin unto thee, and mine iniquity have I not hid." The unglamorous work is that most of what we owe has a return address. If you shaded a number on an expense report, the fix is an email with a corrected figure. If you told a story about a coworker that you knew was thinner than you made it sound, the fix is going back to the person you told it to. If you said something to your spouse about money that was technically true and functionally false, the fix is a sentence beginning "I need to correct something I said."

Psalm 24 puts the honesty requirement in exactly those terms: the one who ascends the hill is the one who "hath not lifted up his soul unto vanity, nor sworn deceitfully" (24:4). Sworn deceitfully. Not "thought impure thoughts" — made a promise he shaped to be misread. That is a workplace sin, a family sin, a Tuesday sin.

Two audits, not one

Psalm 19:12–13 asks for two different things and it is worth refusing to blur them: "Who can understand his errors? cleanse thou me from secret faults. Keep back thy servant also from presumptuous sins; let them not have dominion over me."

Secret faults are the ones you cannot see. By definition you will not find them by introspection — that is what makes them secret. The only real method is to ask someone with standing to tell you: a spouse, an adult child, a colleague who reports to you. The question has to be narrow enough to be answerable. Not "how am I doing?" but "what do I do that makes it harder to work with me?" or "when I get short with the kids, what happens right before?" Then you say thank you and you do not argue. Arguing is how you teach someone never to tell you again.

Presumptuous sins are the opposite category: the ones you can see perfectly well and have built a justification around. The word marks intent — these are the deliberate ones, and the psalmist's fear is not that he will stumble into them but that they will "have dominion." These are found by a different method: write down the three rationalizations you used most this month. "Everyone does it." "I'll make it up next quarter." "She started it." "It's not technically lying." Each rationalization is load-bearing for something. Find out what.

Do these as two separate lists on two separate sheets, because if you merge them you will spend the whole exercise on the comfortable one.

Clean hands as an exam, not a verdict

Psalm 24:3–4 reads like a bouncer at a door: "Who shall ascend into the hill of the Lord? or who shall stand in his holy place? He that hath clean hands, and a pure heart." Read as a qualification it disqualifies everyone. Elder David A. Bednar's reading is more useful precisely because it splits the requirement in two: hands are made clean as we put off the natural man and overcome sin through the Savior's Atonement; hearts are purified as we receive His strengthening power to become better ("Clean Hands and a Pure Heart," October 2007). Two different works, two different helps.

Which means there is a diagnostic question hiding in the verse, and it is uncomfortable: what have you stopped doing that you still miss? Clean hands with an unpurified heart is a real condition — the behavior is gone and the appetite is intact — and it is not hypocrisy, it is an unfinished stage. Naming it is not a confession of failure. It is knowing which of the two works you are currently in.

David does not wait to be examined; he requests it. "Examine me, O Lord, and prove me; try my reins and my heart" (26:2). That is a stranger prayer than it looks. Try praying it and meaning it, once, and see whether anything comes up within forty-eight hours that you would rather not have thought about.

"Be still" is a scheduling problem

Psalm 46:10 — "Be still, and know that I am God" — is not, in the Hebrew, primarily about quiet. The verb is harpû, a form of a root meaning to slacken, to let drop, to relax one's grip (Strong's H7503). Nearer to "let go" than to "hush."

And it is hard in a way that is worth taking seriously rather than sentimentalizing. In a set of studies published in Science, Timothy Wilson and colleagues found that people did not enjoy sitting alone for six to fifteen minutes with nothing to do but think; in one study, among participants who had already said they would pay money to avoid being shocked again, 67 percent of men (12 of 18) and 25 percent of women (6 of 24) gave themselves at least one electric shock rather than sit with their own thoughts ("Just think: The challenges of the disengaged mind," 2014). The samples are small and the gender gap is a footnote, not a finding to lean on. The core result is enough: unstructured stillness is aversive, and we will reach for almost anything else.

So do not tell yourself you will be still. Schedule it against something. Ten minutes, phone in another room — not face-down on the table, in another room — same time daily, and accept that it will feel like nothing happened. Psalm 27:14 is the companion instruction and the hardest sentence in the block: "Wait on the Lord: be of good courage, and he shall strengthen thine heart: wait, I say, on the Lord." He repeats it because nobody obeys it the first time. Waiting is the one spiritual practice with no output you can show anyone.

Do not rush anyone from Psalm 22 to Psalm 23

The two psalms sit next to each other, and we love the second one. This week someone in your ward or your family is in the first one, and the strongest temptation in ministering is to move them along.

There is old wisdom worth borrowing here. Jewish law on visiting a mourner is explicit: "The comforters are not permitted to open [conversation] until the mourner opens first" (Shulchan Arukh, Yoreh De'ah 376:1), a ruling drawn from Job's friends, who sat with him seven days and said nothing "for they saw that his grief was very great" (Job 2:13). The visitor chooses neither the subject nor the timing; the visitor is present and lets the grieving person set the agenda, including the agenda of silence.

Concretely, for this week: if you are ministering to someone in a hard stretch, go, and do not bring a lesson. Ask one question and then stop talking. Do not say "at least." Do not offer the reframe, however true it is — Psalm 22:24 is true, that God "hath not despised nor abhorred the affliction of the afflicted," but it is theirs to arrive at, not yours to hand them. And bring something that solves a logistics problem: a meal, a ride, an hour of childcare. Psalm 22:26 — "the meek shall eat and be satisfied" — is not a metaphor you need to explain.

When Psalm 1 does not describe your life

Psalm 1 promises that the person who delights in the law will be "like a tree planted by the rivers of water... and whatsoever he doeth shall prosper" (1:3). The verb behind "prosper" (tsalach, Strong's H6743) carries the sense of pushing forward, advancing, succeeding at what one sets out to do.

Plenty of faithful readers will get to that verse on a Tuesday morning and think: that is not my life. The layoff happened anyway. The treatment did not work. The child left anyway. It is worth noticing that the Psalter does not leave the promise standing unqualified — the very next psalms in this block dismantle the arithmetic. Psalm 30:6–7: "in my prosperity I said, I shall never be moved... thou didst hide thy face, and I was troubled." Psalm 22:1 in its entirety. Psalm 31:12: "I am forgotten as a dead man out of mind: I am like a broken vessel." The book that opens with the prosperity of the righteous also contains the sentence Christ chose from the cross (compare 22:1 with Matthew 27:46).

The practical move is to keep the practice and drop the ledger. Psalm 1:2 says the blessed man's "delight is in the law of the Lord; and in his law doth he meditate day and night," and that is an hours claim, not a mood. Day and night is not fifteen minutes before bed on the nights you remember. So audit the hours honestly this week — where does the reading time actually come from, and what gets cut for it? — and make the trade without attaching a payout to it. The tree by the water is not planted because the fruit is guaranteed on your schedule. It is planted because that is where the water is.

Questions for the week

  1. What is the true first sentence of the prayer I have been opening with something safer?
  2. Who is the actual person on the other end of the thing I have not made right — name, not category — and what is the specific sentence I owe them?
  3. What are my three most-used rationalizations this month, and what is each one holding up?
  4. What have I stopped doing that I still miss?
  5. Whom will I ask, this week, "what do I do that makes it harder to be around me?" — and can I hear the answer without arguing?
  6. When, on the calendar, are my ten minutes of stillness, and where will the phone be?
  7. Who in my circle is in Psalm 22 right now, and what is the logistical problem I could solve for them without saying a word about it?
  8. Where did this week's study hours actually come from — and if the answer is nowhere, what am I willing to give up?
