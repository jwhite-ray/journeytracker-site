September 28–October 4, 2026 · Come, Follow Me

Isaiah 40–49

"Comfort Ye My People" · the Church's lesson page

Gods on the Oxcart, Names on the Hands

≈30 min · listen or read · the week's introduction — not one of the five sessions

The book turns to Babylon this week, and the hosts bring in the panel's Hebrew Bible scholar, who explains how the gods of Babylon ended up as cargo on tired pack animals. Along the way Zion wins the first round of an argument with God, and the “waters of baptism” turn out to be an 1840 gloss. Then the conversation takes on Cyrus and the brass plates, “I create evil,” “no God beside me,” and a servant nobody can pin down.

Read Isaiah 39 and 40 back to back and you can feel the floor move. Chapter 39 ends in the late eighth century BC, with Hezekiah showing his treasury to envoys from Babylon and Isaiah warning that all of it "shall be carried to Babylon" (39:6). Chapter 40 opens after the blow has landed. Jerusalem's "warfare is accomplished," her "iniquity is pardoned," and she "hath received of the LORD's hand double for all her sins" (40:2). The exile is no longer a warning; it is the setting. Chapter 40 has no date formula, no king of Judah, and no Assyria. By chapter 44 a Persian king is named as the one who will say to Jerusalem, "Thou shalt be built" (44:28), and by chapter 48 the order is to leave: "Go ye forth of Babylon, flee ye from the Chaldeans" (48:20).

That turn is the whole week's first question. The lesson takes a clear position: these prophecies "were meant to comfort the Jewish people over 150 years in the future." Mainstream biblical scholarship takes the opposite position, and has for about two centuries. It assigns chapters 40–55 to an anonymous prophet among the exiles in the 540s BC, usually called Second Isaiah or Deutero-Isaiah. The honest questioner puts the lesson's stance fairly: it is a legitimate faith position, but not the position of most biblical scholarship, and readers deserve to know that. The stakes are higher for Latter-day Saints than for anyone else at the table, because Nephi quotes chapters 48 and 49 in full (1 Nephi 20–21) from brass plates that left Jerusalem around 600 BC. That is roughly sixty years before Cyrus. This guide lays out the question where it bites hardest, in Session 4, and does not pretend it is settled in either direction.

There are other ways into these chapters than the authorship question, and the panel uses all of them. For Jewish readers, Isaiah 40 arrives every August on the Sabbath after the fast of the ninth of Av, the day of mourning for both Temples; for a Jew, Isaiah 40 is less a chapter than a date on the calendar, what you hear after the worst day of the year. For the practical disciple, what does not change under any answer is the listener: someone tired, displaced, and suspicious that God has stopped paying attention. And the honest questioner warns that these chapters raise problems a careful reader cannot talk away. Some of these knots loosen. Some do not.

The five sessions follow the block's own movements. Session 1 (chapters 40–41) takes the double comfort, the herald's road, the complaint "My way is hid from the LORD," and the first trial of the gods. Session 2 (chapters 42–43) meets the servant, gentle and then blind, and ends in the courtroom: "Ye are my witnesses." Session 3 (chapters 44–45) is the most argued-over stretch: the idol-maker's woodpile, Cyrus the "anointed," "I make peace, and create evil," and "beside me there is no God." Session 4 (chapters 46–48) watches Babylon's gods loaded onto pack animals, reads the chapter Nephi chose to copy, and states the brass-plates problem plainly. Session 5 (chapter 49) reads the second servant song, Nephi's "nursing fathers," and Zion's complaint that she has been forgotten, and closes with a capstone that runs Donald W. Parry's six ways of reading Isaiah across 49:14–16.

The questions at the end of each session are not a quiz. Most have more than one defensible answer, and some ask about your own life rather than the text.

25–30 minSession 1: The Voice in the Desert

Reading assignment: Isaiah 40, 41

Comfort, twice

The chapter opens with a doubled word: "Comfort ye, comfort ye my people, saith your God" (40:1). In the synagogue the reader sings it, Nachamu, nachamu ʻami, five days after Jews sit on the floor reading Lamentations by candlelight, and the Sabbath takes its name from that first word, Shabbat Nachamu. The rabbis of Pesikta de-Rav Kahana noticed the doubling and built a sermon on it. Jerusalem sinned doubly (the Hebrew of Lamentations 1:8 repeats the root), she was punished doubly, "double for all her sins" (40:2), and so she is comforted doubly.

"Double for all her sins" can sound like punishment beyond what was deserved. The midrash does not explain that away. It says that however much the suffering was, the comfort will match it. There is no pretending the catastrophe was small. The same homily opens with a line from Job ("Shall mortal man be more just than God?") and ties it to Boaz, who spoke kindly to a poor foreign widow in Ruth 2. If a man of flesh and blood gives comfort, the midrash has God reason, how could I not? The argument for God's compassion is drawn from a decent human being's behavior. Hold on to that: the comfort of Isaiah 40 does not begin by minimizing the grief.

A voice, a road, and a punctuation mark

Then a voice: "The voice of him that crieth in the wilderness, Prepare ye the way of the LORD, make straight in the desert a highway for our God" (40:3). Most readers hear John the Baptist, because the Gospels do (Mark 1:3; John 1:23), and because Handel set it that way. Punctuation decides 40:3. The Masoretic accents put the wilderness with the road, not the voice: "In the wilderness prepare ye the way of the LORD," parallel to "make straight in the desert." The Qumran Community Rule read it that way and sent its members into the desert to prepare the way by studying the Law. The Gospels follow the Greek, which puts the voice in the wilderness. The punctuation carries a theology with it.

The rabbi quotes the Jewish Publication Society's rendering, "A voice rings out: 'Clear in the desert a road for the LORD!'", and draws the consequence. The voice is not in the wilderness. The road is. The academic describes what the poet first pictured: a processional highway across the desert from Babylon to Zion, with the LORD processing at its head as Babylon's gods did in theirs. At the head of that procession is a shepherd who "shall gather the lambs with his arm, and carry them in his bosom, and shall gently lead those that are with young" (40:11).

Restoration scripture takes up the cry in a third direction. Lehi applies it to John the Baptist (1 Nephi 10:8), as the Gospels do, and then the Doctrine and Covenants makes "Prepare ye the way of the Lord" the message of latter-day missionaries and of an angel crying through heaven (D&C 33:10; 65:1; 133:17). Isaiah's road was for a God returning with his exiles. The Restoration makes it the path of a Second Coming.

"What shall I cry?"

The next verses carry a small textual discovery. The KJV, following the Masoretic Text, reads "And he said, What shall I cry?" (40:6). A Qumran manuscript, the Greek, and the Vulgate read "And I said." The small change turns 40:1–8 into a prophetic call, a counterpart to Isaiah 6, whoever the "I" is. The Great Isaiah Scroll also lacks part of 40:7–8 in its original hand; the longer wording was added later. For a book whose theme is "the word of our God shall stand for ever" (40:8), the evidence shows its scribes still adjusting that very verse.

"My way is hid from the LORD"

The second half of chapter 40 is an argument. God measures the waters in his hand (40:12), weighs the nations as "a drop of a bucket" (40:15), and mocks the man who picks "a tree that will not rot" and hires a craftsman to make a god "that shall not be moved" (40:20), a preview of chapter 44. Then the prophet quotes his audience: "Why sayest thou, O Jacob, and speakest, O Israel, My way is hid from the LORD, and my judgment is passed over from my God?" (40:27). Form critics call this a disputation: the prophet cites the people's complaint and rebuts it with questions ("Hast thou not known? hast thou not heard?" 40:28). These chapters argue more than they announce.

That quoted complaint is also one of the LDS scholar's main pieces of evidence for an exilic setting. The strongest argument is not that the text names Cyrus. The harder argument is about who the chapters are addressed to and how they argue. They are not written to Hezekiah's Jerusalem about a distant future; they talk to people who already feel forgotten.

The practical disciple reads the same verses for what they ask of a tired person. The prophet does not scold the complaint; he quotes it. God's answer is a description of himself, the one who "fainteth not, neither is weary" (40:28), and then the direction the strength flows: "He giveth power to the faint" (40:29). Verse 30 shuts the other door: "Even the youths shall faint and be weary." Youth, fitness, and willpower are named and dismissed as a strategy.

Two Hebrew words open up 40:31. "Wait" is qavah, a root associated with binding or twisting together, and so with expectation; "renew" is chalaph, to change or substitute. Read with those in mind, the verse describes waiting as active expectation, and renewal as an exchange: you bring your exhausted strength, and something else is put in its place. The last line runs from eagles to running to walking. The promise runs downhill in drama and uphill in usefulness. Most discipleship is the last clause, the walking without fainting through the third week of a hard month.

The first trial of the gods

Chapter 41 turns the argument into a courtroom. "Keep silence before me, O islands... let us come near together to judgment" (41:1). God asks who "raised up the righteous man from the east... and made him rule over kings" (41:2), and later, "I have raised up one from the north, and he shall come" (41:25). The figure is not yet named. Then the gods of the nations are summoned: "Produce your cause, saith the LORD; bring forth your strong reasons" (41:21). The test is prediction: "Shew the things that are to come hereafter, that we may know that ye are gods" (41:23). The defendants say nothing. The gods are silent, and the verdict follows: "ye are of nothing" (41:24).

This is where the authorship question first becomes a question about the argument itself. Critical scholars read the LORD's "former things" as older prophecies, including First Isaiah's, that have already come true, and Cyrus as the next case. A unity reading holds that Isaiah of Jerusalem wrote the whole lawsuit in advance. The LDS scholar's verdict is careful: either reading is coherent. They are not equally simple, and a candid reader should admit which one needs the extra assumption. The honest questioner adds a turn that cuts against the critics too. There is a sharp twist here. The text's whole case for its God is that he predicts and the idols cannot. If the chapters were written after Cyrus's rise, that argument is prophecy after the fact offered as proof of foreknowledge; if they were written before, dating them late cancels the very argument the text makes.

"Fear thou not"

Between the courtroom scenes come the week's most loved lines: "Fear thou not; for I am with thee: be not dismayed; for I am thy God: I will strengthen thee; yea, I will help thee; yea, I will uphold thee with the right hand of my righteousness" (41:10). The form has a history. Joachim Begrich proposed in 1934 that these "fear not" oracles copy a priest's answer to a worshipper who had just prayed a lament. The priestly setting is still debated; the comparative evidence is firmer. Oracles delivered to the Assyrian king Esarhaddon in the seventh century use the same "fear not" formula in the voice of the goddess Ishtar of Arbela. Isaiah 41:10, "Fear thou not; for I am with thee," uses an old royal oracle form, now addressed to a defeated people rather than a king.

Latter-day Saints sing these verses. The lesson traces "How Firm a Foundation" to 41:10, 43:2–5, and 46:4. President Thomas S. Monson closed an October 2006 talk with its paraphrase of 41:10, and in October 2021 President Russell M. Nelson, describing the reinforced footings of the Salt Lake Temple, said the hymn had come to have new meaning. The fit is apt: Isaiah's God promises Cyrus's Jerusalem, "to the temple, Thy foundation shall be laid" (44:28).

Chapter 41 ends with the servant named for the first time, "But thou, Israel, art my servant, Jacob whom I have chosen, the seed of Abraham my friend" (41:8), and with a promise to "make the wilderness a pool of water" (41:18). Carry the name "Israel" into Session 2. It is about to get complicated.

Think deeply.

  1. The midrash insists that comfort must match the size of the grief, and that God cannot let a human being like Boaz out-comfort him. When have you been offered comfort that shrank the loss first? How could you comfort someone this week without doing that?
  2. "In the wilderness prepare ye the way" (the road through the desert, the exiles' journey) and "the voice of him that crieth in the wilderness" (John the Baptist) are both live readings of 40:3, and the Restoration adds a third. Does it trouble you that a comma can carry a theology? Which reading do you most need, and why?
  3. The practical disciple asks: where have I quietly concluded that "my way is hid from the LORD"? Can you say it to God in words, as Israel did, instead of carrying it as a mood? What changes when a complaint is spoken rather than carried?
  4. The trial scenes argue that the LORD is God because he foretells. The honest questioner says that argument cuts both ways in the authorship debate. Which side does the twist actually hurt more?
  5. "Fear not" was a formula spoken to kings before it was spoken to exiles. Does learning that it is an old form make 41:10 feel borrowed, or does it make the promise larger, now that it is given to "thou worm Jacob"?

25–30 minSession 2: The Servant Who Is Blind

Reading assignment: Isaiah 42, 43

Behold my servant

"Behold my servant, whom I uphold; mine elect, in whom my soul delighteth; I have put my spirit upon him: he shall bring forth judgment to the Gentiles" (42:1). Study guides call 42:1–4 "the first servant song," and the name has a source. In 1892 Bernhard Duhm lifted four passages out of Isaiah (42:1–4; 49:1–6; 50:4–9; 52:13–53:12); he called them Ebed-Jahwe-Lieder, "servant of YHWH songs," and argued that they came from a different hand and described an individual. The idea has held up worse than its popularity suggests. Tryggve Mettinger argued in 1983 that the songs cannot be cleanly cut out and should be read where they stand. Read that way, the servant who brings judgment to the Gentiles in 42:1 sits a few verses before the servant of 42:19, who is blind and deaf. One title covers an ideal and a failure.

The lesson says the servant passages "can refer to Jesus Christ (see Isaiah 42:1–7), to the house of Israel (see Isaiah 45:4), and to King Cyrus." Every reader in this panel agrees the text lets the word move. Matthew 12:18–21 applies 42:1–4 to Jesus. The academic calls that a legitimate later reading: those are later readings of an open text; they do not settle it.

The rabbi wants you to hear the Jewish readings as a range rather than a single answer. Rashi reads "my servant" in 42:1 as Jacob, Israel itself, and the task of bringing judgment to the Gentiles as fulfilled when the nations come to learn God's ways (Isaiah 2:3). This is why the main Jewish reading takes the servant to be Israel, and it is not a defensive move. But the Aramaic Targum renders 42:1 "Behold my servant, the Messiah," and Radak opens his comment, "this is the King Messiah." So Jewish tradition read the servant as Israel, as the prophet, and as a coming king. The one thing it never did is the thing Christians most often do. For us, the servant is always the one God speaks to, never the one speaking as God. And he names what he thinks Christians miss: when you move 42:1–4 straight to Jesus, as Matthew 12 does, you skip over the tension the prophet builds on purpose.

The bruised reed

What kind of servant is this? "He shall not cry, nor lift up, nor cause his voice to be heard in the street. A bruised reed shall he not break, and the smoking flax shall he not quench" (42:2–3). The practical disciple notices the grammar. The servant's method is described almost entirely in negatives: he does not shout, does not break, does not quench. Yet verse 4 insists "He shall not fail nor be discouraged, till he have set judgment in the earth." Gentleness here is not the absence of purpose but its method.

The lesson invites readers to consider how servant passages apply to them, and this one applies easily. Everyone is responsible for some bruised reeds: a struggling child, a worn-out spouse, a coworker slipping for reasons you do not know, someone on a ministering list who stopped coming. The practical disciple's exercise is concrete. A useful exercise is to name one specific person who is a bruised reed right now and decide on one action this week that protects them instead of testing them.

The servant's commission follows: "I the LORD have called thee in righteousness... and give thee for a covenant of the people, for a light of the Gentiles; To open the blind eyes, to bring out the prisoners from the prison" (42:6–7). The doctrinal connector follows that job description into the Doctrine and Covenants, where the Lord sends "mine everlasting covenant into the world, to be a light to the world, and to be a standard for my people" (D&C 45:9), echoing 42:6, 49:22, and 40:3 at once. The servant has become a covenant and the people who carry it.

"Who is blind, but my servant?"

Then comes the verse the lesson's Scripture Helps singles out. "Hear, ye deaf; and look, ye blind, that ye may see. Who is blind, but my servant? or deaf, as my messenger that I sent?... Seeing many things, but thou observest not" (42:18–20). The chapter ends with a people "robbed and spoiled" (42:22), set on fire by God's anger, "yet he laid it not to heart" (42:25). The rabbi reads the whole chapter as one portrait. God has a servant who fails, who is robbed and spoiled (42:22), and who is still chosen.

Joseph Smith's revision of Isaiah reads 42:19 differently: "For I will send my servant unto you who are blind; yea, a messenger to open the eyes of the blind, and unstop the ears of the deaf." The blindness moves to the people: "Thou art a people, seeing many things, but thou observest not" (JST Isaiah 42:21). The Scripture Helps cite this as the answer to why the servant is called blind. The doctrinal connector, whose whole essay traces Restoration uses of Isaiah, describes it frankly. But the JST does more than clarify; it reverses the point. The KJV poem finds God's chosen instrument flawed and still chosen; the JST protects the servant and blames the people. Latter-day Saints receive it as inspired; honestly described, it is a different reading that fits 42:7 ("to open the blind eyes"), not a recovered original, and no ancient manuscript supports it. The honest questioner puts the cost in one line: a critical reader will notice that it removes a tension the Hebrew seems to hold on purpose: a servant who fails and is still chosen.

The canon as a whole does not choose one servant either. Paul and Barnabas apply "a light of the Gentiles" (49:6) to themselves in Acts 13:47, and D&C 86:11 applies the phrase to priesthood holders. The canon itself refuses to pin down a single referent. The servant keeps moving, and scripture moves with him.

"I have called thee by thy name"

Chapter 43 begins with what may be the most personal promise in the block: "Fear not: for I have redeemed thee, I have called thee by thy name; thou art mine. When thou passest through the waters, I will be with thee... when thou walkest through the fire, thou shalt not be burned" (43:1–2). Notice to whom it is spoken. The Jacob who in 42:25 "laid it not to heart" is, with no transition, "precious in my sight" (43:4).

The promise turns into a gathering: "I will say to the north, Give up; and to the south, Keep not back: bring my sons from far, and my daughters from the ends of the earth" (43:6). In a 2018 youth devotional, President Nelson extended the gathering of Israel to God's children "on both sides of the veil," tying verses like this to temple work for the dead. The doctrinal connector labels it clearly: that expansion is offered as revelation, not exegesis.

Verse 7 then says of everyone called by God's name, "I have created him for my glory," and verse 21 explains what the glory is: "This people have I formed for myself; they shall shew forth my praise." In Isaiah, God's glory is the praise his people give. Moses 1:39 turns the word around: "this is my work and my glory—to bring to pass the immortality and eternal life of man." God's glory stops being what humans give him and becomes what he does for them.

"Ye are my witnesses"

Now the second trial. The blind and deaf servant is brought into court as a witness: "Bring forth the blind people that have eyes, and the deaf that have ears" (43:8). The nations are asked to produce their witnesses; the LORD produces his: "Ye are my witnesses, saith the LORD, and my servant whom I have chosen... I, even I, am the LORD; and beside me there is no saviour" (43:10–11). The LDS scholar describes the evidence God offers in these scenes: his own evidence is that he announced "the former things" before they happened and is now announcing "new things" (42:9; 48:3–7).

The lesson turns this into an exercise: imagine being called to the witness stand in a trial where Jesus Christ is charged with making the claim of 43:11. The doctrinal connector explains the assumption behind it. The Bible Dictionary identifies Jehovah as the premortal Jesus Christ. Read this way, 43:11 is Christ's own claim, as the lesson assumes when it asks you to testify for Jesus in the courtroom of Isaiah 43.

The rabbi brings a much older reading of the same verse, and it raises the stakes of being a witness. The Sifre on Deuteronomy reads 43:12, "ye are my witnesses, saith the LORD, that I am God," almost to the breaking point: when you are My witnesses, I am God; when you are not My witnesses, I am not God, so to speak. God's name in the world rides on a flawed people's testimony. Remember who these witnesses are: the blind and deaf of 43:8. For a Jew that is the servant's burden, and it is a heavy one.

One phrase in 43:10 presses on Restoration theology in a particular way: "before me there was no God formed, neither shall there be after me." Readers who know the Restoration teaching that God's children can become like him will feel that verse press. The Scripture Helps read the verse as aimed at idols and point to the Gospel Topics essay "Becoming Like God," which insists that God's children will always worship him. The honest questioner's judgment: that is a coherent answer, but it comes from outside the text; Isaiah's poet shows no sign of imagining the question. Session 3 takes up the larger monotheism question.

A new thing

The chapter promises "a new thing" (43:19), a new exodus with "rivers in the desert," and then, right after "thou hast wearied me with thine iniquities" (43:24), the lesson's verse: "I, even I, am he that blotteth out thy transgressions for mine own sake, and will not remember thy sins" (43:25). No repentance is described first. The forgiveness is "for mine own sake."

Think deeply.

  1. The servant in 42:1 delights God, and the servant in 42:19 is blind. Mettinger says read them together: "one title covers an ideal and a failure." The JST separates them. What is gained, and what is lost, when a text lets God's chosen instrument be both?
  2. The rabbi says that moving 42:1–4 straight to Jesus skips the tension the prophet built on purpose. Try reading the first servant song first as Israel, then as Christ. Does the Christ reading get richer or poorer after you have sat with the Israel reading?
  3. Name the bruised reed in your life right now. What does your usual response look like, fanning the flame or pointing out how little is left? What would "protecting instead of testing" look like in one specific action this week?
  4. The Sifre says God's name in the world rides on a flawed people's testimony, and the witnesses in 43:8 are blind and deaf. Is that a burden, a comfort, or both? What evidence from your life would you actually bring to the stand for 43:11?
  5. "For mine own sake" (43:25) forgives before any repentance is described. Does that make forgiveness feel more certain to you, or less connected to anything you do? How does it sit beside "Return unto me" (44:22)?

25–30 minSession 3: Half for the Fire, Half for a God

Reading assignment: Isaiah 44, 45

Water on the thirsty, a name on the hand

Chapter 44 opens gently. "I will pour water upon him that is thirsty, and floods upon the dry ground: I will pour my spirit upon thy seed" (44:3), and the offspring "spring up... as willows by the water courses" (44:4). Then an unusual picture of belonging: "One shall say, I am the LORD's; and another shall call himself by the name of Jacob; and another shall subscribe with his hand unto the LORD" (44:5). Modern translations render that last clause as writing on the hand, "The LORD's." The doctrinal connector sets it against a verse you will reach in Session 5, "I have graven thee upon the palms of my hands" (49:16). God's people write his name on their hands, and God writes his people on his. That two-way marking is the logic of covenant, and of Latter-day Saint ordinances. At baptism disciples take upon them the name of Christ (Mosiah 5:8), and 43:1, "I have called thee by thy name; thou art mine," is the Lord's half of the exchange.

Then comes the claim that runs through both chapters: "I am the first, and I am the last; and beside me there is no God" (44:6). The rabbi quotes a midrash (Exodus Rabbah 29:5) that glosses the verse in three strokes: I am first, I have no father; I am last, I have no brother; beside me there is no God, I have no son. You do not need to be told who that was aimed at. He does not raise it to wound. I mention it so you know that, in a Jewish ear, chapters 44–45 are the charter of an undivided God.

The woodpile

Then the satire. A man plants a tree and "the rain doth nourish it" (44:14). He burns part of it to cook, "roasteth roast, and is satisfied: yea, he warmeth himself, and saith, Aha, I am warm, I have seen the fire" (44:16). "And the residue thereof he maketh a god... and prayeth unto it, and saith, Deliver me; for thou art my god" (44:17). Isaiah 44:9–20 is the most cutting satire in the Hebrew Bible.

Was it fair? The academic's answer is precise: accurate in detail, and deliberately unfair in what it made of the details. Mesopotamian temples knew perfectly well that craftsmen made statues. That is why they had the mīs pî ("washing of the mouth") ritual, in which a new image was ritually "born," and the craftsmen's hands were symbolically cut off with a tamarisk-wood knife while they declared that they had not made it. Mesopotamian priests knew exactly what Isaiah 44:19 mocks, that a person had made the image, and they had built a ritual to deal with that problem. Isaiah is arguing past the practice rather than misunderstanding it. The ritual erases the human maker; the prophet puts him back at the center, sweating at the forge ("he is hungry, and his strength faileth," 44:12) and warming himself at the leftover wood.

Why would a prophet argue that way? Nathaniel Levtow reads such parodies as political speech. A conquered people with no cult image of its own could make no louder claim than to call the victor's gods lumber. The honest questioner accepts the explanation and declines its conclusion: that makes the satire understandable, not fair. And he turns it toward the reader. Any religion whose rituals outsiders could caricature should feel that point: the Babylonian would have said what a Christian says about a crucifix, that the object is not the god.

A lie in the right hand

The satire ends with a diagnosis rather than a punch line: "He feedeth on ashes: a deceived heart hath turned him aside, that he cannot deliver his soul, nor say, Is there not a lie in my right hand?" (44:20). The verb translated "feedeth" is the ordinary Hebrew word for shepherding or grazing; the man is pasturing himself on ashes. What is striking is that he knows the facts. His problem is not lack of information; he split the wood himself.

The practical disciple moves the woodpile into a modern house: a career built so carefully that it is now expected to supply identity and security, a savings target treated as the thing that will finally make us safe, a public image maintained so long that we believe it. None of these is wicked to build. The problem is the moment the made thing is asked to "deliver." The self-examination question the text itself supplies is almost word for word: What am I holding in my right hand, in the place of trust and strength, that I would recognize as a lie if I let myself ask?

The next verses answer the idol-maker's "Deliver me" with the real deliverer: "I have blotted out, as a thick cloud, thy transgressions... return unto me; for I have redeemed thee" (44:22). And the section closes with a burst of song: "Sing, O ye heavens; for the LORD hath done it" (44:23).

Cyrus by name

Then the name. The LORD "saith of Cyrus, He is my shepherd, and shall perform all my pleasure: even saying to Jerusalem, Thou shalt be built; and to the temple, Thy foundation shall be laid" (44:28). And: "Thus saith the LORD to his anointed, to Cyrus, whose right hand I have holden" (45:1). "Anointed" is mashiach, the word for Israel's anointed kings and priests. Here, and only here in the Hebrew Bible, it is given to a foreign king who, as the text itself says twice, does not know Israel's God: "I have surnamed thee, though thou hast not known me" (45:4; also 45:5). The rabbi adds a translator's note. Christian translations capitalize and lowercase this word as theology requires. The Hebrew does neither.

Outside the Bible, the closest parallel survives in clay. The Cyrus Cylinder, written after Cyrus took Babylon in 539 BC, says that Marduk searched for a righteous king, "took a righteous king, his favorite, by the hand, he called out his name: Cyrus." Compare "whose right hand I have holden" (45:1) and "I, the LORD, which call thee by thy name" (45:3). Two peoples described the same conqueror in almost the same words, each crediting him to its own god. The LDS scholar notes how the Hebrew answers the Babylonian claim: Marduk did not choose Cyrus; the LORD did, "though thou hast not known me" (45:4–5). Whatever one decides about authorship, the chapters fit the political language of the 540s closely.

The honest questioner states the critical case for dating in its strongest form, and it is not what many Latter-day Saints assume. The strongest version of the critical case is not simply "prophets can't predict." It is a claim about audience. These chapters do not predict an exile; they speak to people already in it, and Isaiah son of Amoz never appears in them. Believers answer that a prophet can see beyond his own time; Session 4 lays out the full range of responses.

The rabbis were uneasy about a gentile mashiach for their own reasons. The Talmud (Megillah 12a) rereads 45:1 as God complaining to the Messiah about Cyrus: I said he would build my house and gather my exiles (45:13), and all he did was issue a permit, "Whoever is among you... let him go up" (Ezra 1:3). Liberation by decree, without rebuilding, is not redemption.

"I make peace, and create evil"

In the middle of the Cyrus oracle sits the week's hardest sentence: "I form the light, and create darkness: I make peace, and create evil: I the LORD do all these things" (45:7). The Hebrew raʻ ranges from moral wickedness to disaster; set against shalom, it most naturally means calamity, and many modern translations say "woe." That helps, and the honest questioner will not let it help too much. A God who creates catastrophe rather than sin is still a God who creates catastrophe, and 45:7 claims all of it, darkness included. Readers who are bothered by it are reading it correctly.

Why say it so starkly, and why here? The verse follows the commissioning of a Persian king. Shaul Shaked notes that it sounds like a Zoroastrian hymn crediting Ahura Mazda with light and darkness, but that calling God the creator of evil goes further than a Zoroastrian could accept. The verse may take in Persian ideas and push back against them in the same line. The academic sums it up: either way, the claim is single-source: no second power answers for darkness. The text does not soften it.

Jewish prayer did soften it, and says so. Every morning before the Shema, Jews bless God "who forms light and creates darkness, who makes peace and creates all things." The Talmud (Berakhot 11b) admits that "evil" is written and "all things" is said as a euphemism, and then asks why "darkness" was not softened too. Rava's final answer is that darkness is kept on purpose, so the day's prayer mentions night and the night's prayer mentions day. So the liturgy both keeps and sands down the prophet's hardest line. Radak reports that Saadia Gaon read the verse against anyone who believes in two gods, one good and one evil. There is one God, and He does not get to hand the dark half of the world to someone else.

"There is none else"

These two chapters contain the Bible's most insistent monotheism. "I am the first, and I am the last; and beside me there is no God" (44:6). "Is there a God beside me? yea, there is no God; I know not any" (44:8). "I am the LORD, and there is none else, there is no God beside me" (45:5). Mark S. Smith traces how older Israelite religion, with its divine council, reached this kind of statement in exactly this corpus. The setting explains the timing: a deported people whose god had apparently lost to Marduk could concede, or argue that no other god had ever been in the contest. For a tradition that affirms divine plurality, the academic's finding is blunt: a theology can decide how to relate to that development, but not that it didn't happen.

Restoration scripture pulls against these lines. Abraham 3:19 has the Lord "more intelligent than they all," and D&C 121:32 speaks of "the Council of the Eternal God of all other gods." Latter-day Saints respond in two ways, and the doctrinal connector names both. First, they identify the speaker. If Jehovah is the premortal Christ, then "Look unto me, and be ye saved, all the ends of the earth" (45:22) is his voice, which is how the risen Christ echoes it to the Nephites (3 Nephi 15:9). Second, they read "no God beside me" as a statement about whom to worship and trust, set against idols that cannot speak or save. They do not read it as a metaphysical census of the cosmos.

Neither voice lets that answer carry everything. The doctrinal connector: that fits the courtroom poems, where the rival "gods" are found to be "nothing" (41:24), but Isaiah's own sense is almost certainly stronger. The LDS scholar: still, "Is there a God beside me? yea, there is no God; I know not any" (44:8) is as absolute as the Hebrew Bible gets. Latter-day Saint readers should face that sentence directly. The doctrinal connector points to what may be the most honest Restoration text on the subject, D&C 121:28, which promises a time when "nothing shall be withheld, whether there be one God or many gods." It does not claim Isaiah as an ally; it defers the question. Session 4 adds one more piece of evidence from chapter 47.

Every knee

Chapter 45 ends in a world-wide call. God mocks those who "pray unto a god that cannot save" (45:20), and swears "That unto me every knee shall bow, every tongue shall swear" (45:23). Christians know 45:23 from Philippians 2:10–11, where Paul applies it to Jesus, and Restoration scripture echoes it (Mosiah 27:31; D&C 88:104).

Jews say the same verse daily. At the end of every Jewish prayer service we say Aleinu, and it is built out of this block. Its line about the nations who "pray to a god that cannot save" is 45:20 word for word; its refrain "He is our God, there is none else" is 45:5–6; its hope that "every knee must bend, every tongue swear" is 45:23. The prayer begins with a bow to the One who says there is no God beside him. We say them aloud, bowing, every day.

Think deeply.

  1. The idol parody is "accurate in detail, and deliberately unfair." How would a Babylonian priest answer Isaiah 44? What practice of your own could an outsider caricature the same way, and what would you say?
  2. What are you holding in your right hand, something you made yourself and now expect to deliver you? What would it take to look at it and say, "I made this"?
  3. The Cyrus Cylinder credits Marduk; Isaiah credits the LORD, "though thou hast not known me." Can God use someone who does not know him and would credit someone else? Where have you seen that, and how did you decide whom to credit?
  4. The honest questioner says readers bothered by 45:7 "are reading it correctly." The liturgy keeps "darkness" and softens "evil." Which move is more honest to your experience of suffering: a God who creates calamity, or one who only permits it? What do you lose with each?
  5. "Is there a God beside me? yea, there is no God; I know not any" (44:8). Of the two Latter-day Saint readings and D&C 121:28's deferral, which leaves you most at peace with the text, and which asks you to face the sentence directly?

25–30 minSession 4: Carried, Fallen, Refined

Reading assignment: Isaiah 46, 47, 48

Gods on the backs of animals

"Bel boweth down, Nebo stoopeth, their idols were upon the beasts, and upon the cattle: your carriages were heavy loaden; they are a burden to the weary beast" (46:1). Bel is Marduk, the god of Babylon; Nebo is Nabu, whose statue was carried in procession from Borsippa for the New Year (Akitu) festival. The Nabonidus Chronicle records that in the empire's last year, as the Persians closed in, the gods of other cities were brought into the capital. Statues were being hauled into the capital as the Persians closed in. The two gods at the center of Babylon's great festival have become freight on struggling animals.

The honest questioner, who judged the woodpile satire unfair, judges this passage differently. Isaiah 46:1–2 is sharper and more accurate. There is an irony in the history, too. The Cyrus Cylinder says Cyrus won Marduk's favor partly by returning cult images to their shrines. The same Cyrus of 45:1.

Then the verb turns: "Hearken unto me, O house of Jacob... which are borne by me from the belly, which are carried from the womb: And even to your old age I am he; and even to hoar hairs will I carry you: I have made, and I will bear; even I will carry, and will deliver you" (46:3–4). Babylon carries its gods; Israel's God carries Israel. The idol comes back at 46:7: "They bear him upon the shoulder, they carry him, and set him in his place... yet can he not answer."

The practical disciple asks the question the chapter is built on. The whole chapter turns on one question: are you carrying your religion, or is it carrying you? A faith made entirely of our own effort, our own performance of spiritual habits, ends up as cargo. "How Firm a Foundation" sets 46:4 in the image of old age, gray hair and still carried "like lambs," which matters to anyone caring for an aging parent or feeling their own strength decline. The promise is not visibility but continuity: "I am he," from the womb to the gray hair. And the test is concrete: if my spiritual routines stopped for a week because of illness or crisis, would I feel held, or would I feel I had dropped something?

The lady of kingdoms in the dust

Chapter 47 is addressed not to Israel but to Babylon, and the lesson points to it as evidence that "God's power is greater than worldly power." "Come down, and sit in the dust, O virgin daughter of Babylon... there is no throne" (47:1). She said "I shall be a lady for ever" (47:7), and "I am, and none else beside me; I shall not sit as a widow, neither shall I know the loss of children" (47:8). Both will come "in a moment in one day" (47:9). Her experts are summoned and dismissed: "Let now the astrologers, the stargazers, the monthly prognosticators, stand up, and save thee" (47:13). Chapter 47's sneer at Babylon's "astrologers, the stargazers, the monthly prognosticators" (47:13) likewise targets something real, a state-sponsored science of celestial omens.

Two details here reach back into Session 3. First, the word "evil" returns: "Therefore shall evil come upon thee" (47:11). The same root turns up two chapters later: "therefore shall evil come upon thee" (47:11), where it plainly means disaster falling on Babylon. That supports the "calamity" reading of 45:7, with all the discomfort already noted. Second, Babylon's boast, "I am, and none else beside me" (47:8, 10), is the LORD's own formula. That is the LORD's own formula in her mouth, and Babylon was not claiming to be the only city in existence, only to have no equal. If the formula can mean "without rival" when Babylon says it, it can mean that when God says it. It is a small piece of textual evidence for the incomparability reading. It does not erase 44:8.

The chapter should not be read without noticing how its judgment is pictured. "Uncover thy locks, make bare the leg, uncover the thigh... Thy nakedness shall be uncovered, yea, thy shame shall be seen" (47:2–3). The honest questioner puts it plainly, and ties it to the end of Session 5's reading: readers may still notice that the judgment is pictured as the sexual shaming of a woman's body, and that the "comfort ye" chapters close with enemies made to eat their own flesh.

The chapter Nephi chose

Chapter 48 is the first of the two chapters Nephi copied onto his plates, and it is not a gentle one. Chapter 48 is one of the harshest in the block. The house of Jacob swears by the LORD "but not in truth, nor in righteousness" (48:1). God says "thou art obstinate, and thy neck is an iron sinew, and thy brow brass" (48:4), and "I knew that thou wouldest deal very treacherously" (48:8). He told them things in advance "lest thou shouldest say, Mine idol hath done them" (48:5), and held other things back until now, "lest thou shouldest say, Behold, I knew them" (48:7).

The honest questioner, having spent his essay on problems, ends on that verse. The chapters that insist on God's foreknowledge also say he deliberately tells people things they did not know, so that no one can claim to have had it figured out. That applies to defender and critic alike.

The furnace, honestly read

Then the lesson's verse: "Behold, I have refined thee, but not with silver; I have chosen thee in the furnace of affliction" (48:10). The practical disciple starts with a warning. This verse is easily misused: quoted at sufferers as though it explained their suffering, which it does not claim to do. Read in place, it comes right after "For my name's sake will I defer mine anger... that I cut thee not off" (48:9). The furnace is where God did not give up on a people who gave him every reason to. The word "chosen" is set inside the fire, not after it.

Eight verses later comes a sentence that the lesson gives to children: "O that thou hadst hearkened to my commandments! then had thy peace been as a river, and thy righteousness as the waves of the sea" (48:18). The verse is a regret, not a threat, and it implies that some of our furnaces are not refinement at all but the plain consequences of not listening. That leaves two questions to hold apart: which of my difficulties are simply afflictions, in which I can look for God's company, and which are the results of not hearkening, where the answer is to change rather than endure? Confusing the two means either blaming ourselves for suffering we did not cause or calling "refinement" a pattern we could stop. And 48:10 is a verse to read for yourself before quoting it to anyone else.

The chapter closes with the exodus order: "Go ye forth of Babylon, flee ye from the Chaldeans, with a voice of singing" (48:20). That was literal instruction for leaving a literal city. D&C 133 repeats it three times, and the third time says what it means by it. The third time it states its own re-reading: "Go ye out from among the nations, even from Babylon, from the midst of wickedness, which is spiritual Babylon" (133:14).

Wrestle: the brass-plates problem, stated plainly

Now the week's hardest question for Latter-day Saints, with all the pieces on the table. It is best taken with 1 Nephi 20 open beside Isaiah 48.

What Nephi's text contains. The Book of Mormon does not quote Isaiah 48–49 at arm's length. Nephi presents them as scripture read from the brass plates so his brothers could liken it to themselves (1 Nephi 19:23–24). The quotation includes the Cyrus-and-Babylon lines. 1 Nephi 20:14 keeps "he will do his pleasure on Babylon," and 20:20 keeps "Go ye forth of Babylon." The academic states the conflict without hedging: in the critical reconstruction those lines belong to the 540s, and a historian working by the usual methods cannot put them on plates in 600 BCE. Latter-day Saint scholars have offered answers that fall outside this essay's scope; the conflict itself should be stated plainly.

What Nephi's text changes. Some differences run against the grain of the KJV. Where Isaiah 48:2 says the people "stay themselves upon the God of Israel," 1 Nephi 20:2 says they do not; 1 Nephi 20:22 adds "notwithstanding he hath done all this, and greater also" before "There is no peace"; 1 Nephi 20:10 drops "but not with silver." Other differences carry no such late-addition flag, and they sharpen the accusation.

One famous difference does carry a flag. 1 Nephi 20:1 reads "out of the waters of Judah, or out of the waters of baptism." Royal Skousen, editor of the Book of Mormon Critical Text Project, explains that the phrase was not in the 1830 edition; Joseph Smith added it in 1840 as a parenthetical comment, and after the 1920 edition dropped the parentheses some writers treated it as lost Isaiah. The doctrinal connector values it as commentary (membership in Jacob's house can come by covenant) and is clear about what it is. But the phrase is Joseph's gloss, not Isaiah's text.

The italics. The KJV prints in italics words its translators supplied. Skousen's figures, as summarized by FAIR, are that about 23 percent of the differences in the Book of Mormon's biblical quotations involve italicized words. David P. Wright argues that the variants respond to the 1611 English, not any Hebrew source. Critics see a text worked out from an English Bible. Stan Spencer grants the pattern and explains it with a "Missing Words" model, a revealed text without the italicized words in which Joseph Smith sometimes supplied what was missing. Skousen reads the same numbers as evidence that Joseph did not know what the italics meant. Others read them the opposite way. Nephi's own framing, "I did liken all scriptures unto us," explains some of the changes, such as the new address that opens 1 Nephi 21. Whether likening explains the rest depends on which model of translation a reader already holds.

The range of Latter-day Saint responses. Joshua M. Sears's 2022 chapter is the most useful map. He sorts Latter-day Saint responses into four approaches. Here they are, with what each costs.

  • Book of Mormon authority. Talmage, McConkie, Millet and others take the Book of Mormon's attribution as settling the question, largely without engaging the critical arguments.
  • Defending unity. Sperry, Ludlow, Gileadi, Jackson and others argue that the critical division rests on ruling out prediction. Kent P. Jackson puts the question as "Can a prophet see beyond his own time?" and also sets a limit: the Book of Mormon, he writes, "cannot be used as proof that all of 'Second' Isaiah comes from Isaiah son of Amoz"; it vouches only for the chapters it quotes. Sears judges frankly that this work has mostly persuaded the already persuaded. The cost the LDS scholar names: unity readings must explain why the chapters address exiles and treat Cyrus's rise as already under way, not only how a prophet could know a name.
  • Historical nuance. Welch's 1998 FARMS chapter argues that it requires only that the chapters it quotes were "authoritatively attributed to Isaiah prior to 600 B.C.," which leaves room for a disciple to edit Isaiah's material. Welch also observes a striking silence: the Book of Mormon never quotes the Cyrus material of Isaiah 45 or the "Third Isaiah" chapters 56–66, and Nibley had already observed that its Deutero-Isaiah quotations come only from chapters 48–54 and part of 55. William Hamblin suggested a prophet active after the first deportation of 597 BC. Hamblin's timeline is very tight, and mainstream dating puts these chapters closer to 540 than to 597.
  • Divine intervention. Blake Ostler's "expansion" model and Brant Gardner's "Assyrian edition" allow the English translation to contain material later than the plates; Grant Hardy says we know less about inspired translation than about ancient Israel. The expansion and intervention models keep the critical dating, but they sit uneasily with Nephi's statement that he read these words from the plates (1 Nephi 22:1).

Sears ends by treating the question as finally theological, one that need not unsettle faith. The LDS scholar's judgment on that ending is exact: that may be the honest place to stand, but it is a statement of faith, not a solution, and neither Sears nor Welch claims otherwise. The doctrinal connector says the same from the other side. The Book of Mormon brings no outside evidence to the dating debate; its claim stands or falls with the book itself, and readers should know that most biblical scholars reject it.

A Jewish footnote seven centuries old. The rabbi adds a perspective neither side usually brings. Abraham ibn Ezra, in twelfth-century Spain, commented on 40:1 that these consolations concern "our exile," then noted that the sages said Samuel wrote the book of Samuel, true up to "And Samuel died," and closed with ve-ha-maskil yavin, "and the one who understands will understand." His translator explains the hint: the second part of Isaiah was, in Ibn Ezra's view, not written by the same prophet. Ibn Ezra never says it outright. He leaves it for the careful reader. For the rabbi, the point is what it shows about holiness. Our canon is holy because the community received it and reads it, not because we can certify each author. He leaves the brass plates to Latter-day Saints to work out on their own terms, and says only this: the question is old, it is not a modern attack, and one of the most honored names in rabbinic commentary felt the pressure of it and answered with a wink rather than an anathema.

Think deeply.

  1. "Are you carrying your religion, or is it carrying you?" Run the practical disciple's test honestly: if your spiritual routines stopped for a week, would you feel held or would you feel you had dropped something? What does your answer say about what those routines are for?
  2. The lesson reads 48:10 as refinement; the practical disciple warns it is "easily misused" and that some furnaces are "the plain consequences of not listening." Take one hardship in your life now. Which kind is it? How would you know?
  3. Babylon says "I am, and none else beside me," the same words God uses. Does that change how you read the monotheism of chapters 44–45? What would Babylon's "I am" look like in a modern person, institution, or in yourself?
  4. Lay out the four Latter-day Saint approaches to the brass-plates problem and name what each costs. Which cost are you most willing to pay? Is Sears's "finally theological" a resting place for you, or a place to keep working?
  5. Ibn Ezra felt the pressure of the authorship question and "answered with a wink rather than an anathema." The rabbi says a canon is holy because a community receives and reads it. Could a Latter-day Saint say something like that, given Nephi's claim about the plates? What would have to be true for it to work, and what would it leave out?

25–30 minSession 5: Graven upon the Palms

Reading assignment: Isaiah 49

The second song: Israel sent to Israel

"Listen, O isles, unto me... The LORD hath called me from the womb" (49:1). The servant speaks for himself this time: his mouth "like a sharp sword," hidden "in his quiver" like "a polished shaft" (49:2). Then God names him: "Thou art my servant, O Israel, in whom I will be glorified" (49:3). Two verses later the same servant is sent "to bring Jacob again to him" (49:5). How can Israel restore Israel? A few scholars delete "Israel" in 49:3 as a gloss, since one medieval Hebrew manuscript (Kennicott 96) lacks it; most keep it and read the servant as a faithful core, or the prophet, standing for the nation and sent to it. Jewish commentators had their own answers. At 49:3, Radak reads "Israel" as a title of honor: you are the Israel in whom I glory, as a master glories in a faithful servant. Ibn Ezra thought the speaker was the prophet himself.

Nephi's version opens differently. Before "Listen, O isles," 1 Nephi 21:1 adds a whole address: "Hearken, O ye house of Israel, all ye that are broken off and are driven out because of the wickedness of the pastors of my people." It turns the servant song toward scattered branches like Lehi's. The doctrinal connector tracks the other changes (verse 8 has "O isles of the sea," verse 15 ends "O house of Israel") and names the pattern. Each change aims the poem at scattered Israel on an island. Jacob later says of his people, "we are upon an isle of the sea" (2 Nephi 10:20). Isaiah 49 in the Book of Mormon has been addressed to Lehi's family.

"I have laboured in vain"

Then the servant says something scripture rarely lets a chosen figure say: "I have laboured in vain, I have spent my strength for nought, and in vain" (49:4). "For nought" is tohu, the "without form" of Genesis 1:2; "in vain" is hevel, vapor. The servant feels that his work has gone back to chaos and evaporated. The lesson sends readers here "when your efforts and service seem to be 'for nought, and in vain.'" What matters practically is that scripture puts the words of discouragement into the servant's own mouth and does not correct them.

The same verse turns: "yet surely my judgment is with the LORD, and my work with my God." He moves the ledger from visible outcomes to God. And God's reply is not reassurance about results but a larger job: "It is a light thing that thou shouldest be my servant to raise up the tribes of Jacob... I will also give thee for a light to the Gentiles" (49:6). For the parent of a child who has left the Church, the teacher whose class isn't listening, the minister whose visits are declined, the practical disciple has one question. Ask yourself: Whose evaluation of this work am I actually living by?

"A light to the Gentiles" is where the servant keeps moving. In Acts 13:47, Paul and Barnabas apply "a light to the Gentiles" (49:6) to their own mission.

Nursing fathers: Nephi reads Isaiah 49

The chapter moves from the servant to the city and her children. "I will lift up mine hand to the Gentiles, and set up my standard to the people: and they shall bring thy sons in their arms... And kings shall be thy nursing fathers, and their queens thy nursing mothers" (49:22–23). The lesson's Scripture Helps ask how the Gentiles will nurse Israel, and the lesson sends readers to 1 Nephi 22 to see how Nephi likened these chapters.

Nephi's brothers ask whether these things are "spiritual" or will happen "according to the flesh" (1 Nephi 22:1). He answers both, and reads the nursing Gentiles as "a mighty nation among the Gentiles, yea, even upon the face of this land" (22:7). In its own setting, Isaiah 49:22–23 most naturally pictures foreign rulers sponsoring the return of Judah's exiles. Cyrus is the obvious model. Nephi moves the prophecy across an ocean and far into the future. The LDS scholar puts the move in its company: this is not historical-critical exegesis. It follows the way the New Testament uses the same chapters. Nephi grounds it in the Abrahamic covenant, "In thy seed shall all the kindreds of the earth be blessed" (22:9), and Jacob, reading the same chapter to his people, adds a phrase Isaiah lacks: "the Mighty God shall deliver his covenant people" (2 Nephi 6:17). Covenant is the Book of Mormon's key to all ten chapters.

"But Zion said"

Verse 13 is a hymn: "Sing, O heavens; and be joyful, O earth... for the LORD hath comforted his people." Verse 14 answers it: "But Zion said, The LORD hath forsaken me, and my Lord hath forgotten me."

The rabbi reads this verse from the synagogue calendar. For the seven Sabbaths between the ninth of Av and Rosh Hashanah, the prophetic readings are the "seven of consolation," all from Isaiah 40–63. Two of the seven come from our block. The first is Isaiah 40, God telling the prophets "Comfort ye my people." The second begins at 49:14, and a medieval liturgist passed on a reading of the order as a conversation: God sends comforters, and Zion answers that she has been forsaken. She will not be comforted by prophets. The third reading calls her "not comforted" (54:11). Only in the fourth does God say, "I, even I, am he that comforteth you" (51:12), and comfort her himself. We read the book out of order so it becomes an argument between God and a grieving people, and the people are allowed to win the first round. Isaiah 49:14 is not a lapse of faith to be corrected.

The Talmud (Berakhot 32b) reads the next verse as a bargain about memory. "Yea, they may forget" recalls the golden calf ("These be thy gods," Exodus 32:4, with the same Hebrew word); "yet will I not forget thee" recalls Sinai ("I am the LORD thy God," with the same word for "I"). God forgets the sin and remembers the covenant. And the rabbi reminds Christian readers who "thee" is in 49:16. The one engraved is Zion, a city with walls. It is not first a metaphor for an individual soul. God has a city on His hands, and He cannot put her down.

Capstone: six ways to read 49:14–16

The six-outlooks essay takes these three verses as its test case: "But Zion said, The LORD hath forsaken me, and my Lord hath forgotten me. Can a woman forget her sucking child, that she should not have compassion on the son of her womb? yea, they may forget, yet will I not forget thee. Behold, I have graven thee upon the palms of my hands; thy walls are continually before me." The passage has a clean structure: a complaint (v. 14), a question (v. 15), and an answer with a picture attached (v. 16). It is also where the week's hard questions meet: a ruined city, a Nephite copy with changes, and a Latter-day Saint reading almost everyone reaches for first. A method that separates the ways of reading is a real help with a verse that carries this much.

The method comes from Donald W. Parry, professor of Hebrew Bible at BYU, in his chapter "Ways to Read Isaiah: Six Outlooks": the historical near view, the distant view, dual fulfillment, likening, personal reading, and reading for doctrine. He advises beginning with the near view. Parry does not discuss Isaiah 49 in that chapter. The readings below apply his framework and are my own.

1. The near view. Zion is Jerusalem pictured as a woman, and she talks back. The chapter has been triumphant, and verse 13 calls creation to sing. Verse 14 starts with "But." Zion does not join the song. Her complaint uses two verbs, "forsaken" and "forgotten," and those two roots appear as a pair at the end of Lamentations: "Wherefore dost thou forget us for ever, and forsake us so long time?" (Lamentations 5:20). Patricia Tull Willey and Rachel Adelman read Isaiah 40–55 as a deliberate answer to Lamentations. On their reading, Zion in 49:14 is quoting her own lament, and 49:15–16 is the reply she did not get in Lamentations.

The near view also puts the authorship question in its sharpest form. Zion is "desolate, a captive, and removing to and fro" (49:21); her walls exist only as something God keeps in view. Nephi quotes the chapter from plates that left Jerusalem around 600 BC. That is before the city fell, not only before Cyrus. The Latter-day Saint reading holds that Isaiah wrote to a ruin he had not yet seen; the majority reading holds that a later prophet wrote in its shadow. Both readings agree on the setting the text imagines, and the near view starts there.

Then the picture. "Graven" is from a root for cutting or inscribing: the mark is permanent, not ink. Nineteenth-century commentators offered two explanations: Robert Lowth thought of a practice of marking the hands with a sign of the city or temple, and Campegius Vitringa thought of an architect who keeps a building's plan in front of him. The second half of the verse, "thy walls are continually before me," fits Vitringa's reading. And there is an edge to the image. Leviticus 19:28 forbids Israelites to "print any marks upon you." God does to himself what Israelites were forbidden to do, and the name he marks there is Zion. Recall 44:5 from Session 3, where the people write "The LORD's" on their hands.

2. The distant view. The verses after 49:16 outrun any return from Babylon: children who "make haste," a land "too narrow by reason of the inhabitants" (49:19), kings as nursing fathers. The return under Cyrus was small and hard, as Ezra and Nehemiah describe it. Readers who want a fulfillment the size of the language have looked forward. Nephi is the earliest reader we can name who does this. His copy already leans that way; his 49:15 reads "yet will I not forget thee, O house of Israel" (1 Nephi 21:15). The addressee changes from a single city to a scattered people.

3. Dual fulfillment. Parry allows a prophecy more than one fulfillment. Isaiah 49:14–16 has at least three layers, and they should be kept separate rather than merged. The first is the city, whose walls rose again under Nehemiah (Nehemiah 6:15). The second is the people: Nephi's 21:14 adds "but he will show that he hath not." The words "he will show" point to a demonstration that is still to come. The third is the layer most Latter-day Saints reach for first, the nail wounds of the risen Christ. Elder Jeffrey R. Holland described the engraving as written in scar tissue with Roman nails, and the risen Lord invites the Nephites to "feel the prints of the nails in my hands" (3 Nephi 11:14). Both essays are clear about the layer. The honest point is that Isaiah 49:16 itself says nothing about wounds or nails. That is a Christian re-reading, and a moving one, but Isaiah was not describing crucifixion wounds. And the six-outlooks writer argues that saying so helps. Keeping the layers apart makes the third one stronger, not weaker. In 49:16 a permanent cut in the hand is God's devotion to a ruined city; the Christian reading finds the same kind of mark, made for someone else, in the body of Christ.

4. Likening. Nephi "did liken all scriptures unto us" (1 Nephi 19:23), and he chose Isaiah 48–49 to do it. The Dead Sea Scrolls' pesher commentaries also applied prophecy to their own community, but they made a different claim. Pesher says the application is the hidden meaning of the text. Likening applies the text openly and does not claim that the prophet had the reader in view. Likened, Zion's complaint belongs to anyone who hears everyone else singing and cannot join in. And the structure of God's answer matters. He does not rebuke her. He asks a question, makes an admission ("yea, they may forget"), and offers an image. The promise is given on the far side of that honesty, not in place of it.

5. The personal reading. The lesson gives 49:14–16 to children as a promise that the Lord will never forget them, and many readers take "thy walls are continually before me" as a word about a home, a marriage, or a life that has come down. Nothing in the verse limits it to those uses, and nothing in it intends them. The six-outlooks writer allows the reading with one condition. It needs to keep "thee" singular, as the Hebrew and the KJV both have it. Set that beside the rabbi's reminder that the "thee" is first a city. Both are true: the singular is a city, and a person may still hear it as herself. And he turns the reading outward. Holding someone "continually before me" is, for a human being, a matter of simple, concrete acts: a name written on a list you actually look at, a calendar reminder, a message sent on the anniversary of a loss.

6. Reading for doctrine. The doctrine sits in one Hebrew root. "Compassion" in 49:15 is meraḥem; "womb" is reḥem; both come from rḥm, which also gives raḥamim, mercy. Hebrew ties mercy to the body of a mother. The verse argues from lesser to greater: if the love most rooted in the body can fail, the love that exceeds it cannot. And "they may forget" is not hypothetical. Lamentations remembers what mothers did in the siege: "The hands of the pitiful women have sodden their own children" (Lamentations 4:10), and "pitiful" is from the same root. Isaiah is not using a pleasant greeting-card comparison. He is writing to people who had seen compassionate mothers fail. It teaches that God's memory of his people outlasts even the worst failure of human love.

Three doctrinal claims follow from the text. God is described in maternal terms, as when he carries Israel "from the womb" and "even to hoar hairs" (46:3–4). God's remembering is a covenant act; the servant is given "for a covenant of the people" (49:8), and the engraving works like a signature. Third, divine memory is shown as costly. A mark cut into the hand is permanent and painful, which is why the Latter-day Saint layer fits so well: the belief that the risen Christ still carries his wounds develops a claim Isaiah had already made, that God's remembering is marked on his own body. The essay ends where the passage does. Zion said she was forgotten, and the answer did not deny that forgetting happens or that her walls had fallen. It said that her name and her walls were cut into the hands of the one she was accusing.

The end of the block

The chapter does not end on the palms. It ends with captives taken back from the mighty (49:25), and then: "I will feed them that oppress thee with their own flesh; and they shall be drunken with their own blood, as with sweet wine" (49:26). The honest questioner asked you in Session 4 to notice that the "comfort ye" chapters close with enemies made to eat their own flesh. The comfort of Isaiah 40–49 is real, and it comes out of a war.

The panel ends in different places, and they belong together. These chapters do not ask for heroics. The practical disciple's list is small: wait, leave the bruised reed unbroken, look at your right hand, let yourself be carried, move the ledger, remember someone who feels forgotten. Pick one, he says, and do it by Sunday. The doctrinal connector ends with Nephi's own distinction between what Isaiah said and how Nephi applied it. Nephi was open about doing this; readers who keep his distinction between text and likening get both books whole. The LDS scholar ends on the people who first heard 49:15–16, in Hezekiah's Jerusalem or by the canals of Babylon, and on Nephi's family in the same position across an ocean. Latter-day Saint scholarship does not need to solve the authorship question to see that, but it should stop pretending the question is already solved. And the rabbi ends where Shabbat Nachamu begins. The comfort is not only a private assurance. It is a road home for a community that had every reason to believe it was forgotten, and a God who, in the midrash's words, will not let a human being out-comfort Him.

Think deeply.

  1. The servant says "I have laboured in vain," and scripture does not correct him; God answers by giving him a larger task. Where do you feel your work has gone to tohu and hevel? What would it mean in practice to "move the ledger," and why might a bigger assignment be a better answer than reassurance?
  2. The synagogue lets Zion "win the first round": the prophets' comfort fails, and only God's own comfort works. What does that say about the limits of what one person can do for another's grief? When has a human comforter failed you, and what finally reached you?
  3. The six-outlooks writer says keeping the layers apart makes the Christological reading of 49:16 "stronger, not weaker." Test that. Read 49:16 first as God's permanent mark for a ruined city, then as the nail prints. What does the first reading add to the second?
  4. "Yea, they may forget." The text concedes that the strongest human love can fail, and Lamentations shows it did. Does a promise mean more or less when it is given after that admission? Who in your life has been forgotten, possibly by you, and what one concrete act would hold them "continually before" you this week?
  5. The block closes with 49:26, enemies drunk on their own blood, right after the palms. How do you hold the tenderness of 49:15–16 and the violence of 49:26 in the same chapter? Is it enough to say the comfort came out of a war?
