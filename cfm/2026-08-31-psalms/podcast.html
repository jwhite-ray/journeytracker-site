Where we are

NATHAN: The Psalter is five books, and the divisions aren't modern. You can see the seams in your King James — 41:13, 72:18, 89:52, 106:48. Each is a little doxology with nothing to do with the psalm it's stapled to.

ELI: Like a page break somebody wrote a blessing on.

NATHAN: Exactly. The academic on our panel walks through Gerald Wilson's argument that those seams carry an argument. Books One through Three are David and a promised permanent dynasty — and Book Three ends at Psalm 89 asking God why he's "made void the covenant of thy servant."

ELI: The dynasty fails.

NATHAN: Publicly, in the text. Book Four changes the subject: it opens with the one psalm attributed to Moses, and its core announces that the LORD reigns. The human throne collapsed; the divine throne didn't. And our block starts at the end of that book — which reframes Psalm 102. Read alone it's one miserable man. Read in position it's an exile psalm: "thy servants take pleasure in her stones, and favour the dust thereof." Stones and dust are what's left when the walls come down.

ELI: So the editors set the most private collapse beside the most public one.

NATHAN: And then Book Five closes with five untitled hymns, 146 through 150. Every one opens with hallelu-yah and closes with hallelu-yah — "Praise ye the LORD" top and bottom. Five psalms, ten hallelujahs.

ELI: Hence the episode title, which I didn't choose and now have to defend.

NATHAN: You'll manage. And they're anonymous. After a book saturated with superscriptions, the last five poems belong to nobody — which, as the academic essay puts it, is another way of saying they belong to whoever says them.

What praise is

The oath

ELI: Psalm 110. Seven verses. Why is it even in this block? Everything around it is praise, and this thing has corpses in it.

NATHAN: The six-outlooks essay says exactly that — "a coronation oracle with corpses in it." It's here because it's the most-quoted Old Testament text in the New Testament: David Hay catalogued more than thirty references, almost all of them to verses 1 and 4.

ELI: Verse 1.

NATHAN: "The LORD said unto my Lord, Sit thou at my right hand." And the whole load is carried by typography. LORD in full capitals is YHWH. "Lord" in mixed case is 'adoni — the ordinary way a subject addresses a superior. Two different words.

THE QUESTIONER: And Hebrew has no capital letters. So that distinction is a translator's decision.

NATHAN: Defensible — the words really are different — but a decision. And the plain-sense reading is a court oracle: a temple prophet at a coronation relaying, YHWH has said to my lord the king, sit at my right hand. Our LDS scholar says that's the setting nearly all critical scholarship assigns it.

ELI: Verse 4, then. That's the one with our name on it.

NATHAN: "The LORD hath sworn, and will not repent, Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek." And it solves a real problem: Israel's priesthood ran through Levi, and the king came from Judah. Verse 4 gives him priestly standing by an end run around Levi — Melchizedek in Genesis 14 is "king of Salem" and "priest of the most high God," holding both offices. Salem is Jerusalem. The king is a priest by the city's older charter.

ELI: Okay. Lovely reconstruction. What does it cost?

NATHAN: (pause) It costs the reading most people in the pews have. If verse 1 is a court oracle addressed to a human king, then in its original setting it isn't the Father speaking to the Son. It's a prophet speaking to a man in a chair.

ELI: So if the court-oracle reading is right, the riddle dissolves.

How much priesthood is in there

176 verses

NATHAN: Psalm 119. Twenty-two stanzas, one per Hebrew letter, eight verses each, every verse in a stanza beginning with that letter. Twenty-two times eight is 176. That's why your King James prints ALEPH, BETH, GIMEL — and no English translation reproduces the constraint itself.

THE QUESTIONER: What the poem omits is more interesting.

NATHAN: That's the academic's point. No temple. No sacrifice. No priest. No exodus. No David. No Zion. The speaker is consoled entirely by a text — which is why it's dated late. It presupposes a community whose center of gravity has moved from a shrine to a scroll. Kent Reynolds argues the sheer length is itself the point: less a hymn than a curriculum.

ELI: And our rabbi adds a use the reading assignment won't show you. Because it's an alphabet, you can carve a person out of it — there's a custom of reciting the stanzas matching the letters of a dead person's name. In practice, it's what you say in a funeral home.

NATHAN: He also pushes back on the manual's framing. The lesson reads 119 as a psalm about the journey, which is genuinely textual. But notice what the lamp in verse 105 is lighting. Those eight recurring nouns are all legal terms. "Thy word is a lamp unto my feet" is not, in its own idiom, about scripture in general. It's about obligations you can name.

ELI: And note the limit in the image. A lamp at foot level shows the next step, not the destination. Most disappointment with revelation is a demand for headlights, answered with a lamp, and read as silence.

THE QUESTIONER: Can I raise the thing about 119 that runs opposite to expectation? The psalmist is not a penitent. He's a claimant. "I have more understanding than all my teachers." Latter-day Saints tend to find this psalm congenial and Paul's argument in Romans hard.

Verse nine

Two psalms in one poem

ELI: 139. The one people love. "If I make my bed in hell, behold, thou art there" — Sheol, the realm of the dead. God's reach isn't bounded by death. And the psalm doesn't say what God does there. D&C 138 does: he "organized his forces and appointed messengers."
