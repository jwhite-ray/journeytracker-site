The Oath and the Stone

Written by an AI persona connecting the block to Restoration scripture and modern teaching — not a real person.

Two sentences in this week's reading carry more Restoration freight than anything else in the Psalter: one about a priesthood (Psalm 110:4), one about a rejected building stone (Psalm 118:22). Both get quoted, argued with, and rebuilt in the Book of Mormon and the Doctrine and Covenants. In neither case does the Restoration simply repeat the psalm — it changes what the psalm is about, and that change is the part worth reading for.

The oath that renamed a priesthood

Melchizedek appears in exactly two places in the Hebrew Bible: three verses in Genesis 14, and Psalm 110:4. That is the whole of him — a king-priest with no genealogy and no recorded death — and then a psalm swearing an eternal priesthood by his name: "The LORD hath sworn, and will not repent, Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek."

Hebrews makes him stranger still: "without father, without mother, without descent, having neither beginning of days, nor end of life; but made like unto the Son of God" (Hebrews 7:3). Read as a claim about a man, it is baffling, and has baffled readers for two thousand years.

Restoration texts move the phrase off the man and onto the office. Alma 13:7: "This high priesthood being after the order of his Son, which order was from the foundation of the world; or in other words, being without beginning of days or end of years." The Joseph Smith Translation of Genesis 14 says the same of an order "which came, not by man, nor the will of man; neither by father nor mother." Having relocated the eternity, Alma hands Melchizedek a thoroughly mortal biography: a king over Salem whose people "had waxed strong in iniquity and abomination," who preached repentance to them, and who got it (Alma 13:17–18). Hebrews leaves you an enigma. Alma leaves you an ordinary man holding an extraordinary priesthood, which is a far more useful thing to be given.

D&C 107:3–4 completes the move and reframes the psalm itself: "Before his day it was called the Holy Priesthood, after the Order of the Son of God. But out of respect or reverence to the name of the Supreme Being, to avoid the too frequent repetition of his name, they, the church, in ancient days, called that priesthood after Melchizedek." If that is right, Psalm 110:4 is not naming Melchizedek as anyone's source. It is using a reverential substitute, and the sentence underneath reads: thou art a priest for ever after the order of the Son of God. A psalm about the Son says that the Son's priesthood is named for the Son.

Hebrews fastens on one further word — not the name but the swearing: "not without an oath he was made priest ... The Lord sware and will not repent" (Hebrews 7:20–21). D&C 84:39–40 keeps it: "this is according to the oath and covenant which belongeth to the priesthood ... which he cannot break, neither can it be moved." The most technical phrase in Latter-day Saint priesthood vocabulary grows straight out of one clause in this week's reading.

"The LORD said unto my Lord"

The KJV typography of Psalm 110:1 does real work: LORD in full capitals is the divine name; "Lord" in mixed case is not. So the One named YHWH tells another — whom the psalmist calls my Lord — to sit at His right hand. Jesus made this the hinge of a question the Pharisees could not answer (Matthew 22:41–45); He needed no argument, only to read it aloud.

Stephen, seeing the same throne, reports a variation: "Jesus standing on the right hand of God" (Acts 7:55–56) — the psalm's seated Lord risen to His feet for a dying man. Elder Jeffrey R. Holland named Stephen's martyrdom as one of four scriptural illustrations that "the Father, the Son, and the Holy Ghost are separate persons, three divine beings" ("The Only True God and Jesus Christ Whom He Hath Sent," October 2007 general conference). What Psalm 110:1 contributes to that argument is not a vision but a sentence, sitting in the oldest layer of Israel's worship.

The stone, the shout, and the handkerchief

Psalm 118:22 — "The stone which the builders refused is become the head stone of the corner" — is quoted in the Synoptic Gospels, in Acts, and in 1 Peter (Matthew 21:42; Acts 4:10–11; 1 Peter 2:7). In its own setting it is most plausibly about a king, or the nation itself: despised by the great powers, then vindicated. Jacob's argument depends on that reading rather than replacing it.

Jacob 4 explains why the builders refused — the only text I can find that does. His diagnosis is not wickedness but method: they "despised the words of plainness ... and sought for things that they could not understand. Wherefore, because of their blindness, which blindness came by looking beyond the mark, they must needs fall" (Jacob 4:14). Then the psalm surfaces almost verbatim — "they will reject the stone upon which they might build and have safe foundation" (4:15), and "how is it possible that these, after having rejected the sure foundation, can ever build upon it, that it may become the head of their corner?" (4:17). Jacob is naming a failure mode, a preference for complication over plainness, and it is not confined to one people. Helaman later moves the same stone indoors, from a builder's rejected block to a private foundation under storm (Helaman 5:12).

Seven verses on, the psalm becomes liturgy. "Save now, I beseech thee, O LORD" (118:25) is the Hebrew behind hosanna; the Church's own account of the shout says the word "comes from two Hebrew words that, as used in Psalms 118:25, are rendered in the King James Version as 'Save [us] now, I beseech thee.'" The next verse, "Blessed be he that cometh in the name of the LORD," is the triumphal-entry cry (Matthew 21:9).

Now set 3 Nephi 11:17 beside it. After the multitude at Bountiful had gone forth one by one and put their hands in the wounds, "they did cry out with one accord, saying: Hosanna! Blessed be the name of the Most High God!" Both halves of Psalm 118:25–26 are there, the plea and the blessing of the name — but inverted. At Jerusalem the crowd cried save now to a man riding toward his death. At Bountiful they cried it after touching the proof that He had already done it. Petition had become acknowledgment.

The Saints have been saying it ever since. The Kirtland dedicatory prayer ends "singing Hosanna to God and the Lamb!" (D&C 109:79). President Russell M. Nelson called the shout "a sacred tribute to the Father and the Son, symbolizing the reaction of the multitude when the Savior made His triumphal entry into Jerusalem" ("Hosanna Shout," April 2020 general conference). Waving a white handkerchief, Latter-day Saints are performing Psalm 118.

Written for a generation not yet created

Psalm 102 is a complaint — bones burned as a hearth, a sparrow alone on a roof — and then, mid-poem, the psalmist notices something about his own document: "This shall be written for the generation to come: and the people which shall be created shall praise the LORD" (102:18). A man too miserable to eat his bread discovers he is making evidence for people who do not yet exist.

Moroni says the same thing with a specificity the psalm does not claim: "I speak unto you as if ye were present, and yet ye are not. But behold, Jesus Christ hath shown you unto me, and I know your doing" (Mormon 8:35). The psalmist writes toward an unseen generation; Moroni says he has been shown ours.

The verses around 102:18 are gathering language, and their sequence matters: "the set time, is come" (102:13), then "When the LORD shall build up Zion, he shall appear in his glory" (102:16), then "When the people are gathered together, and the kingdoms, to serve the LORD" (102:22). Building precedes appearing — the same order as a house raised "that the Son of Man might have a place to manifest himself to his people" (D&C 109:5), and as "The Lord who shall suddenly come to his temple" (D&C 133:2). The psalm is not predicting a temple in Ohio. It is stating an order of operations the Restoration then follows.

How far east is from west

Psalm 103:12 states a fact without a mechanism: "As far as the east is from the west, so far hath he removed our transgressions from us." Two verses later the psalmist grounds God's mercy in God's knowledge — "he knoweth our frame; he remembereth that we are dust."

Alma 7:12 supplies how that knowledge was acquired: "he will take upon him their infirmities, that his bowels may be filled with mercy, according to the flesh, that he may know according to the flesh how to succor his people according to their infirmities." The psalm says God knows what we are made of; Alma says He went and found out from the inside. That is the distance between sympathy and empathy — the clearest case in this block of a Restoration text supplying a mechanism the psalm only gestures at. D&C 58:42 then pushes past distance to erasure: "he who has repented of his sins, the same is forgiven, and I, the Lord, remember them no more." President Camille N. Johnson preached this same relief in April 2023 ("Jesus Christ Is Relief").

Lamp and rod

Psalm 119:105 — "Thy word is a lamp unto my feet, and a light unto my path" — and 1 Nephi 15:24 — the rod of iron "was the word of God; and whoso would hearken unto the word of God, and would hold fast unto it, they would never perish" — are routinely quoted as though they said the same thing. They do not. A lamp is for seeing; a rod is for holding. And the hazard in Lehi's dream is a mist of darkness, precisely the condition in which a lamp fails: you can hold a light in fog and see nothing. The rod works in the dark because it does not depend on sight.

Psalm 119 knows both problems. Its famous verse is the lamp; its last verse is not. "I have gone astray like a lost sheep; seek thy servant; for I do not forget thy commandments" (119:176). The longest chapter in the Bible, 176 verses in praise of the word, ends with a man lost anyway, asking to be found. President Russell M. Nelson's counsel on 119:105 allows for exactly that: you "stop going in the wrong direction," study the map, then proceed "with repentance and restitution required to get on the 'strait and narrow path'" ("Living by Scriptural Guidance," Ensign, Nov. 2000).

Where Psalm 139 stops

This is the psalm most often over-read, so mark its edges. "Whither shall I go from thy spirit? ... If I ascend up into heaven, thou art there: if I make my bed in hell, behold, thou art there" (139:7–8). The "hell" here is Sheol, the realm of the dead: God's reach is not bounded by death. The psalmist does not say what God does there.

D&C 138 does. Joseph F. Smith saw "the Son of God appeared, declaring liberty to the captives" (138:18) — and then something the psalm gives no hint of: "the Lord went not in person among the wicked," but "organized his forces and appointed messengers" (138:29–30). Thou art there becomes an organized missionary effort.

The harder case is 139:13–16: "Thou hast covered me in my mother's womb ... Thine eyes did see my substance, yet being unperfect; and in thy book all my members were written." This gets read as premortal existence, and it is not, quite. The psalm's horizon is God's attention to a body being formed — verse 15's "curiously wrought in the lowest parts of the earth" is womb imagery. What it gives you is a God who knew you before there was a you to know.

Restoration scripture goes further, and the extra distance is worth naming rather than blurring. Abraham 3:23: "thou wast chosen before thou wast born." D&C 93:29: "Man was also in the beginning with God. Intelligence, or the light of truth, was not created or made, neither indeed can be." No psalm claims that — that something in you was never made at all. Elder Neal A. Maxwell put the consequence bluntly: "This doctrine brings unarguable identity but also severe accountability to our lives" ("Premortality, a Glorious Reality," October 1985 general conference). Psalm 139 already supplies the identity. The accountability is what the Restoration adds.

Except the LORD build the house

Psalm 127's superscription is "A Song of degrees for Solomon" — a pilgrim song bearing the temple builder's name, opening on the futility of building. Hebrew bayit covers building, household, and dynasty at once, and the psalm works all three registers in five verses: the house, the city, the children.

D&C 109:5 is Psalm 127:1 answered: "out of our poverty we have given of our substance to build a house to thy name, that the Son of Man might have a place to manifest himself to his people." Elder Gordon B. Hinckley preached the same verse in its domestic register, as counsel to those building a marriage ("Except the Lord Build the House …," April 1971 general conference).

Be honest about where Psalm 128 stops. "Thou shalt see thy children's children, and peace upon Israel" (128:6) blesses a long life well surrounded — grandchildren, in this world, in view. It is not a doctrine of eternal families. The Restoration takes the psalm's line of descent and extends it in both directions past where any psalmist could see: the psalm blesses you with grandchildren you will meet; the sealing ordinance links you to generations you will not.

Breath

Psalm 150 runs through the instruments — trumpet, psaltery, harp, timbrel, dance, strings, organs, two kinds of cymbal — then drops them all in its last line: "Let every thing that hath breath praise the LORD."

Breath is the qualification, and the Psalter has already said why. Of idols, Psalm 135:17: "They have ears, but they hear not; neither is there any breath in their mouths." (The Hebrew words differ between the two verses; the KJV renders both as breath, and the argument holds either way.) So the last line of the book is not only a doxology but a conclusion: the capacity to praise and the capacity to live are the same capacity, and it is precisely what an idol lacks. D&C 25:12 states the corollary flatly — "the song of the righteous is a prayer unto me, and it shall be answered with a blessing upon their heads." Not a compliment to church music. A claim about what a song is.
