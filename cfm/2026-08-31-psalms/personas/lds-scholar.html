The Problems Worth Keeping: Nineteen Psalms, Read Closely

Written by an AI persona in the voice of a Latter-day Saint academic — not a real scholar.

Nineteen chapters is a lot of Psalter for one week, and the selection is not random. All of them sit in Book V, the last of the Psalter's five divisions, and most cluster in two of that book's most obviously curated stretches — the Hallel (113–118) and the closing Hallel (146–150). What follows reads them as the Hebrew reads, and is candid where Latter-day Saint instincts and the philological evidence pull apart. Those are the interesting places.

The book you are actually holding

The Psalter is divided into five books, and the seams are visible: Psalms 41:13, 72:18–19, 89:52, and 106:48 each end with a doxology that has nothing to do with the psalm it is attached to. Psalm 150 does the same job for Book V, except that the whole psalm is the doxology. Gerald H. Wilson's The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter (1985) made the case that these divisions, along with the placement of royal psalms at the seams, reflect deliberate editorial shaping rather than accident. Psalms 146–150 each open and close with halelu-yah — five psalms, ten hallelujahs, visible in the KJV as "Praise ye the LORD" at the top and bottom of each. The book ends on purpose.

How settled that ending was is another question. The great Psalms scroll from Qumran Cave 11 (11QPsa) contains material from this end of the Psalter in a substantially different order, interleaved with compositions that never made it into the Masoretic book; Peter W. Flint argues it reflects a real competing edition rather than a private anthology. The Greek Psalter also divides differently: our Psalm 116 is two psalms in the Septuagint (LXX 114 and 115, split at v. 10), our Psalm 147 likewise (LXX 146 and 147, split at v. 12). This is not trivia. Paul quotes Psalm 116:10 in 2 Corinthians 4:13 — "I believed, therefore have I spoken" — and the Greek is unambiguous where the Hebrew is not; he'emanti ki 'adabber can as easily mean "I kept faith even when I said." The New Testament author is reading the Greek.

Psalm 102, and a heading unlike any other

The superscriptions are ancient text, not modern editorial matter, and Psalm 102's is unique in the Psalter. Every other heading that gives circumstance also gives a person — "A Psalm of David, when he fled from Absalom." This one gives a condition and a posture and no name at all: "A Prayer of the afflicted, when he is overwhelmed, and poureth out his complaint before the LORD." The Hebrew 'ani is the poor or afflicted one generically. The heading hands the psalm to anyone who qualifies.

That anonymity matters. Verses 1–11 are individual and bodily — "my bones are burned as an hearth," "I am like a pelican of the wilderness" — and then verses 13–22 turn without transition to a corporate crisis: "thy servants take pleasure in her stones, and favour the dust thereof" (102:14). Stones and dust are what you have when the walls are down. Verse 16, "When the LORD shall build up Zion, he shall appear in his glory," presupposes that Zion is not built up. Its second half is exilic or later, whatever the first half originally was; the seam is where a personal lament was annexed to a national one.

Hebrews 1:10–12 then takes 102:25–27 — "Of old hast thou laid the foundation of the earth" — and applies it to the Son. In the Hebrew this is addressed to YHWH by a dying man; the author of Hebrews is working from the Greek, where the surrounding verses make the addressee easier to hear as a second figure. Latter-day Saints, who identify Jehovah of the Old Testament with the premortal Christ, will find the result congenial — but the christological reading here is produced by the Greek text and its New Testament use, not by anything in the Hebrew.

Psalm 110: what the Hebrew carries, and what it does not

Verses 1 and 4 of this psalm are quoted or alluded to some thirty-two times in the New Testament; David M. Hay's Glory at the Right Hand (1973) remains the standard catalogue, and Martin Hengel's essay "Sit at My Right Hand!" traces how 110:1 became the load-bearing proof text of early christology. No other Old Testament chapter is used that heavily.

Start with the typography. "The LORD said unto my Lord" (110:1) prints two different words. The first is YHWH; the second is 'adoni, "my lord," the form with a first-person suffix that in the Masoretic Text is used of human superiors — kings, masters — and not of God (the divine form is 'adonai). Read as it stands, this is a court oracle: YHWH's word announced to the king, seating him at the right hand. That is the setting nearly all critical scholarship assigns the psalm — a royal enthronement text from Jerusalem, where the king is also, uniquely in Israel, given priestly standing. Jesus' question in Matthew 22:41–45 depends on collapsing that distinction, and it is a real argument precisely because the Hebrew keeps the two figures apart.

Verse 3 is a wreck. Bratcher and Reyburn's UBS Handbook on the Book of Psalms carries six separate textual notes on this one verse and concludes that the meaning of its second half can no longer be recovered with certainty. The disputes are all small and all consequential: 'ammeka ("thy people") versus 'immeka ("with thee," which the Greek and Latin read); "the beauties of holiness" (be-hadrey) versus a well-attested variant "on the holy mountains" (be-harrey); and, at the end, the Masoretic "thou hast the dew of thy youth" against the Greek's "from the womb, before the morning star, I have begotten thee," which reads the same consonants yldtyk as "I have begotten you." That Greek reading is the one the church fathers built on. Anyone who wants to make a doctrinal argument from Psalm 110:3 in any direction is standing on a verse whose text we do not have.

Verse 4 is textually sound and interpretively loaded: "The LORD hath sworn, and will not repent, Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek." The phrase is 'al-dibrati malki-tsedeq — "according to the manner/order of Melchizedek." Melchizedek appears only twice in the Hebrew Bible, here and in Genesis 14:18, a point Birger A. Pearson makes in the Encyclopedia of Mormonism entry. That is the entire Old Testament basis for a priesthood that, in Latter-day Saint usage, bears his name.

Here the Restoration material genuinely engages the problem rather than decorating it. Doctrine and Covenants 107:3–4 states that the priesthood "was called the Holy Priesthood, after the Order of the Son of God," and was renamed "out of respect or reverence to the name of the Supreme Being, to avoid the too frequent repetition of his name." Whatever one makes of its authority, that is an etiology — an account of why a Melchizedek label attaches to something not originally named for him. The JST expansion of Genesis 14 and Alma 13:14–19 fill out the same figure; John W. Welch calls the Alma 13 material one of the earliest extended expositions of Melchizedek's significance anywhere. Worth noting honestly: the Joseph Smith Translation material published in the Latter-day Saint Bible touches Psalms 11, 14, 24, and 109 and stops there. There is no JST of Psalm 110. The Restoration's Melchizedek material comes from Genesis, Alma, and Section 107 — not from a revision of this psalm.

Psalm 119: the architecture English deletes

Psalm 119 has 176 verses because 22 × 8 = 176. Twenty-two stanzas, one per Hebrew letter, eight verses each, every verse in a stanza beginning with that letter. The KJV prints the letter names — which is why the Come, Follow Me "Scripture Helps" asks about them — but no English translation reproduces the constraint itself. Underneath the acrostic runs a second pattern: eight near-synonyms for divine instruction — torah (law), 'edot (testimonies), piqqudim (precepts), huqqim (statutes), mitzvot (commandments), mishpatim (judgments), davar (word), 'imrah (saying/promise) — distributed so that virtually every verse carries at least one. Verse 122 is the standard exception.

Kent Aaron Reynolds argues in Torah as Teacher that the psalm's sheer length is itself the point: it enacts the unending contemplation it recommends. And Jon D. Levenson's essay "The Sources of Torah" observes what ought to unsettle a quick reading — the torah of Psalm 119 is not simply the Pentateuch. In 176 verses the psalmist never once cites a specific commandment. He repeatedly asks to be taught (vv. 12, 26, 33, 64, 124, 135, 171); he asks God to "open thou mine eyes, that I may behold wondrous things out of thy law" (v. 18); he speaks of the word "settled in heaven" (v. 89). This is instruction as ongoing revelation to a living student, not a closed code being consulted. Latter-day Saints will recognize that shape. The lesson's "path" theme, incidentally, is textual and not imposed: derek (way) runs throughout, and the psalm ends by admitting failure — "I have gone astray like a lost sheep" (v. 176) — a striking last line for a poem of this scale.

The house that is three houses

Psalms 127 and 128 are the week's only Songs of Ascents — two of the fifteen (120–134) that carry the heading shir ha-ma'alot. Psalm 127 is the only one of the fifteen attributed to Solomon, and the attribution looks triggered by the text rather than the reverse. Verse 1 is about building a bayit, the word that does triple duty in Hebrew for temple, dynasty, and household — Solomon built the first, inherited the second, and the psalm spends its second half on the third. Verse 2's "his beloved" is yedido, from the root behind Jedidiah, the name Nathan gave Solomon in 2 Samuel 12:25. Catchwords like these are how psalm headings tend to get assigned.

The same verse's "bread of sorrows" is lehem ha-'atzavim, using the word of the curse in Genesis 3:16–17. The psalm sets that toil against children received as nahalah, an inheritance — a legal term for land granted, not wages earned. Psalm 128 then walks the same household out the door: vine, olive plants, table, and then in vv. 5–6 the blessing comes "out of Zion" and the man lives to see Jerusalem's good. The ascent ends where the pilgrim was headed.

The Hallel, the stone, and the shout

Psalms 113–118 form the Hallel recited at Passover and the pilgrim festivals; rabbinic tradition calls it the "Egyptian Hallel," and reserves "Great Hallel" for Psalm 136, whose ki le-'olam hasdo refrain is plainly antiphonal — a leader's line, a congregation's response, twenty-six times. When Matthew 26:30 says the disciples sang a hymn and went out to the Mount of Olives, this is the material. Psalm 135 belongs here too, and it is frankly an anthology: vv. 15–18, the idol polemic the lesson highlights, are lifted almost verbatim from Psalm 115:4–8, and v. 7 reproduces Jeremiah 10:13. Late Israelite praise is substantially quotation.

Two verses of Psalm 118 carry unusual weight. Verse 22, "The stone which the builders refused is become the head stone of the corner," puns in Hebrew: 'even (stone) against bonim (builders). And verse 25, hoshi'ah na — "Save now, I beseech thee" — is the source of "Hosanna." The Church's own account notes that Psalm 118 was recited daily through the seven days of Tabernacles and that the cry moved from petition to acclamation — which is why the crowds in Matthew 21:9 use it as praise. The Hosanna Shout entered Latter-day Saint practice at the Kirtland Temple dedication in 1836 and has been part of temple dedications since — a direct liturgical descendant of this verse.

Two places not to look away

Psalm 137 ends: "Happy shall he be, that taketh and dasheth thy little ones against the stones." The Hebrew word for "happy" is 'ashre — the same word that opens Psalm 119:1 ("Blessed are the undefiled"), Psalm 127:5 ("Happy is the man that hath his quiver full"), Psalm 128:1 ("Blessed is every one that feareth the LORD"), and Psalm 146:5. The KJV's alternation between "blessed" and "happy" hides it: the beatitude form of the wisdom tradition is being turned on infanticide. Adele Berlin, in her study of exilic psalms, reads 137 as memory-work by a community whose grievance is real: Edom's cry in v. 7, "Rase it, rase it," and Babylon demanding entertainment from the people it had just deported. The psalm's middle verses (5–6) are a self-curse — may my hand fail, may my tongue stick — and v. 9 is the same oath energy pointed outward. It is not a commandment. It is testimony about what atrocity does to the people who survive it, preserved without editorial comment. The honest response is to say so rather than allegorize the babies into bad habits.

Psalm 139 is the other. Verses 13–16 are, for Latter-day Saints, nearly reflexive premortality proof: "thou hast covered me in my mother's womb… in thy book all my members were written." The Hebrew does not support that. Verse 13's "possessed my reins" is qanita kilyotay — you acquired my kidneys, the seat of the inner life. Verse 15's "lowest parts of the earth" stands in parallel with "made in secret," and the consensus of commentators, including the UBS handbook, is that it is a metaphor for the womb — dark, hidden, unsearchable — possibly trading on an old mother-earth image. Verse 16's "my substance, yet being unperfect" is golmi, the unformed embryo. The whole passage is about gestation: God's knowledge of a body being assembled, not a spirit existing before it. The Latter-day Saint doctrine of premortal existence has better footing in Jeremiah 1:5, Abraham 3:22–23, and Doctrine and Covenants 93:29; claiming Psalm 139 for it costs more than it gains. What the psalm does say is bracing enough on its own terms — including vv. 21–22, "I hate them with perfect hatred," which the lesson manual itself flags as needing explanation, and which sits one verse before "Search me, O God, and know my heart."

Every thing that hath breath

Psalm 148 works outward in the order of Genesis 1 — heavens, heavenly bodies, waters above, then earth and its creatures, and finally humans, kings before children. Psalm 149 puts a two-edged sword in the hands of the praising congregation, which is not where a modern reader expects praise to go. And Psalm 150 answers who is included: kol ha-neshamah, "every thing that hath breath." Neshamah is the word of Genesis 2:7, the breath God breathes into the man. The Psalter opens, in 1:1, by sorting humanity with 'ashre — happy is the man who does not walk in the counsel of the ungodly. It closes by handing the congregation to everything that has been given breath. That is an argument, not just an invitation.
