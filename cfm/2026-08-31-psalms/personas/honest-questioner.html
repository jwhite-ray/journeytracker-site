Four Beatitudes and a Smashed Child: Reading the Whole Block

Written by an AI persona that surfaces the hard questions honestly — not a real person.

The framing problem

The lesson is titled "Let Every Thing That Hath Breath Praise the Lord," which is a real quotation from a real verse at the real end of the Psalter (Psalm 150:6). It is not a misrepresentation. It is a selection.

Here is what else is in the assigned nineteen chapters. A man so wrecked he cannot eat: "My heart is smitten, and withered like grass; so that I forget to eat my bread" (102:4). A man who eats ashes and mingles his drink with weeping (102:9), and who blames God for it: "Because of thine indignation and thy wrath: for thou hast lifted me up, and cast me down" (102:10). A congregation holding a sword while it sings, "To execute vengeance upon the heathen... to bind their kings with chains" (149:6–8). A psalmist who declares, "I hate them with perfect hatred" (139:22). And Psalm 137:9.

Track what the lesson page actually covers: Psalms 102–3 and 116 (comfort), 110 and 118 (messianic fulfillment), 119 (the path), 135 (idols), 139 (God knows me), 146–50 (praise). Psalm 137 is in the reading block and appears nowhere in the study ideas. Psalms 127–28 appear only as a "Scripture Helps" link. To the manual's credit, that same section asks outright, "Why does this psalm speak about 'perfect hatred'?"—so the curriculum is not pretending Psalm 139 is uniformly tender. But no one is being asked what to do with the last verse of 137.

That is the honest shape of the week: these psalms are not one genre wearing one mood. Walter Brueggemann's argument in "The Costly Loss of Lament" (JSOT 36 [1986], 57–71) is that when a worshipping community stops praying the complaint psalms, it loses the standing to raise questions of justice with God at all — the second casualty he names is the stifling of theodicy itself. Whether the manual's framing costs anything of that kind is a fair question to hold open all week.

Psalm 137:9

"Happy shall he be, that taketh and dasheth thy little ones against the stones."

Do not blunt this. The word behind "happy" is 'ashrei, the beatitude formula—the same word that opens Psalm 1, and the same word that appears three other times in this very block: "Happy is the man that hath his quiver full of them" (127:5), "Blessed is every one that feareth the LORD" (128:1), "Happy is he that hath the God of Jacob for his help" (146:5). This block contains four beatitudes. One of them pronounces blessing on a man who kills infants by throwing them at a wall. That is not an ugly aside; it is the psalm's closing line, its rhetorical climax, and it is grammatically identical in form to the blessings the lesson wants you to memorize.

Readers have handled this four ways, and every one of them costs something.

Excision. The 1971 Roman Liturgy of the Hours dropped Psalms 58, 83, and 109 entirely and cut verses from roughly twenty others, including 137:7–9 (tabulated by Felix Just, SJ, at catholic-resources.org). The stated reason, per the General Instruction, was "a certain psychological difficulty." Musical settings do the same: David W. Stowe's Song of Exile: The Enduring Mystery of Psalm 137 (Oxford, 2016) traces the psalm through Rastafari Jamaica, Irish and Korean and Cuban immigration, and the civil rights movement, and notes that the final verses are nearly always dropped. The cost: you have decided which scripture counts, on grounds of taste, and you must say who holds that authority. For a tradition that qualifies the Bible only "as far as it is translated correctly," cutting on grounds of discomfort is a different move entirely.

Allegory. Augustine read the "little ones of Babylon" as nascent evil desires, to be destroyed while still small: dash them against the Rock, "and that Rock is Christ" (Exposition on Psalm 137). C. S. Lewis, in "The Cursings" (chapter 3 of Reflections on the Psalms, 1958), does something similar with the small indulgences that seem too helpless to resist. The cost: a method that converts infants into bad habits will convert anything into anything, and it disappears the actual Babylonian children, whose deaths the verse actually blesses.

Relocation to the victim. Erich Zenger's A God of Vengeance? Understanding the Psalms of Divine Wrath (trans. Linda M. Maloney; Westminster John Knox, 1996) argues that these psalms are laments that surrender the last word to God rather than seize it—the speaker who prays for vengeance is precisely the speaker who is not taking it. Read this way, 137:9 is someone who watched Babylon do this to Judah's children saying so out loud, in liturgy, instead of picking up a rock. The cost: the psalm is still canonized as prayer, still addressed to God, and never repudiated inside the text. Descriptive-not-prescriptive works as a category for a narrative like Judges. It is harder to apply to a hymn the community was expected to sing.

Talionic fulfillment. Verse 8 says "as thou hast served us," and Isaiah 13:16 already promised precisely this fate for Babylon's infants. On this reading, 137:9 is not proposing a policy; it is asking God to do what God said he would do. The cost is obvious: the problem moves upstairs. You have not removed the blessing on infanticide; you have made God its author.

Notice that Latter-day Saints have no unique advantage here and one unique disadvantage: the Restoration's high view of prophetic inspiration makes it harder, not easier, to say that a canonized psalm is simply wrong.

Psalm 110:1 and the argument in Matthew 22

Psalm 110 is the most-quoted Old Testament text in the New Testament—David M. Hay counted more than thirty references and allusions in Glory at the Right Hand: Psalm 110 in Early Christianity (SBL, 1973). The KJV's typography carries the whole load of verse 1: "The LORD said unto my Lord." Small capitals mark YHWH; mixed case marks 'adoni, "my lord."

In Matthew 22:41–46 Jesus asks the Pharisees whose son the Messiah is, they answer David's, and he replies: then why does David, in the Spirit, call him Lord? Two premises bear the weight. David wrote the psalm; and the second "lord" is someone other than David.

Mainstream scholarship pressures both. The superscription reads le-David, and the Hebrew lamed can mean "of," "to," "for," "belonging to," or "concerning." The psalm reads naturally as a court oracle in which a temple prophet or singer addresses the reigning king: my lord is the king, and the poem is a coronation piece. Leslie C. Allen's Psalms 101–150 (Word Biblical Commentary 21, rev. ed.) treats a pre-exilic royal setting as the leading view; a Hasmonean date has been proposed and has not won broad support. The psalm's Hebrew is also damaged: verse 3's consonants ילדתיך can be vocalized as "your youth" (MT, hence the KJV's "the dew of thy youth") or as "I have begotten you," which is how the Septuagint and Vulgate read it.

If the court-oracle reading is right, the puzzle Jesus poses dissolves—not because the Messiah is not David's Lord, but because the psalm was never a riddle. Faithful readers have four moves, and each costs something.

Hold Davidic authorship because Jesus asserted it, and treat the critical consensus as wrong. Cost: the positive case for Davidic authorship rests almost entirely on the superscription, which is the thing in dispute; you are defending the conclusion with the premise.

Treat the argument as ad hominem—Jesus reasoning validly from premises his opponents granted, without endorsing them. Cost: Matthew gives no signal of this, and the reader is left deciding case by case when Jesus's premises are his own.

Treat it as accommodation to first-century knowledge. Cost: you now owe an account of what else the mortal Jesus did not know, and Latter-day Saint teaching on Christ's foreknowledge does not make that account easy.

Treat the psalm as a royal poem the Spirit always intended messianically. This is probably the most defensible position, and it still does not rescue this argument, which turns on authorship, not typology.

How much priesthood is in Psalm 110:4?

The verse says: God swore an oath; you are a priest forever; 'al-divrati malki-tsedeq. That is the entire content. No ordination, no conferral, no keys, no offices, no distinction between two priesthoods, no organizational structure. Dibrah means something like "manner" or "cause"—rendering it "order" already imports an institution the Hebrew does not supply. And malki-tsedeq can be read as a proper name or as the phrase "my righteous king."

Everything else Latter-day Saints associate with the phrase comes from elsewhere: Genesis 14:18–20, Hebrews 5–7, Alma 13:14–19, the Joseph Smith Translation's long expansion at JST Genesis 14:17–40, and Doctrine and Covenants 107. Read D&C 107:2–4 slowly; it concedes more than most lessons notice. It says the priesthood was called "the Holy Priesthood, after the Order of the Son of God," and that the ancient church renamed it after Melchizedek to avoid repeating the divine name. On its own terms, "Melchizedek Priesthood" is a reverential substitution, not a claim that the historical Melchizedek founded an institution.

Avram R. Shannon's "After Whose Order? Kingship and Priesthood in the Book of Mormon" (BYU Studies 60, no. 4) argues that even Book of Mormon priesthood language works on Judahite king-priest categories rather than the modern Aaronic/Melchizedek division—a Latter-day Saint scholar at a Church university saying the ancient material does not map cleanly onto the modern structure. Whether that is a problem or a description of how restoration works is the question to sit with. The circle to avoid is reading D&C 107 into Psalm 110:4 and then citing Psalm 110:4 as evidence for D&C 107.

Psalm 139: the tender verses and the hatred, in one poem

Verses 13–16 are where many Latter-day Saints hear premortal existence. The Hebrew is not obviously about that. "Thou hast possessed my reins" (v. 13) is kilyotai, kidneys—the seat of the interior life. "The lowest parts of the earth" (v. 15) is a standard poetic figure for the womb's hiddenness. "My substance, yet being unperfect" (v. 16) translates golmi, a word occurring nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible, meaning an unformed mass or embryo. The subject is embryology and divine knowledge: God saw the body before it was a body.

There is a real scholarly defense of the other reading. Mitchell Dahood, in Psalms III: 101–150 (Anchor Bible; Doubleday, 1970), 295, argued that verse 15 reflects a belief that the soul originated in another sphere—the passage Scripture Central cites when explaining Latter-day Saint premortality. Honesty requires the next sentence: Dahood's Ugaritic-driven method is the most contested in modern Psalms scholarship, routinely faulted for reaching for a Ugaritic cognate where ordinary Hebrew will do. The strongest external support for the Latter-day Saint reading here comes from the field's least-followed commentator. That does not make it false. It does mean the verse is a resonance with a doctrine taught plainly elsewhere (Abraham 3:22–23; D&C 93:29), not a proof of it. The lesson page, notably, claims no such thing; its heading is "The Lord knows my heart."

Then verses 19–22, five verses after "fearfully and wonderfully made": "Do not I hate them, O LORD, that hate thee?... I hate them with perfect hatred." The Roman office deletes exactly these verses; the manual, to its credit, asks about them. The one structural fact worth carrying is what comes next: verse 23 is "Search me, O God, and know my heart"—the psalmist submits the hatred he has just professed to inspection, without withdrawing it. That is an observation, not a resolution.

Psalm 127, and who is left out

"Lo, children are an heritage of the LORD... Happy is the man that hath his quiver full of them: they shall not be ashamed, but they shall speak with the enemies in the gate" (127:3–5). The image is not sentimental. Grown sons are arrows, and the payoff is legal and military standing at the city gate—social power in a patrilineal agrarian society.

It has nonetheless been read as a mandate. Kathryn Joyce's Quiverfull: Inside the Christian Patriarchy Movement (Beacon, 2009) documents a movement that took its name from this verse. And in a Latter-day Saint ward, 127:5 read alongside 128:3–6—the fruitful vine, the olive plants, the grandchildren—describes a blessing some faithful readers will never receive, through no decision of their own. No verse in this block is addressed to them.

The strongest internal counterweight is that Psalm 127's first half is an anti-striving poem: "It is vain for you to rise up early, to sit up late, to eat the bread of sorrows: for so he giveth his beloved sleep" (127:2). Read whole, it argues against treating any outcome as an achievement. That helps. It does not erase verse 5, which still names a happiness and attaches it to a count.

Psalm 118:22 and Psalm 119, briefly

"The stone which the builders refused is become the head stone of the corner" (118:22) sits inside a thanksgiving liturgy in which a speaker recounts deliverance. The Aramaic Targum reads the stone as David, rejected among Jesse's sons and found worthy to be king. The New Testament reads it as Jesus (Matthew 21:42; Acts 4:10–11; 1 Peter 2:7), and Jacob 4:15–16 works the same image the same way. The honest state of the question: "prophecy" and "later re-reading" are mutually exclusive only if prediction is the sole permitted mode of prophecy. Accepting both costs something real—the verse's original sense becomes theologically inert, and the New Testament's method becomes one no modern exegesis class would accept as reading.

Psalm 119 is 176 verses in 22 acrostic stanzas of 8, one per Hebrew letter; the manual's Scripture Helps asks about exactly this. Its difficulty for Latter-day Saints runs opposite to the usual direction. The psalmist says "I have kept thy precepts and thy testimonies" (v. 168), "I have more understanding than all my teachers" (v. 99), "I have not departed from thy judgments" (v. 102). This is not a penitent; it is a claimant. Restoration readers, catechized on 2 Nephi 25:23, tend to find this psalm congenial and Paul's argument in Romans hard—itself the tension worth naming. The psalm does not resolve it: after 175 verses of blamelessness, the last line is "I have gone astray like a lost sheep" (v. 176). Claim and confession share the poem, unreconciled.
