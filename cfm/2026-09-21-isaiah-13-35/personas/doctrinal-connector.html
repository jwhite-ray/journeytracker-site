The Throne Grasped and the Key Given

Written by an AI persona connecting the block to Restoration scripture and modern teaching — not a real person.

Two figures stand at opposite ends of this week's reading. In Isaiah 14 a king climbs: "I will ascend into heaven, I will exalt my throne above the stars of God... I will be like the most High" (14:13–14). In Isaiah 22 a steward is dressed: "I will clothe him with thy robe... and the key of the house of David will I lay upon his shoulder" (22:21–22). One reaches for a throne; the other has authority laid on him. Restoration scripture builds out both portraits, the first toward the premortal council and the second toward priesthood keys and the temple.

A taunt that became a biography

Isaiah 14:4 calls what follows a "proverb," a taunt-song "against the king of Babylon." The Hebrew in 14:12 is helel ben shachar, "shining one, son of dawn," which the Latin Vulgate rendered lucifer, "light-bearer," the ordinary Latin word for the morning star. The "mount of the congregation, in the sides of the north" (14:13) is the language of the Canaanite divine assembly on Mount Zaphon, and Michael S. Heiser has argued that 14:12–15 draws on the Ugaritic myth of Athtar, a lesser god who climbed toward Baal's vacant throne and could not fill it. On the page, then, Isaiah uses a myth of a star's failed ascent to mock a human tyrant now rotting in Sheol under "the worm" (14:11).

Latter-day Saint doctrine does not need to force the Hebrew, because it reached the premortal rebellion by a separate path. Moses 4, received in 1830, tells the story with no reference to Babylon. Satan offers, "I will redeem all mankind... wherefore give me thine honor"; the Beloved Son answers, "Father, thy will be done, and the glory be thine forever" (Moses 4:1–2). Satan "sought to destroy the agency of man" and was "cast down" (4:3; compare Abraham 3:27–28). Only then, in 1832, does D&C 76 name him in Isaiah's terms: "Lucifer, a son of the morning... he is fallen! is fallen" (D&C 76:26–27).

The Restoration reading is therefore typological: the king of Babylon behaves as Lucifer did, so the taunt fits both. The manual pairs Isaiah 14:4–20 with Moses 4:1–4, and the two share a grammar, the repeated "I will." Satan says "I will redeem... surely I will do it"; the king says "I will ascend... I will exalt... I will sit... I will be like the most High." Only the Son puts the glory somewhere else. Note one more line: the tyrant "opened not the house of his prisoners" (14:17).

Nephi copied these chapters (2 Nephi 23–24) with differences. In 2 Nephi 23:3 the Lord's "anger is not upon them that rejoice in my highness." In 23:15 the one thrust through is "Every one that is proud," not "every one that is found." Chapter 23 ends with an added line: "for I will be merciful unto my people, but the wicked shall perish" (23:22). These variants aim the oracle morally, but they do not remove its horror. Nephi's text still has children "dashed to pieces before their eyes" (23:16, 18), and a doctrinal reading should not soften what the Book of Mormon leaves standing. Nor should it hide the dating problem. Many critical scholars place an oracle naming Babylon and the Medes in the sixth century BC, which presses hard on its presence on brass plates that left Jerusalem around 600 BC. Believers have to carry that tension honestly.

The key on the shoulder

In Isaiah 22:15–25 the steward Shebna is hewing himself a showy tomb "on high... in a rock" (22:16), a smaller version of the king's "I will ascend." The Lord removes him and installs Eliakim in the language of investiture: "I will clothe him with thy robe, and strengthen him with thy girdle" (22:21), garments Exodus also assigns to the priesthood. Then comes the image Revelation later gives to Christ: "the key of the house of David... so he shall open, and none shall shut; and he shall shut, and none shall open" (22:22; compare Revelation 3:7).

In Restoration scripture that phrase becomes sealing language: "whatsoever you seal on earth shall be sealed in heaven... whosesoever sins you retain on earth shall be retained in heaven" (D&C 132:46; compare 124:93). Joseph Smith calls the sealing power, "in one sense of the word, the keys of the kingdom, which consist in the key of knowledge" (D&C 128:14). President Dallin H. Oaks described the same two-sidedness: "the function of priesthood keys both enlarges and limits" ("The Keys and Authority of the Priesthood," April 2014).

Now return to the tyrant who "opened not the house of his prisoners." Isaiah 24 pictures "the host of the high ones that are on high" and the kings of the earth "gathered together, as prisoners are gathered in the pit... and after many days shall they be visited" (24:21–22). Restoration scripture reads that visit as the redemption of the dead. The Son "visited, and preached the gospel" to "the spirits of men kept in prison" (D&C 76:73); Joseph's letter on baptism for the dead proclaims that "the prisoners shall go free" (D&C 128:22); and in his 1918 vision President Joseph F. Smith saw Isaiah among the spirits, the prophet who foretold "the opening of the prison to them that were bound" (D&C 138:42). The one who grasped the throne would not open the prison; the one given the key does. The manual never sets these chapters side by side, but they belong together.

Then the nail. "I will fasten him as a nail in a sure place" (22:23), a peg on which hang "all the glory of his father's house" (22:24). But the next verse says the nail "shall be removed, and be cut down, and fall" (22:25). Read plainly, Eliakim too gave way under his family's weight. Spencer Kraus proposes reading verse 25 conditionally ("should the nail... be removed"), noting an 1844 Times and Seasons editorial that called the nail "a mystery to the world." Either way, keys in mortal hands are held in trust and can be lost. Only one steward is a nail that never gives, which is why the Church's modern chapter heading (an editorial summary, not scripture) applies the nail to the Messiah, and why Helaman's words fit here: build "upon the rock of our Redeemer... which is a sure foundation, a foundation whereon if men build they cannot fall" (Helaman 5:12; compare Isaiah 28:16).

The feast on the mountain, and the veil

Isaiah 25:6–8 is the week's richest temple passage. "In this mountain" the Lord spreads "a feast of fat things... of wines on the lees well refined" for "all people"; he destroys "the veil that is spread over all nations"; he "will swallow up death in victory" and "wipe away tears from off all faces."

The Doctrine and Covenants lifts the menu word for word. Sending elders to Missouri in 1831, the Lord says Zion is founded so "that a feast of fat things might be prepared for the poor; yea, a feast of fat things, of wine on the lees well refined... a supper of the house of the Lord, well prepared, unto which all nations shall be invited" (D&C 58:8–9). The guest list follows: "First, the rich and the learned, the wise and the noble; And after that cometh the day of my power; then shall the poor, the lame, and the blind, and the deaf, come in unto the marriage of the Lamb" (58:10–11). That list fuses two more of this week's passages: the learned who fail with the sealed book (29:11–12), and the blind and deaf who are healed (29:18; 35:5–6).

The rent veil has its own counterpart: "the veil of darkness shall soon be rent" (D&C 38:8), and to those who sanctify themselves the Lord "will unveil his face" (D&C 88:68). A feast on the Lord's mountain where a veil is removed and death is overcome is, for Latter-day Saints, the shape of the temple. That is association rather than exegesis, though the pilgrim of 30:29 who goes "into the mountain of the LORD" invites it.

"Thy dead men shall live"

Isaiah 26 sets two verses against each other. Of the "other lords" who ruled Judah: "They are dead, they shall not live; they are deceased, they shall not rise" (26:14). Of the Lord's own: "Thy dead men shall live, together with my dead body shall they arise. Awake and sing, ye that dwell in dust" (26:19). Scholars debate whether this is bodily resurrection or, like Ezekiel's dry bones, national revival, and many date chapters 24–27 well after Isaiah.

Restoration scripture does not leave it there. Abinadi: "there is a resurrection, therefore the grave hath no victory, and the sting of death is swallowed up in Christ" (Mosiah 16:8), which is Isaiah 25:8 by way of Paul (1 Corinthians 15:54–55), anchored to an actual risen Christ. D&C 88:97–101 even keeps Isaiah's contrast between those who rise and those who do not, but turns exclusion into sequence: those "who have slept in their graves shall come forth" at the first trump, while "the rest of the dead... live not again until the thousand years are ended." Elder D. Todd Christofferson put the stakes plainly: "Because He was resurrected, Jesus cannot have been only a carpenter, a teacher, a rabbi, or a prophet" ("The Resurrection of Jesus Christ," April 2014). If 26:19 is only national, Isaiah is a poet of hope; if literal, a witness. The Book of Mormon treats him as a witness.

A voice from the dust and a sealed book

Isaiah 29 is where the Restoration's claims are strongest and most contested. In the Hebrew, Ariel (Jerusalem) is besieged and "brought down," whispering "out of the dust" like a ghost (29:4); the "book that is sealed," unreadable by learned and unlearned alike (29:11–12), is a simile for a people deaf to their own prophets.

Nephi applies the siege to his descendants: after the Lord "shall have camped against them round about... those who shall be destroyed shall speak unto them out of the ground" (2 Nephi 26:15–16). Moroni carries the image to the last page (Mormon 8:23; Moroni 10:27, "as one speaking out of the dust"). 2 Nephi 27 then expands the sealed-book passage: a sealed portion kept "until the own due time of the Lord" (27:10), "three witnesses" (27:12), a learned man who asks for the book to be brought to him (27:15), and the Lord's refrain, "I am able to do mine own work" (27:20–21). Joseph Smith–History 1:63–65 gives Martin Harris's account of his February 1828 visit to Charles Anthon: "I cannot read a sealed book."

The difficulties are real. The Church's own history topic acknowledges that Anthon later "denied having authenticated the characters," and that Harris and Joseph "later spoke of the visit to Anthon as a fulfillment of Isaiah's prophecy." Most researchers also conclude that the small plates, including 2 Nephi, were dictated last, probably in June 1829, more than a year after the visit. A skeptic sees a prophecy shaped to an event already past; a believer sees the Lord explaining to the translator what the scholar's refusal meant. There is a third frame. Nephi read Isaiah to his people because "I did liken all scriptures unto us" (1 Nephi 19:23). Monte S. Nyman observes that Joseph Smith's own Bible revision of Isaiah 29 keeps Jerusalem as the subject, and offers as one explanation that "Nephi was merely applying the scriptures to the known destiny of his people." The Restoration itself practices likening. It does not claim Isaiah was thinking of Palmyra; it claims the same God is doing a like work again.

That work is 29:13–14: a people honoring God "with their lips" while their fear of him "is taught by the precept of men," so he "will proceed to do a marvellous work... and a wonder." D&C 4:1 echoes it: "a marvelous work is about to come forth among the children of men." President Russell M. Nelson tied the book to the gathering: "if there were no Book of Mormon, the promised gathering of Israel would not occur" ("The Gathering of Scattered Israel," October 2006). In the same talk he cites Isaiah 27:6, Israel filling "the face of the world with fruit."

Precept upon precept, turned right side up

One reuse needs a warning label. In Isaiah 28:10–13, "precept upon precept... line upon line... here a little, and there a little" is mockery. In Hebrew it sounds like nursery babble (tsav latsav, qav laqav): drunken priests sneer at Isaiah's teaching as baby talk, and the Lord answers that he will speak to them exactly so, "that they might go, and fall backward, and be broken" (28:13). It is a judgment. Nephi turns it into a blessing: "I will give unto the children of men line upon line, precept upon precept... and blessed are those who hearken" (2 Nephi 28:30; compare D&C 98:12; 128:21). That is sound doctrine about incremental revelation, but it is not what Isaiah 28 means.

The same chapter supplies the covenant contrast that runs through the block. Jerusalem's rulers boast, "We have made a covenant with death" (28:15); the earth is defiled because its people have "broken the everlasting covenant" (24:5). The Lord's preface to the Doctrine and Covenants echoes that verse and joins it to Isaiah's Babylon: "they have strayed from mine ordinances, and have broken mine everlasting covenant," each following an idol "which waxeth old and shall perish in Babylon, even Babylon the great" (D&C 1:15–16). The next verse is the call of Joseph Smith (1:17). The Restoration presents itself as the answer to Isaiah 24:5.

The highway home

Chapter 35 closes the block, and the revelations lean on it heavily. "In the barren deserts there shall come forth pools of living water; and the parched ground shall no longer be a thirsty land" (D&C 133:29, echoing 35:7); "an highway shall be cast up in the midst of the great deep" (133:27) for the returning tribes. The ransomed who "come to Zion with songs and everlasting joy upon their heads" (35:10) reappear in the gathering revelations, "singing with songs of everlasting joy" (D&C 45:71) and "with songs of everlasting joy upon their heads" (66:11; compare 101:18).

"The way of holiness" (35:8) is the final answer to Isaiah 14. The king who said "I will ascend" was "brought down to hell, to the sides of the pit" (14:15). No one climbs this highway by force: "the unclean shall not pass over it... but the redeemed shall walk there" (35:8–9), even "the wayfaring men, though fools," who "shall not err therein." Isaiah 27:12 has already said how they arrive: "ye shall be gathered one by one, O ye children of Israel." The block moves from a throne seized to a road given, from a prison left shut to a key that opens. Restoration scripture takes that shape into the premortal council, the sealing power, the redemption of the dead, the Book of Mormon, and the gathering, and it reads Isaiah best precisely where it admits it is likening him.
