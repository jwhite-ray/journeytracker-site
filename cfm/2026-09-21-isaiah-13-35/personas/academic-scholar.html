The Day Star, the Dragon, and a Covenant with Death: Isaiah 13–35 in Its Ancient World

Written by an AI persona in the voice of a non-Latter-day-Saint Hebrew Bible academic — not a real scholar.

A book assembled over centuries

Nearly all critical scholars regard the book of Isaiah as the product of some three centuries of composition. Some oracles plausibly go back to Isaiah son of Amoz, active in Jerusalem under Ahaz and Hezekiah in the late eighth century BCE; chapters 40–55 come from the Babylonian exile; and much else was added and reapplied along the way. Joseph Blenkinsopp's Anchor Yale commentary is readier to find exilic and later layers throughout, while J. J. M. Roberts's Hermeneia volume, First Isaiah, argues that the eighth-century prophet often reworked his own early oracles for later crises, leaving traces of their first setting in place.

This week's selection samples three collections: the "oracles against the nations" (chapters 13–23, a standard prophetic genre; compare Amos 1–2), the cosmic poems of chapters 24–27 that nineteenth-century scholarship labeled the "Isaiah Apocalypse," and the woe oracles of chapters 28–33, mostly tied to Hezekiah's Assyrian crisis. Chapter 35 stands apart again, reading like a preview of Second Isaiah.

Babylon, or Assyria? The burden of chapters 13–14

The superscription reads "The burden of Babylon, which Isaiah the son of Amoz did see" (13:1); massaʼ, "burden," is a technical heading for an oracle. The problem is the target. In Isaiah's lifetime the great enemy was Assyria, and Babylon was a restless Assyrian province. The poem names the Medes as the destroyers (13:17), which fits the sixth century, when Babylon was the empire that had destroyed Jerusalem. Most critical scholars therefore date the oracle, in its present form, to the exile.

Yet the seams show. Right after the taunt over the king of Babylon, Yahweh swears, "I will break the Assyrian in my land" (14:25). And the taunt song (14:4b–21) has an odd detail: the fallen king is denied burial, "cast out of thy grave like an abominable branch" (14:19–20). No Babylonian king is known to have died that way, but the Assyrian Sargon II was killed in battle in 705 BCE and his body never recovered. H. L. Ginsberg argued in "Reflexes of Sargon in Isaiah after 715 B.C.E." that the poem originally targeted Sargon and was later retitled for Babylon. Many scholars accept some version of this two-stage history.

Chapter 13 also raises an ethical problem that should be stated plainly. Verses 15–18 describe infants "dashed to pieces before their eyes," wives "ravished," and soldiers with "no pity on the fruit of the womb," and the poet calls these soldiers Yahweh's "sanctified ones" (13:3). This is the real texture of ancient siege warfare, the same acts Assyrian kings boasted of in their inscriptions, here imagined by a victim nation as falling on its oppressor. It should not be softened into allegory.

Helel ben Shachar

The most famous line in the block is 14:12: "How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning!" The Hebrew helel ben shachar means "shining one, son of dawn," almost certainly the morning star, the planet Venus. The Septuagint rendered it heōsphoros, "dawn-bringer," and Jerome's Vulgate lucifer, "light-bearer," an ordinary Latin word for the morning star, which the King James translators kept as a proper name. Most modern translations read "Day Star."

The passage is a mashal (14:4, "proverb"), a taunt song, and its subject is explicitly human: "Is this the man that made the earth to tremble?" (14:16). But the insult borrows a myth. The usurper vows to sit "upon the mount of the congregation, in the sides of the north" (14:13), Hebrew har moʻed beyarkete tsafon, "the mount of assembly in the recesses of Zaphon." Zaphon is the Ugaritic name of the storm-god Baal's sacred mountain, and Shachar, "Dawn," is himself a Ugaritic deity. John Day's Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan treats this background in detail. A frequently cited parallel is the Ugaritic episode in which ʻAthtar, an astral god associated with Venus, tries to occupy Baal's vacant throne, finds his feet do not reach the footstool, and comes down to rule the earth instead. Scholars debate whether one specific myth lies behind Isaiah 14, but the setting of divine mountain, star god, and failed ascent is unmistakably Canaanite.

Only later, once Jewish and Christian readers had developed the idea of a fallen angelic adversary, was 14:12 read as describing him. In the poem the fall is into sheʼol (14:15), the underworld, where the rephaʼim (14:9, "the dead"; cognate with Ugaritic rpum, the royal dead) rise from their thrones to jeer: "Art thou also become weak as we?" (14:10). This is ancient underworld imagery put to work as political satire.

Jerusalem at the water's edge: Isaiah 22

Chapter 22 puts us on the ground in Jerusalem. The prophet ridicules a city "wholly gone up to the housetops" in celebration (22:1) and lists emergency works: "ye have numbered the houses of Jerusalem, and the houses have ye broken down to fortify the wall. Ye made also a ditch between the two walls for the water of the old pool" (22:10–11). In the 1970s Nahman Avigad excavated the Broad Wall in Jerusalem's Jewish Quarter, a massive late eighth-century fortification built directly over demolished houses, and connected it to Hezekiah and to this verse.

Most commentators set 22:1–14 in or just after 701 BCE, when Sennacherib devastated Judah. His annals claim forty-six fortified cities taken and Hezekiah shut up in Jerusalem "like a bird in a cage," followed by heavy tribute. The city survived, and the prophet rages at its revelry, "let us eat and drink; for to morrow we shall die" (22:13), when it should be mourning.

The oracle against Shebna, "which is over the house" (22:15), meets a rare echo in stone. A tomb inscription from Silwan, across the Kidron Valley, now in the British Museum and deciphered by Avigad, reads roughly: "This is [the tomb of ...]yahu who is over the house. There is no silver and no gold here." Many scholars think the name is Shebnayahu, and the tomb the very one mocked in 22:16; plausible, not provable. Eliakim receives "the key of the house of David" (22:22), the steward's authority, but 22:25 abruptly declares that "the nail that is fastened in the sure place" will fall. Most critics read that verse as a later addition: a promotion oracle revised in light of later events.

The "Isaiah Apocalypse" and the swallowing of Death

Chapters 24–27 abandon named nations. The earth is emptied (24:1), a nameless "city of confusion" (qiryat tohu, "city of chaos," with the tohu of Genesis 1:2) falls (24:10), and the "host of the high ones" are jailed in a pit with earthly kings (24:21–22). The inhabitants have "broken the everlasting covenant" (24:5), berit ʻolam, the phrase of the covenant with Noah (Genesis 9:16), and "the windows from on high are open" (24:18) as in the flood (Genesis 7:11). The poet imagines creation undone a second time.

Dating these chapters is a long-running dispute. Bernhard Duhm (1892) placed them very late; proposals have since ranged from the eighth century to the Maccabean era, and the unnamed city has been identified as Babylon, Nineveh, Tyre, Samaria, and others. William Millar's Isaiah 24–27 and the Origin of Apocalyptic read them as a bridge from prophecy to apocalyptic, rooted in the old myth of the divine warrior. Christopher B. Hays's The Origins of Isaiah 24–27 pushes the other way: the chapters are not apocalyptic, their Hebrew is not demonstrably late, and they celebrate Assyria's retreat in the late seventh century under Josiah. Either way, "apocalypse" is now widely considered a misleading label, since the chapters lack the visionary guides and periodized history of true apocalypses such as Daniel 7–12.

What is not disputed is the mythological substrate. The mountain banquet "unto all people" (25:6) resembles the feasts of the gods in Ugaritic myth. Then 25:8: billaʻ hammawet lanetsach, "he will swallow up death forever." In the Ugaritic Baal cycle, Mot (Death) is a god with an enormous gullet who swallows Baal himself; Isaiah reverses the image, and the God of Israel swallows the swallower. (The KJV's "in victory" reflects a later Aramaic sense of netsach; the Hebrew means "forever.") Hays's A Covenant with Death argues that death and deliverance from death run through the early strata of Isaiah, the prophet turning the region's shared underworld imagery against Judah's rulers and Assyria.

Leviathan and the dead who rise

Isaiah 27:1 is among the clearest cases of dependence on Canaanite myth in the Hebrew Bible. Yahweh will punish "leviathan the piercing serpent, even leviathan that crooked serpent": Hebrew nachash bariach and nachash ʻaqallaton, "fleeing serpent" and "twisting serpent," match the Ugaritic btn brḥ and btn ʻqltn, epithets of Lotan, the seven-headed sea monster Baal defeats. Leviathan and Lotan are the same name. John Day's God's Conflict with the Dragon and the Sea shows how Israel's poets moved this dragon-fight from creation to the end of days.

The resurrection passage in 26:19 answers 26:14: "They are dead, they shall not live; they are deceased, they shall not rise," where "deceased" is again rephaʼim, the shades of the foreign "lords" who once ruled Judah (26:13). Then: "Thy dead men shall live... Awake and sing, ye that dwell in dust" (26:19). Is this bodily resurrection or national revival, as in Ezekiel's valley of dry bones (Ezekiel 37)? The metaphor fits a nation that has "brought forth wind" (26:18). But the wording is very close to Daniel 12:2, "many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake," the Hebrew Bible's one unambiguous statement of individual resurrection, from the 160s BCE. Scholars who date chapters 24–27 late tend to read 26:19 literally; those who date them early tend to read it nationally.

Drunken priests and baby talk: Isaiah 28

Chapter 28 opens with a woe against "the drunkards of Ephraim" (28:1), whose capital Samaria sat like a garland on its hill. Samaria fell in 722 BCE, so the core of 28:1–4 predates that; the text then turns on Jerusalem, where "the priest and the prophet have erred through strong drink" and "all tables are full of vomit" (28:7–8).

Verses 9–10 are the most debated Hebrew in the block. "Precept upon precept... line upon line" renders tsav latsav tsav latsav, qav laqav qav laqav, zeʻer sham zeʻer sham. Most likely the prophet is quoting his opponents, who mock him as a nursery teacher ("them that are weaned from the milk," 28:9), and tsav and qav are near-nonsense syllables. William W. Hallo, in "Isaiah 28:9–13 and the Ugaritic Abecedaries," noted that tsade and qof are consecutive letters and suggested schoolchildren reciting the alphabet. Karel van der Toorn, in "Echoes of Judaean Necromancy in Isaiah 28, 7–22," proposed instead the chirping of spirits consulted by necromancers. Either way, the prophet turns the mockery back: Yahweh will speak "with stammering lips and another tongue" (28:11), the foreign speech of Assyrian soldiers, and the same syllables become a sentence of doom (28:13). The reading of the line as a positive program of gradual instruction is a later reuse, not the Hebrew's sense.

The rulers' boast, "We have made a covenant with death, and with hell are we at agreement" (28:15), is the prophet's sarcastic paraphrase of their foreign policy, almost certainly the Egyptian alliance, with Death and Sheol possibly glancing at underworld deities, as van der Toorn and Hays both explore. Against it Yahweh lays "in Zion for a foundation a stone" (28:16), followed by words better translated "the one who trusts will not panic," perhaps imagined as the stone's inscription. The "strange work" of 28:21 is Yahweh fighting against Jerusalem as he once fought for David at Perazim (2 Samuel 5:20).

Ariel, the sealed vision, and Egypt that sits still

"Woe to Ariel, to Ariel, the city where David dwelt!" (29:1). ʼAriʼel plays on a word for the altar hearth (compare Ezekiel 43:15–16), and the Moabite Mesha Stele mentions an ʼrʼl seized from an Israelite town and dragged before the god Kemosh. The pun is grim: the city "shall be unto me as Ariel" (29:2), a burning altar. Humbled Jerusalem will speak "as of one that hath a familiar spirit, out of the ground" (29:4); the Hebrew ʼov is a ghost summoned by a medium (compare 1 Samuel 28), and the city is reduced to the whisper of the dead.

The sealed book in 29:11–12 is, in context, a figure for spiritual blindness: "the vision of all is become unto you as the words of a book that is sealed." The learned cannot read it because it is sealed; the unlearned cannot read at all. It is an image from a society where literacy belonged to a scribal elite.

Chapter 30 names the policy. Judah's envoys have gone "down into Egypt" (30:2), to "Zoan" (Tanis in the Delta) and "Hanes" (30:4), carrying treasure through the Negev on donkeys and camels (30:6). Egypt was then ruled by the Kushite Twenty-Fifth Dynasty, which backed revolt against Assyria (compare 2 Kings 19:9, where the Kushite Tirhakah marches against Sennacherib). The verdict in 30:7 is a pun lost in the KJV's "Their strength is to sit still": the Hebrew rahav hem shavet means roughly "Rahab, sitting still." Rahab is the chaos dragon, and Egypt is a monster that will not move. The prophet's alternative is political quietism as faith: "In returning and rest shall ye be saved; in quietness and in confidence shall be your strength: and ye would not" (30:15). And he is told to "write it before them in a table" (30:8), a dated written witness, one of the ways a prophetic book begins.

Chapter 35 and the road home

Chapter 35 is the reading's most beautiful poem and the easiest to place. It shares images and whole lines with chapters 40–55: "your God will come" (35:4) matches 40:9–10; the desert highway (35:8) matches 40:3; the watered wilderness (35:6–7) matches 41:18–19; and 35:10, "the ransomed of the LORD shall return, and come to Zion with songs," appears almost word for word at 51:11. The return in view is the return from exile.

Odil Hannes Steck's Bereitete Heimkehr argued that chapter 35 was composed as an editorial bridge joining an earlier Isaiah collection to Second Isaiah, creating a "great book of Isaiah" for the first time. H. G. M. Williamson's The Book Called Isaiah argues that the exilic prophet of 40–55 was himself steeped in the earlier material, edited it, and meant the two to be read together. Either way, chapter 35 shows how the book was built. The blind who see and the deaf who hear (35:5) reverse the prophet's commission to make ears heavy and eyes shut (6:10), and complete the promise that "the deaf [shall] hear the words of the book" (29:18). Isaiah's editors read their scroll as a story of hardening followed by healing, and they wrote chapter 35 to make that arc explicit.
