Hard Questions in the Valley of Vision: Lucifer, the Sealed Book, and the Dead Who Rise

Written by an AI persona that surfaces the hard questions honestly — not a real person.

Few Old Testament weeks generate more online anxiety per verse. Search "Lucifer Isaiah 14" or "Anthon transcript" and you will find confident claims on every side. What follows takes five of those questions in turn: the strongest form of each problem, the strongest faithful responses, and what remains unresolved.

1. Is "Lucifer" in Isaiah 14 about Satan at all?

The problem. Read Isaiah 14 from verse 4 and the subject is plain: "thou shalt take up this proverb against the king of Babylon." The poem is a taunt-song (Hebrew mashal) over a dead tyrant. The underworld stirs to meet him (14:9); the kings of the nations lie "in glory, every one in his own house," but he is "cast out of thy grave like an abominable branch" (14:18–19). Onlookers ask, "Is this the man that made the earth to tremble?" (14:16). The word is man (ha-ʼish). Verse 12's "Lucifer" does not come from the Hebrew. It comes from Jerome's Latin Vulgate, which the King James translators kept. The Hebrew is helel ben shachar, "shining one, son of dawn," which is the morning star, Venus. The Septuagint has heōsphoros, "dawn-bringer." Modern translations follow the Hebrew ("Day Star, son of Dawn," ESV).

The imagery behind the verse is older than Israel. In the Baal Cycle from Ugarit, the god Athtar, who is associated with the morning star, climbs onto Baal's empty throne. His feet do not reach the footstool, and he comes back down. Isaiah's "mount of the congregation, in the sides of the north" (14:13) uses the vocabulary of Zaphon, the Canaanite mountain of the gods. Scholars debate exactly which myth is in play (Michael Heiser, for one, argues that no revolt against the high god El is in view), but the prophet is plainly using a Canaanite story about a star that climbed too high to mock a human king. Some scholars go further and suggest that the song first mocked the Assyrian Sargon II, who was killed in battle in 705 BC. His body was never recovered, which fits "cast out of thy grave," and he had ruled as king in Babylon. On this view the song was later redirected at a Babylonian king.

The Satan reading came later. Tertullian, around AD 200, was already putting "I will be like the Most High" (14:14) into the devil's mouth (Against Marcion 5.11, 5.17). The Latin common noun then hardened into a proper name. Ironically, the same Vulgate uses lucifer in 2 Peter 1:19 for the morning star rising in believers' hearts, an image of Christ, which is why Gail Riplinger's New Age Bible Versions (1993) could accuse modern translations of blasphemy for putting "morning star" in Isaiah 14.

Why it matters for Latter-day Saints. The Book of Mormon repeats the KJV wording: "How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning!" (2 Nephi 24:12). A Latin-derived word thus appears in a text said to come from brass plates older than any Latin Bible. Doctrine and Covenants 76:26 then uses "Lucifer" as a name for the premortal rebel: "he was Lucifer, a son of the morning." The lesson asks readers to compare the king's pride with Satan's (Moses 4:1–4).

Faithful responses. The Church's footnote to 14:12 is candid: it glosses the Hebrew as "morning star, son of dawn" and explains, "The ruler of the wicked world (Babylon) is spoken of as Lucifer, the ruler of all wickedness." That is a two-layer reading: a historical king described in the language of archetypal rebellion. It does not require Isaiah to have been writing about Satan; it requires only that the tyrant's fall follows the devil's pattern, and D&C 76 can be read as a modern revelation using the name tradition had already attached to that pattern. On 2 Nephi 24, a common believing explanation is that where the plates matched the familiar biblical text, the translation used KJV English. Critics reply that this is exactly the evidence of dependence on the KJV. That argument is not settled.

2. Did the Medes really do that? Chapter 13 and the ethics of the day of wrath

The problem. Isaiah 13:15–18 is some of the most brutal writing in scripture: "Their children also shall be dashed to pieces before their eyes; their houses shall be spoiled, and their wives ravished.... they shall have no pity on the fruit of the womb; their eye shall not spare children." God is the speaker ("Behold, I will stir up the Medes against them," 13:17), and the soldiers are "my sanctified ones... my mighty ones" (13:3). Mass rape and infanticide appear as instruments of divine justice, echoing Psalm 137:9's blessing on whoever "dasheth thy little ones against the stones," also aimed at Babylon.

There is a historical problem too. When Babylon fell in 539 BC, the Nabonidus Chronicle says that the army of Cyrus "without battle... entered Babylon," and that there was no interruption of the rites in Esagil or the other temples. Babylon remained a major city under the Persians and Alexander and emptied only gradually as power moved to Seleucia. "It shall never be inhabited" (13:20) came true slowly; the massacre of 13:15–18 did not happen when the city fell.

Responses across the spectrum. One common reading treats the passage as ancient war poetry, using the stock horrors of Near Eastern siege warfare as the vocabulary of total defeat. That explains the language without endorsing it. Eric Seibert's Disturbing Divine Behavior (2009) goes further and argues that believers should separate the text's portrait of God from the God revealed in Jesus. The Latter-day Saint chapter heading reads Babylon as a type of "the world," which moves the oracle into end-time symbolism.

The Book of Mormon's version reworks the moral logic. Where the KJV says "every one that is found shall be thrust through," 2 Nephi 23:15 has "Every one that is proud shall be thrust through; yea, and every one that is joined to the wicked." Where the KJV says "for mine anger, even them that rejoice in my highness," 2 Nephi 23:3 has "for mine anger is not upon them that rejoice in my highness." It also adds a closing line at 23:22: "For I will destroy her speedily; yea, for I will be merciful unto my people, but the wicked shall perish." A believer can read these as restorations or inspired commentary; a critic sees a nineteenth-century reader smoothing the KJV, and David P. Wright's essay "Isaiah in the Book of Mormon: Or Joseph Smith in Isaiah" argues that the Book of Mormon's Isaiah variants in general are revisions responding to the English of the KJV. Either way, the children of 2 Nephi 23:16 are still "dashed to pieces."

3. When were these chapters written, and could they have been on the brass plates?

The problem. Most critical scholars date parts of this week's reading well after Isaiah son of Amoz, who was active roughly 740–700 BC. The clearest case is chapters 13–14. There Babylon, not Assyria, is the world power, and the Medes are the destroyers, which fits the mid-sixth century. Latter-day Saint writer Daniel Ellsworth states the scholarly view plainly: "Isaiah 13, 14, and 21 contain oracles against Babylon, which many scholars find anachronistic." Chapters 24–27, the "Isaiah Apocalypse," are often placed in the Persian period or later for their cosmic, end-of-world outlook. But Richard Hess, reviewing The Oxford Handbook of Isaiah, summarizes J. Todd Hibbard's chapter this way: theories about the layers of 24–27 and their dates, ranging from the eighth to the sixth century, "have achieved no consensus." Chapter 35 reads like Second Isaiah: its last verse nearly duplicates 51:11, and its desert "highway" (35:8) anticipates 40:3.

Latter-day Saints feel this problem most sharply because 2 Nephi 23–24 quotes Isaiah 13–14, and Nephi says his Isaiah came from brass plates taken from Jerusalem around 600 BC. If chapter 13 was written in the 550s, it could not have been on those plates. The same argument is made more often, and more forcefully, about Isaiah 48–49 in 1 Nephi 20–21. Wright argues the Book of Mormon's Isaiah is anachronistic twice over: late-dated text, read through a nineteenth-century lens.

Faithful responses. Several positions exist, not all compatible. First, predictive prophecy is real. Ellsworth argues that the right response is not "a scholarly turf war over authorship of discrete sections" but a Latter-day Saint model of how scripture develops, one that begins from different assumptions, including that prophets can see ahead. Second, a Babylon oracle is not out of place in the eighth century. Merodach-baladan's Babylon was courting Hezekiah in Isaiah's own lifetime (Isaiah 39). The Sargon hypothesis, if correct, places the core of chapter 14 in 705 BC. Third, some believers accept later authorship and propose other routes into the Book of Mormon, such as revelation to Nephite prophets or text supplied during translation. None of these closes the question.

4. Is 26:19 about resurrection, and whose "dead body" is it?

The problem. "Thy dead men shall live, together with my dead body shall they arise. Awake and sing, ye that dwell in dust" (26:19). The lesson uses this verse to teach Christ's resurrection. The chapter heading goes further: "Jehovah will die and be resurrected." That reading depends on the KJV, which prints "men" and "together with" in italics. The translators supplied them. The Hebrew reads roughly "your dead shall live; my corpse(s) shall rise." Ancient versions read the noun as plural, "my dead bodies." The claim that God's dead body rises with them rests largely on two supplied words.

A second question is whether the verse is about individual resurrection at all. Seven verses earlier the same chapter says flatly, "They are dead, they shall not live; they are deceased, they shall not rise" (26:14). The Hebrew words are the same ones (metim, rephaʼim, "live," "rise"), now reversed. The Yale Bible Study course on First Isaiah (John J. Collins and Joel Baden) states the majority view: "More probably, however, resurrection is being used here as a metaphor for the revival of the people, as it is in Ezekiel 37." On 25:8 ("he will swallow up death forever") the same course comments: "This does not mean that the dead will be raised, but that death will be no more." Clear individual resurrection, it notes, appears only with Daniel.

Responses. Jon Levenson's Resurrection and the Restoration of Israel (2006) argues against treating resurrection as a late foreign idea. For him, restoring the nation and raising the dead were never cleanly separate hopes, which blunts the "only a metaphor" objection. Paul, for his part, quoted 25:8 as a resurrection text: "Death is swallowed up in victory" (1 Corinthians 15:54). A believer can accept a national reading in Isaiah's setting and still hold that the metaphor points to something literal. What a careful reader should not do is build the Savior's resurrection on "together with," which is italicized for a reason.

5. Isaiah 29 and the Anthon episode: fulfillment, or a story shaped to fit the text?

The problem. In its own context, Isaiah 29 is about Jerusalem. "Ariel... the city where David dwelt" (29:1) is besieged until her voice comes "out of the ground" like a familiar spirit (29:4). The "sealed book" is a simile: "the vision of all is become unto you as the words of a book that is sealed" (29:11). Judah's leaders, learned or not, cannot understand the prophet. The "familiar spirit" (ʼob) is a medium's ghost, a practice Isaiah condemns (8:19), yet 2 Nephi 26:16 applies the image approvingly to the Nephite record.

The historical problem is sharper. In February 1828 Martin Harris took a sheet of characters from the plates to New York. According to the account in Joseph Smith's 1838 history, Charles Anthon certified the characters, tore up the certificate when he heard about an angel, and said, "I cannot read a sealed book" (JS–H 1:64–65). Anthon's own letters of 1834 and 1841 deny that he authenticated anything. He called it a hoax, and the Church's history topic page acknowledges that "Anthon denied having authenticated the characters." The critical argument turns on sequence: the small plates, including 2 Nephi 27, were translated in June 1829, more than a year after the visit. 2 Nephi 27 is far longer than Isaiah 29. It adds instructions to "take these words which are not sealed and deliver them to another, that he may show them unto the learned," and it adds "I cannot bring the book, for it is sealed" (2 Nephi 27:15–17). On this view, the expanded text was written after the event it claims to foretell, and it fits Harris's story unusually well.

Joseph's earliest account, his 1832 history, already tells the Anthon story in Isaiah's words: Harris went "to the Learned saying read this I pray thee and the learned said I cannot." It closes with "thus the Propicy of Isiaah was fulfilled." Critics argue that the memory itself was shaped by the text.

Faithful responses. Believing historians do not deny that the accounts conflict. Richard E. Bennett of BYU reconstructs Harris's visits to Bradish, Mitchill, and Anthon from both sides' sources. First, Anthon had strong motives to distance himself once the Book of Mormon was published, and his own two letters contradict each other: in 1834 he said he declined to give Harris a written opinion, and in 1841 he said he gave one "without hesitation." Second, the 1832 history says Harris went because "the Lord had shown him" he must, which could explain why the trip was framed in Isaiah's terms from the start. Third, reusing a prophecy is itself an ancient practice. Nephi says outright, "I did liken all scriptures unto us" (1 Nephi 19:23). The New Testament does the same with this chapter: Jesus applies 29:13 to the Pharisees (Matthew 15:8–9), and Paul applies 29:14 to the Corinthians (1 Corinthians 1:19). Joseph Spencer's The Vision of All treats Isaiah as the heart of Nephi's deliberate interpretive project.

What a believer cannot honestly claim is that the Anthon episode is independent evidence for the Book of Mormon. The witnesses are interested parties who contradict each other, and the "prediction" was written down afterward. What a critic cannot honestly claim is that reapplying Isaiah is automatically fraud, or Matthew and Paul are guilty too. The real disagreement is whether Nephi's expansion of Isaiah 29 is revelation or invention, and documents alone cannot settle that.

A coda: the nail that falls

The lesson lists 22:22–23 among its Messiah passages, and Revelation 3:7 applies "the key of David" to Christ. In context Eliakim is a palace steward replacing Shebna, and God will "fasten him as a nail in a sure place" (22:23). Two verses later, "the nail that is fastened in the sure place" will "be removed, and be cut down, and fall" (22:25), apparently because his relatives hung too much weight on him (22:24). The typology is old and legitimate, but the text ends with the peg giving way. Honest reading keeps both.
