"Yet Will I Trust in Him": Welcome to the Book of Job

A weekly introduction in the spirit of history-first Latter-day Saint podcasting — format inspired by Church History Matters. Written by an AI persona; not affiliated with or authored by Casey Griffiths.

Weekly introduction episode — Come Follow Me, August 10–16, 2026: Job 1–3; 12–14; 19; 21–24; 38–40; 42

Let me start with a single Hebrew word. It's a small word — two letters — and it's pronounced lo. And here's the problem: in Hebrew, there are two words pronounced exactly that way. Spell it with one letter at the end and it means "not." Spell it with a different letter and it means "in him." They sound identical. They mean opposite things.

Now open your Bible to Job 13:15, the verse this whole week's lesson is named for: "Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him." One of the most beloved lines in all of scripture. Except — the Hebrew text as written, the one the ancient scribes actually copied, has the other lo. The "not" lo. Read it that way and Job says: "Behold, he will slay me; I have no hope."

The scribes who preserved the Hebrew Bible knew about this. They left the "no hope" spelling on the page and wrote a marginal note saying, in effect, when you read this aloud, say "in him." The King James followed the margin. Modern translations like the NRSV follow the page. And the ancient rabbis — I love this — looked at both readings and declared in the Mishnah, "the matter is even." Undecided. Both are Job.

Here's why I'm telling you this in the first ninety seconds: either way, the second half of the verse doesn't change. Whether Job says "I will hope in him" or "I have no hope," he finishes the sentence the same way — "but I will maintain mine own ways before him." I'm going to argue my case to God's face. Hope or no hope, he keeps talking to God.

That, right there, is the book of Job in miniature. A book where despair and trust are spelled almost identically. A book about a man who may or may not have hope, but who absolutely will not let go of the relationship.

Welcome to the introduction episode for this week's Come Follow Me study. I'm your host, and I'll say it up front: this week is different. We're spending seven days inside what is, by nearly every measure, the hardest book in the Old Testament — the hardest Hebrew, the hardest questions, the hardest God. It's also one of the most rewarding weeks in the whole curriculum, and my job today is to get you oriented before you dive in. As always, three movements: context — what this book is and where it came from; content — a guided tour of the chapters you'll actually read; and controversies — the hard stuff, faced honestly, because Job of all books deserves honesty.

This episode sits on top of seven research essays our study team prepared for the week — two scholars, a rabbi, an honest questioner, a doctrinal connector, a practical disciple, and a walk through one BYU professor's six ways of reading a single passage. I'll draw on all of them; they're linked on this page if you want to go deeper. Let's begin.

Context: What Kind of Book Is This?

Start with a question that sounds simple: what is the book of Job?

Here's what it isn't: it isn't history writing the way Kings or Chronicles are. It opens like a folktale — "There was a man in the land of Uz" — which our academic scholar points out is essentially the Hebrew "once upon a time." No date, no genealogy, no king's reign to anchor it. A hero with suspiciously round numbers: seven sons, three daughters, seven thousand sheep. And at the end, everything doubled — exactly doubled — like the last page of a fable.

And notice where Uz is: not in Israel. Job is not an Israelite. He's from somewhere east, toward Edom or Arabia, and the book scrupulously avoids Israel's covenant story — no Abraham, no Moses, no temple, no law. That's deliberate. This book asks its question on universal ground: not "why do bad things happen to covenant Israel," but "why do bad things happen to good people — any people, anywhere."

Now, structurally, the book is really two books stitched together, and you'll feel the seam the moment you cross it. Chapters 1 and 2, and the back half of chapter 42, are simple prose — short sentences, formulaic repetition, almost liturgical. That's the frame tale, the story of the patient Job who says "the Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away." But everything in between — chapters 3 through 42, verse 6 — is dense, ferocious Hebrew poetry, and the Job of the poetry is not patient. He curses the day of his birth. He accuses God of destroying the innocent and the wicked alike. He demands a court date with the Almighty. The prose Job blesses; the poetic Job argues. When the Epistle of James praises "the patience of Job," James is remembering the frame tale. The poem tells a rougher story — our rabbi, only half joking, calls the poetic Job the least patient man in scripture.

The scholarly picture — both of our scholars land here — is that a poet of genius took up an old, well-known legend about a righteous sufferer. We know the legend was old, because Ezekiel name-drops Job alongside Noah as a proverbially righteous man. And inside that familiar frame, the poet built something radical: a debate that interrogates the very theology the frame seems to teach.

When did this happen? The setting is patriarchal — Job feels like a contemporary of Abraham — but the language tells a different story. Job's Hebrew is famously strange: over a hundred words that appear nowhere else in the Bible, thick with Aramaic influence — so difficult that the medieval rabbi Ibn Ezra wondered whether the whole book had been translated into Hebrew from another language. The scholars who study this most closely converge on the Persian period, the late sixth or fifth century before Christ. That dating matters theologically, because that's after the Babylonian exile. That's a generation asking: we had the covenant promise — obey and be blessed — and then we watched Jerusalem burn. The old equation didn't hold. Job, on this reading, is Israel's own scripture wrestling with unjust suffering in the shadow of national catastrophe.

And Job wasn't asking alone. The ancient Near East had been posing this question for centuries — there's an Akkadian poem scholars nickname "the Babylonian Job," and a Babylonian dialogue between a sufferer and a pious friend that reads eerily like Job's debate cycles. Job stands inside that international conversation — and then outgrows it. In the Babylonian texts, the sufferer is finally counseled to resign himself before inscrutable gods. In Job, the sufferer demands a hearing — and gets one. God actually shows up. That never happens in Babylon.

One more thing about the canon. In the Hebrew Bible, Job sits among the wisdom books, and you can hear the three of them as a conversation: Proverbs says the world runs on moral cause and effect — righteousness prospers, wickedness fails. Ecclesiastes says: does it, though? And Job turns that question into a courtroom drama with God as the defendant. The Bible is big enough to argue with itself, and Job is where it argues loudest.

For Latter-day Saints, Job carries some extra freight — good freight. The Lord himself invokes Job by name to Joseph Smith in Liberty Jail — "Thou art not yet as Job." Job 38:7, the morning stars singing at creation, is one of our premortal-existence anchor texts. Job 19:25 gave us the words of "I Know That My Redeemer Lives." But notice something our doctrinal connector points out: the Restoration doesn't just quote Job. It inhabits him. Doctrine and Covenants 121 and 122 are, formally, a miniature book of Job — a righteous man's anguished complaint, "O God, where art thou?", followed not by an explanation but by a divine answer that reframes everything. We'll come back to that, because it's the single most important key Latter-day Saints hold to this book.

Content: A Tour of This Week's Chapters

Alright. Let's walk the actual reading, so when you open chapter 1 you know the shape of the country.

Chapters 1 and 2: the frame tale. You meet Job — "perfect and upright," and by the way, God agrees; that's the Lord's own assessment. Then the camera cuts somewhere Job can never see: a council in heaven. The "sons of God" present themselves before the Lord, and among them comes a figure your King James Bible calls Satan — hold that name loosely; we'll deal with him in the controversies section. This adversary poses the question that powers the entire book: "Doth Job fear God for nought?" Is Job righteous because he loves God, or because righteousness pays? Take away the payment and watch what happens. And the terrible experiment begins. Four messengers, four disasters, each ending "I only am escaped alone to tell thee." Job loses his wealth, his servants, and — sit with this — all ten of his children in a single day. Then his health. His wife, watching him scrape his boils with broken pottery, says, "Curse God, and die." He won't. And then his three friends arrive — Eliphaz, Bildad, Zophar — and do the best thing anyone does in the whole book: they sit with him on the ground, seven days and seven nights, and say nothing, "for they saw that his grief was very great." Our rabbi will tell you Jewish law derives the etiquette of the house of mourning from that verse: the comforter does not speak until the mourner speaks first. The friends' finest hour is their silence. Remember that.

Chapter 3: the dam breaks. Job speaks first — and what comes out is the rawest poetry in scripture. "Let the day perish wherein I was born." Scholars notice chapter 3 is built as a kind of anti-creation: where Genesis says "Let there be light," Job says of his birthday, "Let that day be darkness" — unmaking creation, day by day, starting with his own. This is God's own certified righteous man, screaming. The book puts that scream in the canon. Don't rush past what that means.

Chapters 12 through 14: Job answers his friends. By now the friends have started explaining. Their theology is simple: God is just; suffering is punishment; therefore, Job, examine yourself. Job's reply runs on two tracks. There's biting sarcasm — "No doubt but ye are the people, and wisdom shall die with you"; he calls them "physicians of no value" whose best wisdom would be to go back to being silent. And there's a stunning legal turn: "I desire to reason with God" — the Hebrew is courtroom language; Job wants to litigate. He even warns the friends that defending God with false arguments is itself a sin: "Will ye speak wickedly for God?" File that away — it gets vindicated in chapter 42. Chapter 13 is where you'll hit "though he slay me" — you now know the textual story there. And chapter 14 holds one of the tenderest passages in the book: "If a man die, shall he live again?" Job asks the resurrection question — asks it, aches over it, imagines waiting in the grave until his "change" comes — and doesn't quite answer it.

Chapter 19: the summit. Job describes his total abandonment — kin, friends, servants, wife, even little children turn from him. And then, from the absolute bottom: I wish my words could be engraved in rock with an iron pen, forever. And then he engraves them: "For I know that my redeemer liveth, and that he shall stand at the latter day upon the earth... yet in my flesh shall I see God." If you sing "I Know That My Redeemer Lives" this Sunday, you're singing this verse. There's a rich story about what "redeemer" — go'el — meant in Job's world, and an honest conversation about the translation; both are coming in the controversies section, and one of our essays spends its entire length on this passage. For now, just mark the shape: the man with no hope, or almost no hope, stakes everything on a Vindicator he cannot see.

Chapters 21 through 24: Job points at the evidence. These chapters are Job the empiricist. The friends keep insisting the wicked always fall. Job says: look around. "Wherefore do the wicked live, become old, yea, are mighty in power?" Their kids are dancing, their houses are safe. Ask anybody who's traveled. Then chapter 24 widens the lens into one of the bleakest social-justice catalogs in the Bible — the poor dispossessed, the naked freezing against the rocks, the wounded groaning in the city — "yet God layeth not folly to them." The wounded cry, and God files no charges. Understand what Job is doing: he's not doubting God exists. He's indicting how the world is run — and he's right about the data, and the book knows it. Tucked in the middle, chapter 23 gives you two of the week's most quoted verses: "Oh that I knew where I might find him!... I go forward, but he is not there; and backward, but I cannot perceive him" — total divine silence — and then, in the same breath, "But he knoweth the way that I take: when he hath tried me, I shall come forth as gold." Notice: the gold verse lives inside the absence chapter. That's not an accident. That's the book's definition of faith.

Chapters 38 through 40: the whirlwind. And then — God answers. Out of the storm: "Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth?" What follows is widely considered the greatest nature poetry ever written: the sea swaddled like a newborn, the morning stars singing together, rain falling "on the wilderness, wherein there is no man," the wild ass who scorns the city, the war-horse who laughs at fear. Here's what you need to know going in: God never mentions the wager. Never mentions Job's suffering. Never explains anything. Instead he gives Job a tour of a cosmos that is wild, exuberant, meticulously cared for — and conspicuously not organized around human beings. Rain where no man is. Is that an answer or a dodge? Honest readers have split on that for two thousand years. But notice one detail our scholars flag: the whirlwind picks up the exact images of Job's chapter-3 curse — light, darkness, doors, the womb — and turns them inside out. The poet is answering Job's un-creation with creation. Whatever else it is, it's not a change of subject.

Chapter 42: the ending. Job responds — "I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee" — secondhand theology has become firsthand encounter. Then the prose frame returns, and two things happen that you should not miss. First, God renders a verdict, and it lands on the friends: "ye have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job hath." Read that twice. The men who defended God with tidy orthodoxy are condemned; the man who raged and demanded his day in court is the one who spoke right. Second: "the Lord turned the captivity of Job, when he prayed for his friends." His healing begins in interceding for the very people who wounded him. Then come the doubled flocks, the long life, and — curiously — ten new children. Whether that ending is a comfort or a problem — well. That's our last controversy.

One programming note: the reading skips from chapter 24 to 38, passing over a young man named Elihu, who shows up in chapter 32 and lectures everyone for six chapters. Most scholars think his speeches were added later by an early reader who felt the book needed a defense attorney — Elihu is introduced by nobody, answered by nobody, and ignored in God's verdict. You won't miss him.

Controversies: The Hard Stuff, Faced Honestly

Okay. Deep breath. Job asks dangerous questions, and it has generated a few of its own. Our honest questioner's essay works through seven; here are the five you're most likely to meet — in the text, or someday in a comment thread — steel-manned, with the faithful responses our essays offer. Fair warning, in the spirit of the book: I'm not going to tie bows on these.

First: the wager and the children. Read the prologue at face value and it's disturbing. God twice volunteers Job for scrutiny — "Hast thou considered my servant Job?" — accepts the adversary's challenge, and ten children die to settle a question about their father's motives. God even says he was moved "to destroy him without cause." Elie Wiesel, who survived Auschwitz, kept circling this book his whole life precisely because it puts God's justice itself on trial. What do we do with it? A few honest frames. One: genre. If the prologue is a folktale frame — and it walks and talks like one — then the wager is narrative scaffolding. Its literary job is to guarantee that you, the reader, know Job is innocent, so that when the friends explain his guilt for thirty chapters you know they're wrong from the first sentence. The frame sets up the poem; it doesn't teach you how heaven operates. Two: the Latter-day Saint theology of testing. Mortality itself, in our doctrine, is a proving ground God designed knowing it would contain agony — "we will prove them herewith" — and Doctrine and Covenants 122 says of a suffering prophet, "all these things shall give thee experience, and shall be for thy good." That doesn't dissolve the problem; it relocates it inside a plan we chose. And three — the frame I respect most: many faithful readers simply hold this unresolved. Notice that Job himself is never told about the wager. He suffers to the end without the explanation the reader got in chapter one. That may be the point.

Second: who is this "Satan"? In the Hebrew, he isn't Satan, capital-S name. He is ha-satan — "the adversary," "the accuser" — with a definite article, which Hebrew doesn't put on personal names. A title, not a name: a member of the divine council, a kind of roving prosecutor who reports on his patrols and can't touch Job without authorization — twice, explicitly, on a leash. The fully developed devil of later theology isn't quite on stage yet; even our own Bible Dictionary glosses the word's root as "adversary." Now, Latter-day Saints have a robust doctrine of a real, personal devil, and our chapter headings read him into Job. Is that wrong? Not necessarily — one faithful frame says later revelation clarifies who this accuser always was; his cynicism about human goodness certainly sounds familiar. Another, held by many believing scholars, says the author used the theological furniture of his own era, and the scene should be read on its ancient terms — especially since it's a literary frame, not a transcript of heaven. But here's what I find genuinely delightful, and our LDS scholar makes this point: the scene is a divine council — the sons of God assembling before the Lord. Post-Enlightenment Christianity gets embarrassed by that and dissolves it into metaphor. Restoration cosmology doesn't blink. Abraham 3 gives us a real council, a plural heavenly society, and Job 38:7's morning stars singing at creation has been a premortal-existence text for us for two centuries. In an odd way, Latter-day Saints are more at home in Job's heaven than most modern readers.

Third: the translation cruxes — 13:15 and 19:25–26. You already know the 13:15 story from the cold open: "yet will I trust in him" versus "I have no hope," one homophone apart, with the tradition itself split — and honestly, once you see it whole, it's the least threatening controversy of the week, because both readings end with Job maintaining his ways before God's face. But 19:25 needs more care, because we've built a hymn on it. "I know that my redeemer liveth... yet in my flesh shall I see God." Here's the honest picture. The Hebrew of verse 26 is among the most broken and obscure in the entire Old Testament — those italicized words in your King James, "worms" and "body," are italicized because they aren't in the Hebrew; the translators supplied them. The phrase rendered "in my flesh" can grammatically mean "from within my flesh" or "apart from my flesh" — embodied or disembodied. And "redeemer," go'el, is a technical term from Israelite family law: the kinsman-redeemer, the nearest relative obligated to buy back your land, ransom you from slavery, avenge your blood. Think Boaz in Ruth. So in context: Job has just inventoried everyone who abandoned him — every kinsman gone — and then declares that one Kinsman remains, a Vindicator who will rise, as a witness rises in court, and clear his name, even if Job is dead when it happens. Rashi read that vindicator as God himself. Most critical scholars doubt the poet had bodily resurrection in view — remember, Job left that question open in chapter 14. So was the chapter heading wrong to say Job prophesies resurrection? Here's the frame our essays converge on: it's a both/and. The near reading — a ruined man claiming God as his kinsman-in-court — is really there, and it's magnificent. And the christological reading is the Restoration doing exactly what Nephi taught: likening, hearing in an ancient cry more than the crier may have known, the same way the New Testament rereads Isaiah. The kinsman who pays what you cannot pay and treats you as family? That is precisely the office we ascribe to Christ. What honesty forbids is only pretending the resurrection reading is the plain sense of undisputed Hebrew. It isn't. What faith affirms is that the doctrine of resurrection never rested on one broken verse — it stands on many witnesses, and Job 19 joins that choir rather than carrying it solo. Our six-outlooks essay walks this single passage through six legitimate readings and shows they don't compete.

Fourth: retribution theology — the controversy inside the book. This is the big one, and unlike the others, the book itself settles it. The friends' theology — suffering implies sin, prosperity implies righteousness — is not a straw man. It's the theology of much of Deuteronomy and Proverbs, eloquently argued. And God condemns it to their faces: "ye have not spoken of me the thing that is right." Latter-day Saint scholar Michael Austin built a whole book around the uncomfortable corollary: the friends' speeches are eminently quotable, and when we quote them as devotional wisdom — and we do — we're quoting positions the text explicitly rejects. And here's where it gets personal, because the friends' sermon is alive and well. "Everything happens for a reason." "What is God trying to teach you?" "Maybe if you'd been more faithful..." Our practical disciple calls this what it is: Eliphaz with better branding — comforting to the untouched, because if sufferers earn their suffering, then I, who am careful, am safe. Job demolishes that math with evidence in chapters 21 and 24; Jesus demolishes it at the man born blind — "neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents"; and the Restoration institutionalizes the demolition: Alma's righteous people were placed in bondage anyway, and the Lord's own commentary was that he "trieth their patience and their faith" — not their guilt. And Liberty Jail — I promised we'd come back. When the Lord tells Joseph, "Thou art not yet as Job; thy friends do not contend against thee, neither charge thee with transgression," notice the assumption: the worst thing that happened to Job wasn't the Sabeans or the boils. It was the theology of his friends. Our own canon ranks the false comfort above the catastrophe. If you take one controversy home this week, take this one, because it's the one you'll actually use — at a funeral, on a ministering visit, in your own head at 2 a.m. asking "what did I do to deserve this?" Job 42:7 is God's answer to that voice too.

And fifth: the tidy ending. Doubled flocks. A hundred and forty more years. Ten new children. Critics press two objections. One: children aren't livestock; a second set doesn't "restore" the first — ten new children do not resurrect ten dead ones. Two: doesn't the happily-ever-after quietly reinstate the exact prosperity theology the poem spent thirty-nine chapters destroying? Righteous man ends up rich — Eliphaz wins after all? And a third wrinkle: Job's famous last words, "I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes," hang on one nearly untranslatable verse. The verb has no object — "myself" is supplied — and the Jewish Publication Society renders it, "I recant and relent, being but dust and ashes": not self-loathing, but a creature's acknowledgment of creatureliness. Whether Job grovels, retracts, or stands reoriented but unbowed is, textually, an open question — and verse 7 immediately confirms he had no secret sin to repent of. So what do we do with the ending? A few honest observations from our essays. The flocks double; the children don't — seven sons and three daughters, same as before — which some readers hear as the text's own quiet signal that children are not replaceable property; in a resurrection-affirming faith, the first ten are not lost. The daughters are named, praised, and given inheritance among their brothers — grace notes no folktale needed. The narrator himself unsettles the comfort, saying the family consoled Job "over all the evil that the Lord had brought upon him" — the adversary has vanished from the accounting. And the restoration turns not on vindication but on intercession: healing begins when Job prays for the friends who failed him. Our academic scholar puts it memorably: the book hands you the happy ending and dares you to be comforted by it. Maybe the truest thing to say is what the scholar Carol Newsom argues — Job deliberately stages irreconcilable voices and refuses to let any one of them have the last word. That refusal isn't a flaw. It's the design. This is a book about unanswerable suffering that declines to fake an answer, even in its own epilogue.

One pastoral word before we land. If any of this rattled you — the textual variants, the folktale frame, the ambiguous Hebrew — remember where you heard it: inside a faithful study of scripture. The rabbis argued for two thousand years about whether Job even existed, on the same page of the Talmud that calls his fear of God greater than Abraham's — and nobody's faith fell over. And the same Talmud distilled from God's verdict in chapter 42 a ruling I'd frame and hang on the wall: a person is not held responsible for what he says in his distress. The book that canonized "Why died I not from the womb?" — and then had God vindicate the asker over his orthodox critics — is not fragile in the presence of your questions. Neither is the restored gospel. Neither, this week, are you.

Where We Go From Here

So here's the road ahead. Five study sessions this week, walking the block in order.

Session one takes the frame tale and the scream — chapters 1 through 3: the council, the catastrophe, the seven days of silence, and the moment the silence breaks. Session two sits inside the debate with chapters 12 through 14 — Job versus the comforters, faithful complaint, and "If a man die, shall he live again?" Session three is chapter 19 and the Redeemer — one chapter, one summit, the full near-and-far reading of the week's most beloved verse. Session four takes chapters 21 through 24 — Job's evidence, the God who seems absent, and the gold refined in the dark. And session five rides the whirlwind home — chapters 38 through 40 and 42: the voice from the storm, the seeing that replaces hearing, and the ending you'll now know how to read with open eyes.

As you read, carry the two sentences this book leaves in your pocket. From the practical side: the friends were at their best in silence and at their worst once they started explaining — so this week, budget your words like money around anyone who's suffering. And from the theological side: God prefers honest fury addressed to him over pious falsehood spoken about him. Whatever you bring to your prayers this week — trust, no hope, or that untranslatable something in between — keep talking to him. That's the whole book. That's the "yet."
