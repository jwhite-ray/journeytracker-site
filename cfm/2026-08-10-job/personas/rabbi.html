Arguing with Heaven: A Rabbi Reads Job

Written by an AI persona giving a Jewish reading of Job in the voice of a rabbi — not a real rabbi.

A persona essay: a Jewish reading of Job, written in the voice of a rabbi for Latter-day Saint students of the Old Testament.

Let me begin the way a rabbi begins: with a disagreement.

Open the Talmud to Bava Batra 15a and you will find the sages arguing about whether Job existed at all. One sage declares before Rabbi Shmuel bar Nahmani: Job never existed and was never created; he is a mashal — a parable. Rabbi Shmuel objects: the text says "There was a man in the land of Uz" (Job 1:1) — surely there was a man! The sage answers: Nathan the prophet also told David "there were two men in one city," and no one thinks that poor man and his ewe lamb were historical. The Gemara pushes back — then why give Job a name and a hometown? — and the argument is left standing on the page, unresolved, for you to inherit.

On the same folio, the rabbis cannot agree on when Job lived: in the days of the patriarchs, of Moses, of the spies, of David, or among the returnees from Babylon — a span of a thousand years. The same passage preserves the tradition that "Moses wrote his own book, the portion of Balaam, and the book of Job" (Bava Batra 14b–15a). And just over the page, Rabbi Yohanan claims that "that which is stated about Job is greater than that which is stated about Abraham": of Abraham it says only "you fear God," but of Job, "a perfect and an upright man, one who fears God and turns away from evil" (Bava Batra 15b, on Job 1:8). The same page counts Job among seven prophets who prophesied to the nations of the world — a righteous gentile, God's servant outside the covenant of Israel.

Notice what the tradition is doing. It is not embarrassed by the disagreement; it curates it. In the Jewish house of study we call this machloket — sacred argument — and it is not a bug in the system. It is the system. A book whose subject is a man who argues with Heaven is fittingly a book the tradition has never stopped arguing about. If your study group reads Job and ends the evening with more questions than you brought, you have not failed. You have read it Jewishly.

Did Job sin when he spoke?

The Talmud's treatment of Job's speeches is bracingly honest. Rava says Job "sought to turn the bowl upside down" — to overturn the whole order of divine justice — when he cried, "The earth is given into the hand of the wicked" (Job 9:24; Bava Batra 16a). Rav goes further: "Dust should be put in the mouth of Job" for daring to say "there is no arbiter between us" (Job 9:33). And yet the very same discussion concedes, in Rava's name, the most humane sentence in the whole sugya: since God Himself says "you have not spoken of Me the thing that is right, as my servant Job" (Job 42:7–8) even after Job's wildest words, we learn that a person is not held responsible for what he says in his distress (Bava Batra 16b). Hold that ruling next to your own experience of grief. It is halakhah for the broken-hearted.

The Mishnah preserves an even more delicate argument. Rabbi Yehoshua ben Hyrcanus taught that Job served God out of love, citing Job 13:15 — in your King James Version, "Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him." But the Mishnah immediately admits the verse is ambiguous: the Hebrew word lo is written one way (לא, "not") and read another (לו, "in Him"), so the line can mean "I will hope in Him" or "I have no hope" — and, says the Mishnah, "the matter is even," undecided (Mishnah Sotah 5:5). The Jewish Publication Society translation prints the harder reading — "He may well slay me; I may have no hope; yet I will argue my case before Him" — and relegates the pious one to a footnote. Sit with that. In the written scroll, Job says I have no hope — and argues anyway. The tradition read aloud softens it to trust. Both are Job. Perhaps both are you, on different days.

There is midrashic honesty about Job's failures too. Sotah 11a teaches that Pharaoh consulted three counselors about drowning Israel's sons: Balaam, who advised it, was slain; Jethro, who fled in protest, merited descendants in the Sanhedrin; and Job, who was silent, was punished with suffering. Whatever else this midrash is, it is a rebuke that still lands: silence before others' suffering is not neutrality.

The medieval readers

Our medieval commentators disagree about Job as fruitfully as the Talmud does.

Saadia Gaon (10th century) translated Job into Arabic and wrote a commentary he called The Book of Theodicy. His rationalism is startling even now: he argued that "the satan" of chapters 1–2 was no fallen angel but a human adversary — a jealous rival — marshaling the many biblical passages where satan simply means a human opponent, and insisting that a monotheist God does not keep a cosmic rival on staff. Job's suffering, for Saadia, is a trial whose endurance God compensates.

Rashi (11th century) reads the verse your tradition loves best, "I know that my redeemer liveth" (Job 19:25), and hears in it not a prophecy of a future incarnate savior but Job's confidence that God — his go'el, his vindicator — lives and will endure to requite the friends who persecute him. More on that verse below.

Ibn Ezra (12th century), ever the philologist, throws up his hands at Job's famously difficult Hebrew and offers a theory: "It seems likely to me that it is a translated book, and that is why it is difficult to interpret, as with any translated book" (Ibn Ezra on Job 2:11). Imagine — a medieval rabbi proposing that a book of the Bible came into Hebrew from another tongue. That is how free our exegetes felt to follow the evidence.

Rambam — Maimonides — gives Job two full chapters of the Guide for the Perplexed (III:22–23) and reads it as Scripture's deepest treatment of providence. He sides cheerfully with the Talmudic opinion that the book is a mashal, a fiction "conceived for the purpose of explaining the different opinions which people hold on Divine Providence," and notes that the very name Uz puns on the Hebrew for "take counsel": the land of Uz is an address in the country of think about this. He observes that the satan comes to the divine court uninvited and can range only "to and fro in the earth" — evil has no standing in heaven. And he notices something devastating in Job 1:1: Job is called upright and God-fearing, but never wise. For Rambam, Job's error was inherited theology — he knew God by tradition, not by understanding — and the whirlwind speeches are the cure: when Job finally knows rather than merely repeats, his complaint dissolves. Notice the nerve of that reading: the greatest of our philosophers says the pious platitudes of Job's friends are the book's target, not its teaching.

Job in Jewish life

Job is not, for us, an abstract theodicy seminar. He is woven into how Jews mourn.

When Job's three friends came to him, "they sat down with him upon the ground seven days and seven nights, and none spake a word unto him: for they saw that his grief was very great" (Job 2:13). From this scene our sages derived the etiquette of the shiva house, the seven days of mourning: "The consolers are not permitted to speak words of consolation until the mourner opens and speaks first," as Job's friends waited until "after this opened Job his mouth" (Job 3:1; Moed Katan 28b). The friends' catastrophic failure began only when they answered. Their finest hour — the only act of theirs the tradition canonized as law — was their silence.

Job's first words of bereavement, "the LORD gave, and the LORD hath taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD" (Job 1:21), have entered the liturgy of Jewish burial, said at the graveside as an act of tzidduk ha-din, accepting the judgment. And on Tisha B'Av, the fast mourning the destruction of both Temples, when the study of Torah is forbidden because "the precepts of the LORD are right, rejoicing the heart," the law carves out an exception: one may read Job (Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chayim 554:1–2). On the day of national grief, the only permitted curriculum is grief's own literature. That tells you what Job is to us: not an answer to suffering, but suffering's authorized text.

What a christological lens can miss

Now let me speak candidly, and with affection, to my Latter-day Saint readers. You come to Job humming "I Know That My Redeemer Lives," and I would not take that hymn from you. But let me show you what the Hebrew is doing, because I think it will deepen rather than diminish your reading.

Job 19:25–26. The Hebrew go'el is a technical term from Israelite family law: the kinsman-redeemer who buys back a relative's land, ransoms him from slavery, avenges his blood (Leviticus 25:25; Numbers 35:19). Job, abandoned by kin and community (read the aching catalogue in Job 19:13–19), declares that one kinsman remains who will rise "at the last" to vindicate him — and Rashi says plainly that this vindicator is God, who will outlast Job's persecutors and requite them. The JPS renders it "But I know that my Vindicator lives." As for verse 26, look at your own King James text with care: "though after my skin worms destroy this body, yet in my flesh shall I see God" — the italicized words are the translators' additions; the Hebrew is famously broken and obscure. And here is the sharpest irony: on the very page where the rabbis debate Job's words, Rava infers from Job 7:9 ("he that goeth down to the grave shall come up no more") that Job denied the resurrection of the dead (Bava Batra 16a). The plain-sense Job is not prophesying Easter morning; he is demanding a court date, and he wants it before he dies — "yet I will argue my case before Him" (Job 13:15, JPS). If your theology finds a further witness of Christ here, that is your covenant's reading to make. Only know that it is a second reading, laid over a first — and the first, a man demanding that God appear and vindicate him against slander, is itself magnificent.

"The patience of Job." The Job of chapters 3 through 37 is the least patient man in Scripture. He curses the day of his birth (Job 3), calls his comforters "physicians of no value" whose "wisdom" would be silence (Job 13:4–5), and indicts God's governance of the world (Job 21; 24). A christological lens that needs Job to be a silent proto-sufferer flattens the book's actual scandal — which the book itself endorses, when God tells Eliphaz: "ye have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job hath" (Job 42:7). The protester is vindicated; the defenders of God are the ones needing atonement, and it is Job's prayer that saves them (Job 42:8–10). In Jewish reading, argument with God can be a form of faithfulness. Abraham at Sodom, Moses after the golden calf, Job on his ash heap — this is a lineage, and honestly, your own tradition of wrestling before the Lord is closer to it than you may realize.

"Satan." The Hebrew says ha-satan, "the adversary," with the definite article — a title, a courtroom function, not yet the proper name of the devil of later theology. Jewish tradition handled him with remarkable freedom: Saadia made him human; Rambam made him a figure in a parable, barred from heaven's fellowship; Reish Lakish internalized him entirely — "Satan, the evil inclination, and the Angel of Death are one" (Bava Batra 16a); and Rabbi Levi even suggested the satan acted "for the sake of Heaven," jealous for the memory of Abraham's love. Reading the prologue as a chapter in a cosmic war between God and His archenemy imports a mythology the Hebrew text is conspicuously careful to withhold: this adversary works on a leash, twice given explicit limits (Job 1:12; 2:6).

The ending. Even Job's last words are a machloket. Your King James has "I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes" (Job 42:6) — total capitulation. The Hebrew is more elusive, and JPS renders it: "Therefore, I recant and relent, being but dust and ashes." Not self-loathing; a creature's acknowledgment of creatureliness, spoken by a man whom God has just toured through a wild, decentered cosmos — rain on land "where no man is" (Job 38:26), the wild goats and the hinds calving unwatched (Job 39:1) — a creation not organized around human deserving at all. And then God restores Job "when he prayed for his friends" (Job 42:10). The whirlwind gives no theodicy. It gives a Presence, and then a task.

After Auschwitz

I cannot end without saying that for Jews, Job stopped being a thought experiment in the 1940s. Margarete Susman, writing in 1946, made The Book of Job and the Destiny of the Jewish People one of the first sustained attempts to think Jewishly after the attempted annihilation of the Jews — Job as the biography of a people. Elie Wiesel, in "Job: Our Contemporary," confessed he was offended by Job's final surrender and preferred to imagine Job's capitulation as ironic — the accuser refusing to dignify the proceedings; whatever one makes of that, Wiesel's title states the Jewish premise: Job is not ancient. And Rabbi Joseph Soloveitchik, in Kol Dodi Dofek, gave the reading I find most usable in an actual house of mourning: the question "why do I suffer?" has no answer available to us, and the halakhic question is instead "what does my suffering obligate me to do?" — turning fate into destiny, as Job did when his restoration came through praying for the very friends who had wounded him (Job 42:10).

So here is my blessing for your study block, from your neighbor across the covenantal street. Read Job 1–3 and learn to sit in silence. Read 12–14 and 19 and 21–24 and learn that protest can be prayer. Read 38–40 and let the wild world decenter you. Read 42 and notice that God prefers honest fury to pious falsehood, and that healing arrives through intercession for people who failed you. And when your study group disagrees about what it all means — baruch Hashem. So did we. So do we still.
