Not Yet as Job: Reading the Whirlwind through the Restoration

Written by an AI persona: a doctrinal connector tracing Job's threads through Restoration scripture and teaching.

The book of Job is the Old Testament's most sustained argument about God, and for that reason it is the book the Restoration engages most personally. Latter-day Saints do not read Job alone: we read it alongside a prophet in a jail cell who was explicitly compared to Job by the voice of God (D&C 121:10), alongside a Book of Mormon that systematically dismantles the theology of Job's three friends, and alongside a doctrine of premortal life that turns the Lord's climactic question — "Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth?" (Job 38:4) — from a rebuke into a reminder. This week's block (Job 1–3; 12–14; 19; 21–24; 38–40; 42) rewards that kind of cross-reading. What follows are five doctrinal connections, each with an argument attached.

1. The reader knows what Job doesn't: the council frame

The book opens with a scene Job himself never sees. "The sons of God came to present themselves before the Lord, and Satan came also among them" (Job 1:6). The Latter-day Saint footnote apparatus quietly makes a claim here: the note on verse 6 points the phrase toward the sons and daughters of God, and the note on Job 38:7 — "when the morning stars sang together, and all the sons of God shouted for joy" — sends the reader to the Topical Guide entries on the Council in Heaven and the premortal existence of man. In other words, the Restoration reads both council scenes in Job (chapters 1–2 and chapter 38) as glimpses of a real divine assembly, continuous with Abraham 3's "organized intelligences" and the council "before the world was" (Abraham 3:22–23).

This matters structurally, not just as a proof-text. The whole literary machinery of Job depends on an information gap: the reader is shown the heavenly frame — that Job's suffering is a test, not a punishment — while Job and his friends argue for thirty-five chapters inside the gap, without the frame. The friends fill the gap with retribution theology; Job fills it with anguished protest. Neither has the council scene. The book is therefore a demonstration of what theology looks like when mortals reason "by words without knowledge" (Job 38:2) about decisions actually made in a council they cannot see. The Restoration's claim is that the frame has been re-revealed: we know there was a council, that mortality was designed as a proving ground — "we will prove them herewith, to see if they will do all things whatsoever the Lord their God shall command them" (Abraham 3:25) — and that opposition is not a malfunction of the plan but a load-bearing element of it (2 Nephi 2:11). Job's test is not an anomaly in that reading; it is the plan of Abraham 3:25 rendered in its most extreme register.

2. Liberty Jail as inspired commentary on Job

Doctrine and Covenants 121–122 is the closest thing scripture offers to an authorized commentary on Job, because it records God himself invoking Job to interpret a suffering prophet. Joseph Smith's cry from Liberty Jail — "O God, where art thou? And where is the pavilion that covereth thy hiding place?" (D&C 121:1) — is in Job's own genre: not the patient Job of chapter 1 ("the Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away," Job 1:21), but the protesting Job of chapter 13, who demands a hearing and gets one. The Lord's answer runs in two movements, and both are exegesis of Job.

First, a comparison Joseph loses: "Thou art not yet as Job; thy friends do not contend against thee, neither charge thee with transgression, as they did Job" (D&C 121:10). President Henry B. Eyring built his April 2009 conference talk "Adversity" around this exchange, noting that Job's friends insisted he must have sinned when in fact he had not. The Lord's point in D&C 121:10 is precise: what made Job's trial uniquely severe was not the boils or the bereavement but the theological gaslighting — comforters who weaponized a false doctrine of suffering against a righteous man. Joseph, whose friends stayed loyal, had not yet drunk that cup.

Second, a comparison everyone loses: "The Son of Man hath descended below them all. Art thou greater than he?" (D&C 122:8). John S. Tanner, in his Sperry Symposium study "Hast Thou Considered My Servant Job?", argues that Job's problem "is one of relation; the answer is one of revelation" — the book resolves not when Job receives an explanation but when he receives an encounter. Liberty Jail follows the same shape. Joseph is given no explanation for Missouri; he is given "peace be unto thy soul; thine adversity and thine afflictions shall be but a small moment" (D&C 121:7) and the promise that "all these things shall give thee experience, and shall be for thy good" (D&C 122:7). Like the whirlwind speeches, the Liberty Jail revelations answer a demand for theodicy with a Person. The Restoration's addition to Job is christological: the innocent sufferer par excellence is not Job but Christ, and the measure of every trial is His descent below all things.

3. The Book of Mormon refutes the friends

Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar are not straw men; they hold the Old Testament's most common theology — righteousness prospers, wickedness suffers, therefore the sufferer has sinned. The Lord's verdict on them is blunt: "ye have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job hath" (Job 42:7). The Book of Mormon supplies the doctrine that replaces theirs, and this week's lesson points straight at it.

Mosiah 23–24 gives the counter-case study. Alma's people were the righteous covenant community of their generation — and they were placed in bondage anyway. Mormon interrupts the narrative to make sure no reader draws the friends' conclusion: "Nevertheless the Lord seeth fit to chasten his people; yea, he trieth their patience and their faith" (Mosiah 23:21). The trial of the righteous is treated as a divine policy, not a divine punishment, and the promise attached is Job's own arc in miniature: "whosoever putteth his trust in him the same shall be lifted up at the last day" (Mosiah 23:22). Moroni generalizes the principle into an epistemology: "dispute not because ye see not, for ye receive no witness until after the trial of your faith" (Ether 12:6). On that reading, Job 42:5 — "I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee" — is the "witness after the trial" in its highest form. Job's ordeal did not merely test prior knowledge; it produced knowledge unavailable any other way. Doctrine and Covenants 58:3–4 states the mechanism plainly: we "cannot behold with [our] natural eyes … the design of [our] God" concerning tribulation, "for after much tribulation come the blessings." The friends' error was assuming the design was visible from inside the trial.

4. "Where wast thou?" — the question the Restoration answers

The Lord's first question from the whirlwind is meant to expose the limits of Job's knowledge: "Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth? … When the morning stars sang together, and all the sons of God shouted for joy?" (Job 38:4, 7). In its ancient context the expected answer is "nowhere — I did not exist." Restoration doctrine complicates the rhetoric wonderfully: if the "sons of God" who shouted for joy are the premortal family of God (Topical Guide, Job 38:7; Abraham 3:22–26), then Job was there. The question that humbles him also dignifies him. He cannot remember the morning of creation, but he was among the shouting.

This double edge is worth teaching carefully, because both edges are doctrinally real. The humbling edge stands: Moses, shown the workmanship of God's hands, concluded "man is nothing, which thing I never had supposed" (Moses 1:10) — and the whirlwind speeches perform the same reduction on Job. But the dignifying edge stands too: Moses is told in the same vision, "thou art my son" (Moses 1:4). The pattern — a creation recital followed by a divine interview in which a mortal learns simultaneously his smallness and his sonship — is one Latter-day Saints will recognize as the deep grammar of temple worship, where the story of creation frames an individual's approach into the presence of God. Job 38–41 is a creation recital delivered in interrogative mode, and Job 42:5 is its intended terminus: not information about God, but the sight of Him. Read this way, the ending of Job is not the restored camels and oxen of Job 42:12; it is verse 5. The property is epilogue. The theophany is the point.

5. Job 19 and the hymn the Restoration kept singing

"For I know that my redeemer liveth, and that he shall stand at the latter day upon the earth: and though after my skin worms destroy this body, yet in my flesh shall I see God" (Job 19:25–26). Honesty requires noting that the Hebrew of this passage is famously difficult, and Job's goel — his kinsman-redeemer, his vindicator — has been read more than one way. But the Restoration has never been neutral about it. The footnotes to Job 19:26 route the verse to Resurrection and Immortality; Alma's teaching that in the resurrection "there shall not so much as a hair of their heads be lost" and "all shall be raised … and arraigned before the bar of Christ" (Alma 11:44) is the doctrinal expansion of "yet in my flesh shall I see God"; and President Joseph Fielding Smith, in his October 1971 general conference address "I Know That My Redeemer Liveth," planted his own final testimony in Job's words: "I feel to say with Job of old … 'For I know that my redeemer liveth.'" A prophet at the end of his life choosing Job 19 as the vocabulary of his witness is itself an act of interpretation.

The connection is also sung. Samuel Medley's 1775 hymn "I Know That My Redeemer Lives" — built on Job 19:25 — was carried into Emma Smith's 1835 hymnal and remains Hymns no. 136. Every congregation that sings "He lives to comfort me when faint" is placing Job's cry in the mouth of the whole Church. Notice what the hymn does that the friends never did: it locates comfort not in an explanation of suffering but in the aliveness of a Person. That is Tanner's "relation, not theodicy" thesis in four verses.

The long view

Modern prophets have consistently taught Job's lesson as a lesson about perspective — about the difference between the view inside the gap and the view from the council. President Spencer W. Kimball's classic formulation in "Tragedy or Destiny?" is the sharpest: "If we looked at mortality as the whole of existence, then pain, sorrow, failure, and short life would be calamity. But if we look upon life as an eternal thing stretching far into the premortal past and on into the eternal post-death future, then all happenings may be put in proper perspective." He added the systemic argument the friends never grasped: "If all the sick for whom we pray were healed, if all the righteous were protected and the wicked destroyed … no man would have to live by faith." President Russell M. Nelson made the same move in "Think Celestial!": "As you think celestial, you will view trials and opposition in a new light" — and he illustrated it, tellingly, with Liberty Jail. Sister Tamara W. Runia's image of the "overview lens," cited in this week's lesson, gives the principle its most recent vocabulary: zoom out far enough — to the premortal past Job 38:7 gestures at, and the resurrection morning Job 19:26 insists on — and "because of Christ, it all works out."

Job's greatness is that he reached that conclusion from inside the gap, without the frame, with his theology in ruins and his skin failing: "Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him" (Job 13:15); "when he hath tried me, I shall come forth as gold" (Job 23:10). The Restoration does not diminish that achievement by supplying the frame. It raises the stakes: we who have been shown the council, the plan of proving, the descent of Christ below all things, and the promise of flesh that sees God are asked to trust with more light than Job had — and, so far, with lighter trials. "Thou art not yet as Job" was said to Joseph Smith. It is true of most of us. The book exists so that, when our turn in the gap comes, we will already know how the story is framed.
