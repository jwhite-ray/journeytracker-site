The Book That Argues Back: Reading Job Without the Cushions

Written by an AI persona: a rigorous academic reading through a Latter-day Saint paradigm.

The title of this week's Come Follow Me lesson — "Yet Will I Trust in Him" — rests on one of the most contested half-verses in the Hebrew Bible. The King James Version of Job 13:15 reads, "Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him." But the Hebrew manuscript tradition preserves two readings at exactly this point. The consonantal text as written (the ketiv) has lo spelled with an aleph — "not": "Behold, he will slay me; I have no hope." The marginal reading the Masoretes instructed to be pronounced (the qere) has lo spelled with a vav — "in him": "in him will I hope." The two words sound identical and mean opposite things. The KJV followed the qere; the NRSV follows the ketiv ("See, he will kill me; I have no hope"), relegating the familiar reading to a footnote. Either way, the second half of the verse is not in dispute: "but I will maintain mine own ways before him" (13:15). Job intends to argue his case to God's face.

That a lesson title can hang on a single ambiguous particle is, in miniature, the situation of the whole book. Job is the Old Testament's most textually difficult, most theologically daring, and most frequently domesticated work. This essay lays out what current scholarship actually says about it — its genre, date, language, structure, and ancient Near Eastern context — and then asks what a Latter-day Saint framework does with a book like this. The answer, I will suggest, is: more than you might expect, and less comfortably than the Sunday School version.

What Kind of Book Is This?

Job is a composite structure, visible even in English. Chapters 1–2 and 42:7–17 are folktale prose: stylized, repetitive, almost liturgical ("the LORD gave, and the LORD hath taken away," 1:21). Everything between — chapters 3 through 42:6 — is dense, difficult poetry. The prose Job is patient; the poetic Job is not. He curses the day of his birth (3:3), accuses God of destroying "the perfect and the wicked" alike (9:22), and demands litigation: "I desire to reason with God" (13:3). The Epistle of James remembers only "the patience of Job" (James 5:11), which tells us the frame tale dominated the book's early reception; the poem tells a rougher story.

Most scholars hold that the poet took up an older, well-known legend — Ezekiel lists Job alongside Noah and Daniel as proverbial righteous men (Ezekiel 14:14), evidence that the figure of Job circulated independently and early — and built inside its frame a radical dialogue that interrogates the very theology the frame seems to teach. Carol Newsom's influential study, The Book of Job: A Contest of Moral Imaginations (Oxford University Press, 2003), reads the book as deliberately polyphonic in Bakhtin's sense: each genre in the book — didactic tale, wisdom dialogue, lament, hymn, theophany — embodies a distinct way of perceiving moral reality, and the book refuses to let any single voice finalize the truth. On this reading, the "contradictions" in Job are not editorial accidents to be harmonized but the book's method.

The language points to a late composition even if the setting is patriarchal. The Hebrew of Job is famously strange — thick with Aramaisms, rare vocabulary, and words found nowhere else — and Edward Greenstein, whose Job: A New Translation (Yale University Press, 2019) is the most philologically aggressive recent treatment, places the poet around the late sixth or early fifth century BCE, in the exilic or early postexilic period. That dating matters theologically: the book would then be wrestling with unjust suffering in the shadow of national catastrophe, not merely individual misfortune. Note, too, that Job is not an Israelite. He is from "the land of Uz" (1:1) — somewhere east, toward Edom or Arabia — and the poem scrupulously avoids Israel's covenant history. This is theodicy conducted on universal ground, on purpose.

Nor is the problem uniquely Israelite. Mesopotamian literature had been posing it for centuries. Ludlul bēl nēmeqi ("Let me praise the Lord of Wisdom"), often called "the Babylonian Job," recounts a righteous official's inexplicable suffering and eventual restoration by Marduk; the Babylonian Theodicy is an acrostic dialogue between a sufferer and a pious friend. Both are edited in W. G. Lambert's standard collection, Babylonian Wisdom Literature. Job stands in this tradition and outgrows it: the Mesopotamian texts counsel resignation before inscrutable gods, while Job insists on a hearing — and gets one.

One structural observation with direct relevance to this week's reading: the Come Follow Me selection (Job 1–3; 12–14; 19; 21–24; 38–40; 42) skips the speeches of Elihu (chapters 32–37) entirely. Whether by design or not, the curriculum thereby reproduces a long-standing critical judgment: Elihu is announced by no one, answered by no one, absent from the epilogue's verdict, and written in a distinct style, and many scholars since the nineteenth century have regarded his speeches as a later insertion by a pious reader dissatisfied with the book's audacity. The manual's readers will never miss him.

The Satan, the Council, and Job 38:7

The figure who sets the plot in motion in chapters 1–2 is not, in the Hebrew, "Satan" as a proper name. He is ha-satan — "the adversary," "the accuser" — with the definite article, which Hebrew does not attach to personal names. As Robert Alter's notes in The Wisdom Books: Job, Proverbs, and Ecclesiastes (W. W. Norton, 2010) make clear, the scene is a divine council: the "sons of God" (bene ha-elohim) present themselves before the LORD (1:6), and the satan functions as a kind of roving prosecutor testing loyalty claims. The developed doctrine of a personal devil belongs to later strata of Jewish and Christian thought; the LDS Bible Dictionary itself glosses the term's root meaning as adversary.

Here the scholarship and the Restoration make interesting contact. Latter-day Saint theology is unembarrassed by a divine council — it is native to our cosmology (Abraham 3:22–28) — and Job 38:7, "when the morning stars sang together, and all the sons of God shouted for joy," has long served Latter-day Saints as a premortal-existence proof-text. The critical reading of 38:7 (divine beings acclaiming creation, as in Ugaritic and Mesopotamian cosmologies) and the LDS reading (premortal spirits rejoicing) are not the same claim, and honesty requires saying so. But they share something most modern Protestant readings lack: both take the verse's plural heavenly society literally rather than dissolving it into metaphor. The Restoration paradigm is, oddly, more at home in Job's Iron Age heaven than post-Enlightenment monotheism is.

The Friends, Retribution, and Doctrine and Covenants 121

The friends' theology is simple and recognizable: suffering implies sin. Eliphaz eventually says it outright — "Is not thy wickedness great?" (22:5) — inventing crimes Job never committed. Job's counterargument in chapter 21 is empirical: "Wherefore do the wicked live, become old, yea, are mighty in power?" (21:7). Chapter 24 extends it into one of the Bible's bleakest catalogs of social injustice — the poor dispossessed, the naked freezing, the wounded groaning — ending with the devastating line, "yet God layeth not folly to them" (24:12). The friends defend the ledger; Job reports the data.

The book's own verdict is delivered twice. Within the poem, God addresses Job, not the friends. In the epilogue, God says to Eliphaz: "ye have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job hath" (42:7). The protester is vindicated; the orthodox comforters must ask the protester to intercede for them (42:8). Michael Austin, a Latter-day Saint literary scholar, builds his book Re-reading Job: Understanding the Ancient World's Greatest Poem (Greg Kofford Books, 2014) around precisely this point: when we quote the friends' speeches as devotional wisdom — and we often do; they are eminently quotable — we are quoting positions the text explicitly condemns. Austin's book won the Association for Mormon Letters award for religious nonfiction and is the most sustained LDS scholarly engagement with Job in print.

Restoration scripture has already institutionalized this reading, arguably before LDS scholarship caught up to it. In Liberty Jail, Joseph Smith opens with a lament that is pure Joban idiom — "O God, where art thou?" (D&C 121:1; compare Job 23:3, 8–9, "Oh that I knew where I might find him!... Behold, I go forward, but he is not there") — and the divine answer does not rebuke the lament. It absorbs it, reframes it ("all these things shall give thee experience," D&C 122:7), and explicitly invokes Job as the measuring case: "Thou art not yet as Job; thy friends do not contend against thee, neither charge thee with transgression, as they did Job" (D&C 121:10). Notice what that verse assumes: the worst thing that happened to Job was not the Sabeans or the boils but the theology of his friends. Sections 121–122 are, formally, the Doctrine and Covenants' own Job cycle — complaint, theophany, reorientation without explanation — and they canonize the legitimacy of the complaint.

"I Know That My Redeemer Liveth": Philology and Reception

Job 19:25–27 is the summit of the book for Christian readers, fixed in that role by Handel and by the LDS chapter headnote, which states that Job "prophesies that he will be resurrected." The scholarly picture is more complicated, and worth stating plainly. The Hebrew of 19:26 is notoriously obscure — translators disagree even on whether Job sees God "in" or "apart from" his flesh — and the word rendered "redeemer," go'el, is a legal term from Israelite kinship law: the nearest male relative obligated to buy back a kinsman's land, redeem him from slavery, or avenge his blood (as in Ruth 3–4). In context, Job — who has just wished his words "graven with an iron pen and lead in the rock for ever" (19:24) — is expressing certainty that a vindicator will arise to argue his case, "at the latter day upon the earth" (19:25), even if Job himself has died. Most critical commentators, Alter and Greenstein among them, doubt the poet had a doctrine of bodily resurrection in view; Job elsewhere seems to deny one ("man lieth down, and riseth not," 14:12) even while reaching toward hope beyond death ("If a man die, shall he live again? all the days of my appointed time will I wait, till my change come," 14:14).

A Latter-day Saint paradigm can hold both registers without forcing either. Philologically, the go'el is a legal vindicator; theologically, the kinsman-redeemer is exactly the office Latter-day Saints ascribe to Christ — a next-of-kin who pays what the debtor cannot. Nephi's principle of "likening" (1 Nephi 19:23) is a reception hermeneutic, not a historical claim: it authorizes reading Job 19:25 christologically as appropriation without requiring us to assert that a fifth-century poet consciously prophesied the resurrection. The verse's afterlife — including Job's wish that his words be permanently inscribed, ironically fulfilled beyond his imagining — is itself part of the datum.

The Whirlwind and 42:6: What Did Job Actually Concede?

The divine speeches (chapters 38–41) answer none of Job's legal questions. Instead they conduct a tour of a cosmos in which humanity is conspicuously not the center: rain falls "on the wilderness, wherein there is no man" (38:26), the wild ass scorns the city (39:7), the war-horse laughs at fear (39:22). Alter argues the poetry here deliberately surpasses everything the human speakers have said — creation rendered "beautiful and incendiary" — as though the argument is carried by aesthetic overwhelm rather than proposition. Job's first response is silence: "Behold, I am vile... I will lay mine hand upon my mouth" (40:4).

His second response, 42:6, is the book's last textual trapdoor. The KJV reads, "Wherefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes." But the Hebrew verb "abhor" has no stated object ("myself" is supplied), and the syntax admits several renderings: "I retract and am comforted concerning dust and ashes," or Greenstein's provocative "I am fed up; I take pity on 'dust and ashes'" — reading "dust and ashes" as the debased human condition (compare 30:19) and Job as unbowed to the end. One need not accept Greenstein's maximal reading to accept his warning: the traditional translation smuggles a groveling penitence into a verse that may describe something closer to reorientation. What the text plainly gives us is 42:5 — "I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee" — the movement from secondhand theology to firsthand encounter. That, and not a confession of secret sin, is the book's resolution; 42:7 immediately confirms that Job had no such sin to confess.

The Restoration and the Retirement of the Ledger

Where does this leave a Latter-day Saint studier? Three observations.

First, LDS institutional engagement with critical Job scholarship is real, if thin. Beyond Austin's monograph, John S. Tanner — BYU English professor and later academic vice president — published "Hast Thou Considered My Servant Job?" (Ensign, December 1990; reprinted in Sperry Symposium Classics: The Old Testament, BYU Religious Studies Center), which already warned Latter-day Saint readers against the friends' error of "misjudging the reasons for affliction." The Maxwell Institute's podcast has hosted Mark Larrimore, author of The Book of Job: A Biography (Princeton University Press, 2013), on the book's reception history — including his argument that the proverbial "patience of Job" is overrated — and BYU biologist-writer Steven Peck on Job, creation, and the de-centering wildness of the divine speeches. The conversation between Restoration faith and Job criticism is happening; it is simply not happening in the manual.

Second, Restoration theology has structural advantages here. Classical theism must explain why an omnipotent creator ex nihilo built a world containing innocent suffering; every element of the design is his. Latter-day Saint cosmology — uncreated intelligence, matter God organizes rather than conjures, law that conditions even divine action, and a mortality designed as testing ground with an "opposition in all things" (2 Nephi 2:11) — redistributes the problem. It does not dissolve it (the whirlwind speeches remain necessary reading precisely because no theodicy fully closes), but it means a Latter-day Saint can read Job's protest as data about a genuinely open, risk-bearing creation rather than as a puzzle to be explained away.

Third, and most practically: the book's canonical function, on both the critical and the Restoration reading, is to retire the ledger theology the friends represent. The manual's own selections make this possible — chapters 21 and 24 are included, and they are Job's empirical demolition of the prosperity calculus. "When he hath tried me, I shall come forth as gold" (23:10) is a magnificent verse, but it sits inside a chapter of divine absence (23:8–9); read whole, chapter 23 models faith as the persistence of the search, not the possession of the answer. That is the version of trust the ketiv and qere of 13:15 jointly teach: whether Job says "I have no hope" or "in him will I hope," the second clause stands — he will maintain his ways before God's face. The Lord of chapter 42, like the Lord of Liberty Jail, calls that speaking "the thing that is right."
