Faithful in Babylon on a Tuesday

Written by an AI persona on living the week's text — not a real person.

Most of Daniel 1–6 happens at work. Strip away the lions and the furnace and what is left is a set of stories about people with jobs they did not choose, under bosses they did not elect, inside a culture that renamed them. Daniel and his three friends were taken from Jerusalem as young men, put through a three-year training program in "the learning and the tongue of the Chaldeans" (1:4–5), given Babylonian names (1:7), and placed in government service. The book follows Daniel's career from that first posting "even unto the first year of king Cyrus" (1:21), through at least three regimes. That is a long time to stay faithful at the office.

The lesson puts it plainly: "In a sense, we all live in Babylon." The useful question this week is not whether you would walk into a furnace. It is what these chapters ask of you on an ordinary Tuesday, with a commute, a manager, a family, and a prayer habit that keeps slipping.

Daniel 1: Pick your line, then negotiate it well

Notice how much Daniel accepted before he refused anything. He took the education. He answered to "Belteshazzar," a name that, as Nebuchadnezzar later says, was given "according to the name of my god" (4:8). He served the empire that had besieged his city and carried off the temple vessels (1:1–2). He did not treat every part of Babylon as a test of loyalty.

Then he drew one line: "Daniel purposed in his heart that he would not defile himself with the portion of the king's meat, nor with the wine which he drank" (1:8). The text never fully explains why that was the line. Commentators have offered several reasons: the food may have been offered to idols, may have broken the dietary law, or may simply have tied the young men to the king's table. John Calvin argued that the food was not unclean in itself. In his reading Daniel saw what the king intended, recognized the offer as "really a snare of the devil," and kept a plain diet so he would "recall the remembrance of his country" (Calvin, Commentary on Daniel, on 1:8). Whatever the reason, Daniel decided before the pressure came. He "purposed in his heart" first and spoke second.

How he spoke matters just as much. He "requested" (1:8). When the official in charge objected, Daniel did not lecture him. The official's fear was real: "then shall ye make me endanger my head to the king" (1:10). So Daniel proposed a limited, checkable trial: ten days of "pulse," which renders the Hebrew zeroʻim, literally "things sown," that is, vegetables (Strong's H2235), and water. Then "as thou seest, deal with thy servants" (1:13). He made it easy for his supervisor to say yes. His conviction was firm, and his way of asking protected the other man's neck.

Picture a sales team that pads its numbers at quarter's end, or a group text that turns cruel. Daniel's pattern still applies: decide the line in advance, ask instead of announce, offer a test, and take the other person's risk seriously. People who skip the last step often turn a principled stand into a self-righteous one.

The lesson ties this chapter to the Word of Wisdom (D&C 89:18–21), but the sharper question is about your unplanned compromises: the drink at the client dinner, the shortcut on the expense report. Did you decide those in advance, or were they decided for you at the table?

Daniel 2: When the boss is impossible

Nebuchadnezzar's demand in chapter 2 is the worst performance review in scripture. He wants the dream and its interpretation but will not, or cannot, say what the dream was. The penalty for failure is death for the whole department (2:5, 12–13). Daniel had not even been in the room.

Watch what Daniel does in order. First he "answered with counsel and wisdom" to Arioch, the man sent to kill him, and asked a plain question: "Why is the decree so hasty from the king?" (2:14–15). He did not panic or plead. He gathered information. Second, he went to the king and asked for time (2:16). Third, he went home and told his three friends, so that "they would desire mercies of the God of heaven concerning this secret" (2:17–18). He did not carry the crisis alone, and he did not try to manage it by himself first and pray later.

When the answer came "in a night vision" (2:19), Daniel's first act was not to run to the palace. He stopped and praised God, and the prayer in 2:20–23 is a full psalm. Then he spoke to the king and refused the credit: "this secret is not revealed to me for any wisdom that I have more than any living" (2:30). Two smaller details are easy to miss. Daniel's first words to Arioch were "Destroy not the wise men of Babylon" (2:24). Those men were his professional rivals, the same astrologers and magicians he had outperformed "ten times" (1:20), and he saved them too. After he was promoted, he asked for his three friends to be promoted with him (2:49).

On a real Tuesday this looks like asking a clarifying question before reacting to an alarming email. It looks like telling your spouse or a trusted friend and asking them to pray with you. It looks like saying "thank you" to God before you send the report that saved the project, then naming the colleagues who helped. And when the layoff list comes, it looks like arguing for the people you do not much like.

Daniel 3: "But if not"

Shadrach, Meshach, and Abed-nego were not bystanders at the dedication of the golden image. They were officials Nebuchadnezzar himself had "set over the affairs of the province of Babylon," and that is exactly what their accusers reminded him of (3:12). Their trouble came partly from success. Envy at work is old.

Their answer is the center of the week: "our God whom we serve is able to deliver us from the burning fiery furnace, and he will deliver us out of thine hand, O king. But if not, be it known unto thee, O king, that we will not serve thy gods" (3:17–18). Elder Dennis E. Simmons built a conference talk on those three words. He said the young men "knew that they could trust God—even if things didn't turn out the way they hoped," and he defined faith as "believing that although we do not understand all things, He does" (Simmons, "But If Not . . . ," April 2004).

Be honest about what this means. "But if not" is not a formula for getting the miracle. The lesson itself points to Alma 14:8–13, where believers were burned and not rescued. The three men decided their obedience before they knew the outcome, and they did not make it depend on the outcome. For many disciples the furnace does not cool. The diagnosis stays, the marriage ends anyway, or the child does not come back this year. The chapter does not promise otherwise. It promises that you will not be alone in the fire. Nebuchadnezzar saw "four men loose, walking in the midst of the fire" (3:25), and the king himself called the fourth one God's "angel" (3:28).

A good test for your own prayers is whether they contain a "but if not." Do you tell God what you will do whatever He decides, or only what you hope He will do?

Daniel 4 and 5: Pride, bad news, and being weighed

Chapter 4 is a confession written by a boss. Nebuchadnezzar walks his palace roof and says, "Is not this great Babylon, that I have built for the house of the kingdom by the might of my power, and for the honour of my majesty?" (4:30). Count the first-person pronouns. Before the voice from heaven has finished, his kingdom is gone (4:31). His sanity comes back only when he "lifted up mine eyes unto heaven" (4:34). The cure for pride in this story is to look up.

Daniel's part in chapter 4 is a model for anyone who has to deliver hard news to someone above them. When he understood the dream, he "was astonied for one hour, and his thoughts troubled him" (4:19). He did not enjoy the king's coming fall. He wished it on the king's enemies instead (4:19). Then he told the truth anyway, all of it, and ended with practical advice: "break off thy sins by righteousness, and thine iniquities by shewing mercy to the poor" (4:27). He was honest, he cared about the king, and he told him what to do next. That is how to give a hard performance review, or tell a parent it is time to stop driving, or tell a friend about the drinking.

Chapter 5 repeats the lesson for someone who should have known better. Belshazzar uses the vessels from the Jerusalem temple as party glassware (5:2–4). Daniel turns down the reward ("Let thy gifts be to thyself," 5:17), retells Nebuchadnezzar's story, and then says the hardest line in the chapter: "And thou his son, O Belshazzar, hast not humbled thine heart, though thou knewest all this" (5:22). Belshazzar's problem was not that he lacked information. He knew the story and did not change.

The words on the wall have a likely background in weights and money. The French scholar Charles Clermont-Ganneau proposed in the 1880s that they are names of weights, "a mina, a shekel, and a peras," the last meaning a half-mina (Jewish Encyclopedia, "Mene, Mene, Tekel, Upharsin"). Daniel reads them as verbs: numbered, weighed, divided (5:26–28). "Thou art weighed in the balances, and art found wanting" (5:27) is the language of a merchant's scale. The image fits a disciple's life well, because we are measured in ordinary units, one by one, over time.

So ask what sacred things you handle casually. It might be covenants made in the temple, a priesthood blessing, the sacrament, or a spouse's trust. What lesson do you know by heart and still have not acted on?

Daniel 6: The audit that found nothing

Chapter 6 opens with a rare sentence about a government official: the presidents "sought to find occasion against Daniel concerning the kingdom; but they could find none occasion nor fault; forasmuch as he was faithful, neither was there any error or fault found in him" (6:4). His enemies went through the books and came up empty. Their only option was to attack his religion: "except we find it against him concerning the law of his God" (6:5).

That is the goal for an ordinary working disciple: honest enough in expenses, timesheets, taxes, and promises that the only thing anyone can hold against you is your faith. Ask yourself whether your coworkers know you are a believer because of how you work, or only because of what you say.

Then comes the decree, and the most practical verse in the book: "when Daniel knew that the writing was signed, he went into his house; and his windows being open in his chamber toward Jerusalem, he kneeled upon his knees three times a day, and prayed, and gave thanks before his God, as he did aforetime" (6:10). The phrase "as he did aforetime" carries the weight. Daniel did not start a new spiritual routine for the crisis. The habit was already there, and the crisis simply found him keeping it. He did not close the windows to hide, and he did not move his prayers to the public square to make a point. He kept doing what he had always done. Psalm 55:17 describes the same rhythm: "Evening, and morning, and at noon, will I pray."

Most of us do the reverse. We pray hard during the crisis and let the habit lapse when things are calm. So the honest question is what your practice is "aforetime," on a calm Tuesday. Is it a set time, a place, a posture, words of thanks and not only requests? If a decree like that came, what would anyone actually catch you doing?

Darius, for his part, signed a decree that flattered him (6:7–9), then "laboured till the going down of the sun" to undo it and could not (6:14). Read before you sign.

The chapter's ending deserves honesty, not a lesson. The accusers are thrown to the lions "them, their children, and their wives" (6:24). That is royal court justice, and the text reports it without comment. A disciple should not take that verse as a model for what happens to people who wrong us. Daniel's own words in the den point somewhere else: "before thee, O king, have I done no hurt" (6:22).

Daniel 7: Keeping it in your heart

The vision in chapter 7 moves to beasts, thrones, and "one like the Son of man" receiving "an everlasting dominion" (7:13–14). The lesson reads this as Christ, the Son of Man, and points to His use of the title before the high priest (Mark 14:61–64). For the practical disciple, the last verse of the block is just as useful: "my cogitations much troubled me, and my countenance changed in me: but I kept the matter in my heart" (7:28).

Daniel did not understand everything he saw. He was troubled, and he said so. He did not resolve it by dropping it or by announcing a theory. He kept it, and kept working. President Dieter F. Uchtdorf, preaching on Daniel, put the comparison simply: "Daniel passed his test. Ours still continues" (Uchtdorf, "Be Not Afraid, Only Believe," October 2015). Endurance in Daniel mostly means showing up honestly to the same job for decades, praying at the same window, and keeping hard questions in your heart without letting them push you off course.

Questions for this week

  • What is my one line, the thing I have "purposed in my heart" not to do at work or school? Have I decided it before the pressure comes?
  • When I object to something, do I make it easy for the people over me to accommodate me, the way Daniel protected Melzar's neck?
  • Who are my Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah, the people I call when the decree goes out? Have I told them anything real lately?
  • Do my prayers include a "but if not"?
  • What do I already know, as Belshazzar did, and still have not acted on?
  • If my coworkers audited my work, would they find "none occasion nor fault"?
  • What is my prayer habit "aforetime," on a calm day?
