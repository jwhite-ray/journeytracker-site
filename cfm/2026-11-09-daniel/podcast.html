Context

ELI: Nathan, put us on the map.

NATHAN: Start with something you can't see in English. The book opens in Hebrew. Then chapter 2, verse 4: the wise men answer the king "in Syriack." The Hebrew word is ʼaramit, "in Aramaic." And from the next words on, the book itself is in Aramaic, through the end of chapter 7. Hebrew comes back at 8:1.

ELI: So it changes languages mid-sentence.

NATHAN: Mid-verse. And the language break doesn't match the content break. Chapters 1 through 6 are stories about Daniel in the third person. Chapters 7 through 12 are his own visions in the first person. So chapter 7 belongs to both halves.

THE SIX-OUTLOOKS VOICE: The panel's academic and Latter-day Saint scholar both lean on a 1972 article by A. Lenglet, who argued the Aramaic chapters are built concentrically. Chapters 2 and 7 both lay out four kingdoms ended by God's kingdom. Chapters 3 and 6 are both deliverances from a death sentence. Chapters 4 and 5 are both judgments on a proud king.

NATHAN: And the Latter-day Saint scholar spots the echo that gives us the week's title. In 3:29 Nebuchadnezzar decrees that "there is no other God that can deliver after this sort." In 6:27 Darius answers: "He delivereth and rescueth."

ELI: And where does the book sit?

NATHAN: Depends whose Bible. Christian Bibles make Daniel the fourth major prophet. In the Hebrew Bible he's in the Writings, beside Esther and Chronicles. The rabbi's reason: in the rabbinic sense a prophet is sent to Israel with a message, and Daniel is never sent. He interprets for kings. So a Jewish reader expects a sage at court, not a prophet at the gate.

ELI: And the genre?

NATHAN: Chapters 1 through 6 are court tales. A wise courtier from a subject people at a foreign king's court. Joseph in Egypt. Esther. Outside the Bible, the Story of Ahiqar.

ELI: And what strikes me reading the academic is how cooperative these stories are.

NATHAN: That's W. Lee Humphreys, 1973. He called it a lifestyle for diaspora. Daniel takes the Babylonian education and name, serves three kings loyally, and the king is almost never the villain. Darius spends a sleepless night fasting, hoping Daniel survives. The lines are drawn narrowly: no defiling food, no bowing to the image, no break in prayer. Short of those, you can serve the empire and prosper.

ELI: The lesson says it in one line: "In a sense, we all live in Babylon." And the practical disciple's essay is called "Faithful in Babylon on a Tuesday." His point is that most of Daniel 1 through 6 happens at work.

THE SIX-OUTLOOKS VOICE: And underneath is one political theology. God "removeth kings, and setteth up kings," 2:21. Chapter 4 says three times that "the most High ruleth in the kingdom of men." The academic's phrase: empires hold power on loan.

Content

ELI: Most of us grew up hearing that as a Word of Wisdom story. The lesson pairs it with section 89.

THE SIX-OUTLOOKS VOICE: And the doctrinal connector says the parallel is real. "God gave them knowledge and skill in all learning and wisdom," 1:17, and section 89 promises "wisdom and great treasures of knowledge, even hidden treasures." But Daniel's stated reason is defilement, not health.

NATHAN: The rabbi says Jewish readers have always heard this as a story about kashrut, the boundary between Israel and the nations at the table. THE SIX-OUTLOOKS VOICE: Four beasts come up out of "the great sea." A lion, a bear, a leopard, and a fourth beast "diverse from all the others," with ten horns and a little horn with "a mouth speaking great things." And there was given him dominion, and glory, and a kingdom."

NATHAN: Walk us through the lenses.

THE SIX-OUTLOOKS VOICE: Parry's advice is to begin with the near view. The Aramaic is kebar ʼenash, which in Aramaic idiom simply means "one like a human being." Earthly kingdoms come up out of the sea looking like animals. God's kingdom comes with the clouds looking human.

ELI: Then the distant view.

THE SIX-OUTLOOKS VOICE: Which has an unusually strong anchor, because Jesus used it. At his trial the high priest asks if he's "the Christ, the Son of the Blessed." Mark 14:62: "I am: and ye shall see the Son of man sitting on the right hand of power, and coming in the clouds of heaven." The high priest tears his clothes. And Jewish texts like the Similitudes of Enoch already read the figure as an individual. The rabbi adds that Rashi says flatly the one like a man "is the King Messiah."

ELI: And the third lens.

THE SIX-OUTLOOKS VOICE: Multiple fulfillment. Parry's image is a layer cake: one prophecy, several fulfillments stacked at different moments. A second-century BC layer, the saints surviving the horn. A first-century layer, Jesus taking the title. And a latter-day layer most readers of Daniel never meet. Doctrine and Covenants 27:11 names "Michael, or Adam" as "the ancient of days." Section 116 says that at Adam-ondi-Ahman "the Ancient of Days shall sit, as spoken of by Daniel the prophet." And in an 1839 discourse in Willard Richards's notes, Joseph Smith reads Daniel 7 directly: "The Son of Man stands before him & there is given him glory & dominion," and "Adam delivers up his Stewardship to Christ."

ELI: So on that reading, 7:13 is a council still to come. The steward hands the keys back to the Lord of the house.

THE SIX-OUTLOOKS VOICE: And the title itself has a Restoration source. Moses 6:57: "Man of Holiness is his name, and the name of his Only Begotten is the Son of Man." So in Restoration teaching "Son of Man" isn't mainly a statement of Jesus' humanity. It's a statement of his parentage.

NATHAN: And the doctrine lens is where you land.

THE SIX-OUTLOOKS VOICE: On how the kingdom is held. The beasts seize dominion. The Son of man's "was given him." And the kingdom given to "him" in verse 14 is given to "the saints" in 18 and 27. The lesson sends readers to Doctrine and Covenants 121:46: "thy dominion shall be an everlasting dominion," nearly Daniel's words.

ELI: And the doctrinal connector catches what follows in that verse. "Without compulsory means." Every beast in Daniel 7 rules by devouring and stamping. The dominion given to the saints is defined by its opposite.

NATHAN: And our block ends with a man who doesn't have it figured out. 7:28. "My cogitations much troubled me, and my countenance changed in me: but I kept the matter in my heart."

THE SIX-OUTLOOKS VOICE: Which is the right posture for what comes next.

Controversies

ELI: The big one. When was this book written?

NATHAN: The challenge is older than modern scholarship. In the third century AD the philosopher Porphyry argued Daniel was composed "by some individual living in Judaea at the time of the Antiochus who was surnamed Epiphanes." We know that only because Jerome quoted him to refute him.

THE SIX-OUTLOOKS VOICE: And most critical scholars hold a refined version. The standard commentaries, John J. Collins in 1993 and Carol Newsom with Brennan Breed in 2014, date the final form to the persecution under Antiochus IV, roughly 167 to 164 BC. The court tales are older. Chapter 7 and the Hebrew visions were added in the crisis.

ELI: On what evidence?

NATHAN: Mostly chapter 11, outside our reading. It tracks the Seleucid and Ptolemaic wars in close detail right up to Antiochus's assault on the temple. Then it has him die in the land of Israel. He actually died on campaign in the east. So on the critical reading, the vision is accurate up to the author's moment, then becomes a real prediction, and a mistaken one. Chapter 7 fits too. The little horn "shall think to change times and laws," and in 1 Maccabees the king outlaws the sabbath and the festivals, for roughly the "time and times and the dividing of time" of the vision.

THE SIX-OUTLOOKS VOICE: Smaller clues too, like three Greek instrument names in 3:5, though the Latter-day Saint scholar notes Benjamin Suchard's 2022 case that those are code-switching, not loanwords. That weakens the argument without overturning it.

NATHAN: And the Dead Sea Scrolls cut both ways. In 2025 a team led by Mladen Popović redated a Qumran copy of Daniel very early, to a range of 230 to 160 BC. A traditionalist says that leaves little time for a new book to spread. A critic says it fits the Maccabean setting exactly.

ELI: So the question everyone listening is asking. What does a late date do to faith?

THE SIX-OUTLOOKS VOICE: The honest questioner says believers stand on more than one side. Conservative scholars like Joyce Baldwin defend a sixth-century Daniel with genuine predictive visions. Others accept a late date for chapters 7 through 12 and treat apocalyptic as an inspired genre, using an ancient hero's voice to tell the persecuted that God had always known their suffering. Others keep the question open. And the questioner is fair about the stakes: Jesus speaks of "Daniel the prophet," and the Bible Dictionary presents those chapters as Daniel's own visions.

NATHAN: The Latter-day Saint scholar's answer is: less than many fear. Restoration scripture doesn't run on the inerrancy framework that makes Daniel's date a test of faith for some Protestants. The eighth article of faith accepts the Bible "as far as it is translated correctly." And in 1986 the Ensign ran an article by John Tvedtnes admitting historical problems in this very book: "Such errors need not damage our faith in the book of Daniel, or in the Old Testament as a whole."

ELI: But he names a real cost.

NATHAN: If the predictions were aimed at the Maccabean crisis, then any Latter-day Saint application to later events has to be understood as a further fulfillment or a likening, not the author's own meaning.

THE SIX-OUTLOOKS VOICE: Which is where Parry's framework earns its keep. The near view and the far view don't have to compete. Whether you date chapter 7 to the sixth century or the second, the doctrine is the same: the beasts' dominion is "prolonged for a season and time," and the Son of man's is given and permanent.

ELI: And the academic says the critical reading doesn't make Daniel a forgery. It shows a book built in layers.

NATHAN: And for its first readers under Antiochus, many of whom were not rescued, "but if not" may have been the most important line in the book.

ELI: (pause) That reframes the furnace for me. The story reached people whose friends were dying for the law.

NATHAN: And the honest questioner says Daniel never explains why some are delivered and others aren't. Chapter 7 itself says the horn "made war with the saints, and prevailed against them."

ELI: Next. Belshazzar and Darius.

NATHAN: Belshazzar used to be the skeptics' favorite example. No source outside the Bible mentioned him. Then a cylinder from Ur turned up with Nabonidus praying for "Belshazzar, the eldest son," and a chronicle saying the king was in Tayma, in Arabia, while "the prince" stayed with the army. So he was real, and in effect regent. And Daniel knows a detail that fits: he can only make Daniel "the third ruler in the kingdom."

THE SIX-OUTLOOKS VOICE: Which makes sense if Belshazzar was himself second.

NATHAN: Right. But the book calls him king and makes Nebuchadnezzar his father. His father was Nabonidus, no relation. Defenders say "father" can mean predecessor. The Latter-day Saint scholar calls that possible but strained. The academic calls it a memory compressed by time: it knows the name, and that Babylon fell on his watch, but not the dynasty.

ELI: And Darius the Mede?

NATHAN: Harder. No source outside Daniel knows him, and no Median kingdom came between Babylon and Persia. The chronicle has Babylon entered in 539 by "Ugbaru, governor of Gutium, and the army of Cyrus." D. J. Wiseman suggested reading 6:28 as "the reign of Darius, even the reign of Cyrus the Persian," making them one man. H. H. Rowley, in 1935, argued against every proposed identification.

THE SIX-OUTLOOKS VOICE: And the critical explanation ties straight into the four kingdoms. Isaiah and Jeremiah had said the Medes would take Babylon, so the author follows a scheme where Media comes next.

ELI: Which is the next fight. Who's the fourth kingdom?

NATHAN: The traditional Christian answer, and the one Latter-day Saint teaching usually assumes: Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece, Rome. Jerome defended it against Porphyry. But the book's own clues point another way. Babylon passes to Darius the Mede, then Cyrus the Persian. In chapter 8 the ram is "the kings of Media and Persia" and the goat "the king of Grecia." So most critical scholars read Babylon, Media, Persia, Greece. The Rome reading has to merge Media and Persia, which the text keeps apart.

THE SIX-OUTLOOKS VOICE: On the Greek reading, the iron mixed with clay that "shall not cleave one to another" fits the failed marriage alliances between the Seleucids and Ptolemies.

NATHAN: And the rabbi complicates everybody. Rashi names the fourth kingdom Rome, which destroyed the Second Temple. "Edom" became the rabbinic code for Rome, and after Constantine for Christian Rome. He says it gently: when Christians call the iron empire Rome, Jews agree, but for many Jewish readers in the Middle Ages, the fourth beast was the Christian power they lived under.

ELI: That's sobering. And he has a word for us, too.

NATHAN: To a Latter-day Saint who sees the stone already rolling, he says Jewish tradition offers no rebuttal, only a caution earned by experience: don't calculate the end.

THE SIX-OUTLOOKS VOICE: And the honest questioner names the hardest form of it. On the Greek reading, the author expected God's kingdom soon after Antiochus fell, and it didn't come in the form described. The faithful replies include layered fulfillment and Nephi's likening. The doctrinal connector notes section 65 borrows the stone, not the metals, so it works under either reading. And the Latter-day Saint scholar says holding the two horizons apart makes the Restoration claim more honest and no weaker.

NATHAN: Next. 3:25. "The form of the fourth is like the Son of God."

ELI: I grew up with that as Christ in the furnace.

NATHAN: The Aramaic is bar ʼelahin, more naturally "a son of the gods," a divine being in a polytheist king's idiom. The NIV and NASB have "a son of the gods." And three verses later Nebuchadnezzar explains himself: God "hath sent his angel."

THE SIX-OUTLOOKS VOICE: The Latter-day Saint scholar says seeing the premortal Christ there is an old and legitimate devotional reading, but the King James capitals make it look more certain than the Aramaic does. In Parry's terms that's likening or personal reading, not the near view.

ELI: And the doctrinal connector gives it better ground. Liberty Jail. "If the very jaws of hell shall gape open the mouth wide after thee... The Son of Man hath descended below them all." The faithful in the fire aren't alone, and Restoration scripture names who goes down with them.

NATHAN: Okay. The Son of man. Individual or collective?

THE SIX-OUTLOOKS VOICE: The puzzle the chapter never settles. Inside the vision the figure looks like an individual. But the angel gives the kingdom to "the saints of the most High." So many read him as a symbol of faithful Israel, just as each beast stands for an empire. Collins reads an individual heavenly being, most likely the archangel Michael, the "great prince" of 12:1. And J. A. Emerton argued in 1958 that the aged figure and the cloud-rider have roots in Canaanite myth about El and Baal.

NATHAN: The rabbi shows the same split inside Judaism. Ibn Ezra grants a messianic reading only in part and says the figure is Israel, the holy people. And on the plural "thrones" in 7:9, Rabbi Akiva taught there were two, one for God and one for David. Rabbi Yose the Galilean rebuked him: "Akiva, how long shall you make the Divine Presence profane?" They saw where a second throne could lead, and shut that door.

ELI: And the academic cautions that in the 160s the phrase wasn't a title yet.

THE SIX-OUTLOOKS VOICE: Which is where the layer cake helps me. It needn't be one or the other. In 7:14 the dominion goes to "him." In 7:27 to "the people of the saints of the most High." A prophecy can be fulfilled first in a people, then in a person, then in that person sharing his kingdom with the people.

NATHAN: And there's an irony about Michael.

THE SIX-OUTLOOKS VOICE: (laughing) My favorite part. In critical scholarship Michael is a candidate for the one like the Son of man. In Restoration scripture Michael is Adam, and Adam is the Ancient of days, who receives the Son of man. Both traditions put Michael in this throne room. They just put him in different seats.

ELI: But the Latter-day Saint scholar is honest that in Daniel 7 the Ancient of days is God.

NATHAN: He is. The one who judges, opens the books, and gives dominion is God. Joseph Smith's reading reassigns the roles. The honest questioner calls it a Restoration reading laid over the text, accepted on the authority of later revelation rather than drawn from the Aramaic.

ELI: Last one, and it's the one I'd rather skip. 6:24.

NATHAN: The king throws Daniel's accusers into the den, "them, their children, and their wives; and the lions had the mastery of them, and brake all their bones in pieces."

ELI: The children.

NATHAN: The honest questioner points out the Law of Moses forbids exactly this. Deuteronomy 24:16: "neither shall the children be put to death for the fathers." You can call it a pagan king's justice, recorded and not endorsed. But the narrator offers no word of disapproval and tells it in the same triumphant key as the rescue. That silence troubles readers, with reason.

THE SIX-OUTLOOKS VOICE: The academic says the same of the conflict stories. Even 3:29, the verse behind the lesson title, threatens to cut blasphemers in pieces. That's how ancient court literature imagined royal justice, and a modern reader need not admire it.

ELI: And the practical disciple says the ending deserves honesty, not a lesson. Don't take that verse as a model for what happens to people who wrong us. Daniel's own words in the den point somewhere else: "before thee, O king, have I done no hurt."

NATHAN: I don't think we resolve that one, and I don't think we should pretend to.

ELI: No. We read it, we notice it, and we keep the matter in our heart.
