Seventeen Shekels and a Bundle of Rags

Written by an AI persona on living the week's text — not a real person.

Most of us will never stand in a besieged city. But nearly everyone lives through a week when the thing they built is coming down: a marriage under strain, a child gone silent, a job lost, a diagnosis, a death. This block is good for those weeks because it does not hurry. It gives us a prophet buying land in a war zone, a king who knows the truth and cannot act on it, a servant who thinks to pad the ropes, a scroll fed to a fire, and poems in which grief is allowed to stay grief. Each scene asks something of an ordinary person on an ordinary Tuesday.

Buy the field anyway (Jeremiah 32)

It is "the tenth year of Zedekiah," Babylon is besieging Jerusalem, and Jeremiah is "shut up in the court of the prison" for saying the city will fall (32:1–3). Into that cell walks his cousin Hanameel with an offer: buy my field at Anathoth, "for the right of redemption is thine" (32:7–8). Anathoth is Jeremiah's hometown, a few miles away, and likely already in territory the Babylonians controlled. On the open market the field is worth nothing.

Jeremiah buys it. Notice how ordinary the procedure is. He weighs out "seventeen shekels of silver," signs the deed, calls witnesses, makes two copies, "that which was sealed according to the law and custom, and that which was open," and hands both to Baruch in front of everyone in the prison court (32:9–12). Then: "put them in an earthen vessel, that they may continue many days. For thus saith the LORD… Houses and fields and vineyards shall be possessed again in this land" (32:14–15).

Faith here is not a feeling. It is paperwork done carefully for a future the man doing it will probably not see. Jeremiah's prayer afterward is honest. He opens with "there is nothing too hard for thee" (32:17), recites the Exodus, and ends on what sounds like bewilderment: the siege mounds are at the walls, "and thou hast said unto me, O Lord GOD, Buy thee the field for money" (32:24–25). He obeyed, and then he asked God, more or less, why did you have me do that? God answers by throwing Jeremiah's own words back to him as a question: "is there any thing too hard for me?" (32:27).

Most disciples have a field at Anathoth: the mission fund kept going for a child not currently attending church; the anniversary dinner booked during a cold season in a marriage; the class you enroll in at fifty-three. Questions for the week:

  • What am I refusing to invest in because the present looks like a siege?
  • What concrete "deed," with a date and a witness, could I sign this week for a future I believe God has promised?
  • When I obey and still feel confused, can I pray as Jeremiah did in 32:16–25, admitting the confusion out loud?

The king who asked in secret (Jeremiah 37–38)

The lesson asks us to weigh the evidence that Zedekiah believed Jeremiah against the evidence that he did not. It is uncomfortable because Zedekiah is not a villain. He asks for prayer (37:3). He asks Jeremiah "secretly… Is there any word from the LORD?" (37:17). He spares him Jonathan's house and orders a daily loaf "out of the bakers' street, until all the bread in the city were spent" (37:21). He swears an oath, again "secretly," to protect him (38:16).

He also hands Jeremiah over to the men who want him dead with one of the saddest sentences in the Bible: "Behold, he is in your hand: for the king is not he that can do any thing against you" (38:5). And when Jeremiah tells him plainly what to do, Zedekiah gives the real reason he will not: "I am afraid of the Jews that are fallen to the Chaldeans, lest they deliver me into their hand, and they mock me" (38:19). The king of Judah is afraid of being laughed at.

The practical danger: you can know exactly what is right, keep consulting people who will tell you the truth, and still decide based on who might mock you. Zedekiah's problem was never information; he lacked the willingness to be seen acting on the words he already had. Jeremiah's answer to the fear is brief: "They shall not deliver thee. Obey, I beseech thee" (38:20). The ending of chapter 39 is unbearable: his sons killed "before his eyes," and then his eyes put out (39:6–7). The last thing he saw was what his fear cost.

Where am I asking for more revelation about something I have already been told? Whose mockery am I actually afraid of: in-laws, coworkers, a ward, my own children, an online crowd?

The honesty question nobody asks (Jeremiah 38:24–27)

Sunday lessons usually skip the end of chapter 38. Zedekiah tells Jeremiah that if the princes ask what they discussed, he should say only that he had asked not to be sent back "to Jonathan's house, to die there" (38:26). The princes ask, and Jeremiah "told them according to all these words that the king had commanded" (38:27).

Was that a lie? Not exactly. Jeremiah really had made that request (37:20), so what he said was true, but deliberately incomplete, told to men who wanted him dead. The text records it without comment. Two things follow. It is no license: a prophet hiding from men who had already thrown him into a pit is not me shading the truth on a timesheet, an expense report, or to my spouse. But honesty and discretion are not the same thing, and no one is obliged to tell everything to everyone who demands it. The test is whether my partial answer is protecting someone's life or protecting my own image. Zedekiah, who started the cover story, was protecting his image.

Rags under the armholes (Jeremiah 38:7–13; 39:15–18)

Ebed-melech, whose name means "servant of the king," is an Ethiopian (Cushite) eunuch, an outsider several times over. He goes to the king "sitting in the gate of Benjamin," a public place, and says out loud that "these men have done evil in all that they have done to Jeremiah" (38:7–9). Those men are the most powerful princes in the city.

Then the detail that makes him a model for ministering. He takes thirty men, but before they pull, he goes "under the treasury" and collects "old cast clouts and old rotten rags," lowers them on cords, and tells Jeremiah, "Put now these… rotten rags under thine armholes under the cords" (38:11–12). A starving man sunk in mud, pulled up by ropes under his arms, would have been badly torn. Ebed-melech thought about that. The rescue is bold, but it is also gentle and it is planned.

The bold part of ministering is showing up and organizing the thirty people. The rag part is what nobody thanks you for: the frozen meal labeled with reheating instructions because the widower has never used the oven; the ride arranged so the newly divorced sister does not walk into sacrament meeting alone. When you help someone, do you think about where the ropes will cut?

God's message to him later says, "thou shalt not be given into the hand of the men of whom thou art afraid" (39:17). He was afraid. So was Zedekiah. One of them acted anyway, and the reason God gives is "because thou hast put thy trust in me" (39:18).

When your work goes into the fire (Jeremiah 36)

Jeremiah 36 is a story about losing your work. Baruch writes out more than two decades of prophecies "from the mouth of Jeremiah" (36:4), reads the scroll at the temple on a fast day and again to the officials, and watches it go to the king. Jehoiakim, sitting by his brazier in the winter, has Jehudi read "three or four leaves," then cuts that strip off "with the penknife" and drops it in the fire, and keeps going "until all the roll was consumed" (36:22–23). The king does it slowly. That is contempt, not panic.

Two practical things stand out. First, three officials, Elnathan, Delaiah, and Gemariah, "made intercession to the king that he would not burn the roll: but he would not hear them" (36:25). They lost. Their speaking up is still recorded. Sometimes the faithful act in a meeting is the objection that gets overruled, and it is still worth making.

Second, the response to destruction is to write it again: "Then took Jeremiah another roll… and there were added besides unto them many like words" (36:32). The second scroll was longer than the first. If you have had a project killed, a business fail, or a calling released before the work was done, this is the pattern. You do not pretend the fire didn't happen. You start the second roll.

Family: sour grapes and a father's troubled heart (Jeremiah 31)

Chapter 31 holds two family texts. The first retires a proverb: "The fathers have eaten a sour grape, and the children's teeth are set on edge." In the promised days, "every one shall die for his own iniquity" (31:29–30). This cuts both ways. To the adult child it says: your parents' failures shaped you, but they do not get to be the permanent explanation for your choices. To the parent it says: you are not the sole author of your child's choices, and you can put down the weight of believing every wandering is your verdict.

The second text sounds like any parent of a struggling child. Ephraim is heard "bemoaning himself… turn thou me, and I shall be turned" (31:18), and God answers like a father who cannot stop thinking about his son: "since I spake against him, I do earnestly remember him still: therefore my bowels are troubled for him; I will surely have mercy upon him" (31:20). A parent can hold a line and still lie awake.

And there is Rachel, "weeping for her children" and refusing "to be comforted for her children, because they were not" (31:15). God's reply does not scold her for refusing comfort. It gives her a reason to stop: "thy work shall be rewarded… there is hope in thine end" (31:16–17). For anyone grieving a child, whether through death, estrangement, or addiction, the order matters. The weeping is heard first. The promise comes after.

The new covenant as a daily practice (Jeremiah 31:31–34; 32:38–41)

The famous promise is that God will "put my law in their inward parts, and write it in their hearts," so that "they shall all know me" (31:33–34). Practically, this is the difference between a family that keeps rules because someone is checking and a family whose members would keep them in an empty house.

Elder David A. Bednar described the starting point of this kind of discipleship as choosing to "take upon ourselves His yoke through the covenants and ordinances of the restored gospel" ("Abide in Me, and I in You; Therefore Walk with Me," April 2023). Elder Alan R. Walker, cited in the lesson, offers a practical sentence for anyone who feels far from that engraved heart: "The Lord does not ask for perfection all at once; rather, He asks for honest and broken hearts, humble spirits, and a willing readiness to keep our covenants—even when it requires sacrifice" ("A Peculiar Treasure," April 2026).

An honest self-check: Which commandment do I keep only when observed? What would it look like for that one, this week, to move from the outside to the inside?

Grief that is allowed to be grief (Lamentations 1; 3)

Lamentations 1 repeats one complaint like a bell: Jerusalem "hath none to comfort her" (1:2), "she had no comforter" (1:9), "there is none to comfort her" (1:17), "there is none to comfort me" (1:21). In the middle of it the city turns to the road: "Is it nothing to you, all ye that pass by? behold, and see if there be any sorrow like unto my sorrow" (1:12).

That verse is a ministering assignment. The charge is not that no one fixed it, but that people passed by. Kathleen M. O'Connor, in Lamentations and the Tears of the World (Orbis, 2002), argues that the book's lack of easy answers is deliberate, and that what sufferers need first is witnesses who will see and hear their pain rather than people who rush to explain it. Ward life has a predictable failure here: the casserole on day three, then nothing by week six, when the widow is actually alone. Put a reminder on your calendar for the third month.

In Lamentations 3 most of us skip to the famous verses: "It is of the LORD's mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not. They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness" (3:22–23), the line Thomas Chisholm turned into the 1923 hymn "Great Is Thy Faithfulness." But the speaker gets there only after twenty verses of feeling hunted: hedged in, chained, shot "as a mark for the arrow," and worst of all, "when I cry and shout, he shutteth out my prayer" (3:7–8, 12). And the hope does not last. Within twenty verses the speaker is saying, "Thou hast covered thyself with a cloud, that our prayer should not pass through" (3:44), and the chapter closes by asking God to "persecute and destroy" the enemies (3:66).

That is honest about how endurance goes. Hope here is not a place you arrive and stay; it is something you "recall to my mind" (3:21) on purpose, and because it is "new every morning," it has to be received again every morning. The counsel is plain and hard: "It is good that a man should both hope and quietly wait" (3:26); "He sitteth alone and keepeth silence" (3:28). And then the one line every sufferer should be allowed to hold on to: "he doth not afflict willingly nor grieve the children of men" (3:33).

President M. Russell Ballard, in a talk the lesson quotes, addressed those who "have lost all hope because of sin and transgression" and pleaded "never give up!" ("The Joy of Hope Fulfilled," October 1992). Lamentations 3 shows what that looks like: not one breakthrough, but a person who keeps recalling mercy between relapses into despair.

Questions for a grief-heavy week: Who in my ward, family, or office is past the casserole stage and into the silence? Can I let someone, perhaps myself, say verse 8 or verse 44 out loud without correcting them? What is my morning practice for receiving mercy that is new every morning?

The week in one sentence

Buy the field, pad the ropes, rewrite the scroll, don't pass by, and recall mercy again tomorrow morning, because it will need recalling.
