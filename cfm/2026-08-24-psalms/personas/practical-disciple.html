What This Week Actually Asks of You

Written by an AI persona on living the week's text — not a real person.

Psalms 49–51; 61–66; 69–72; 77–78; 85–86

Almost none of these psalms were written by people whose problems had been solved. Psalm 51 is a man talking the week after he arranged a death. Psalm 77 is a man who cannot sleep and cannot pray and says so in writing. Psalm 69 is somebody drowning in other people's contempt, his family's included. These are field notes from inside the trouble, not memoirs written later from a comfortable chair.

The temptation is to harvest the pretty verses — "the rock that is higher than I," "create in me a clean heart" — and skip the ugly ones around them. But the practical instruction is mostly in the ugly ones.

Psalm 62: Find out where you are actually putting your weight

Psalm 62 is built on a physical image: "Men of low degree are vanity, and men of high degree are a lie: to be laid in the balance, they are altogether lighter than vanity" (62:9). A scale. The psalmist weighs things — people, money, reputation — and finds they do not register. Twice he says "I shall not be moved" (62:2, 62:6), which is what you say about a floor you are standing on.

So the test is not "what do I believe?" but "what would break if this were taken away?"

  • When something frightens you at 11 p.m., what do you check first — the bank balance, the messages, the metrics at work, the other person's social media?
  • If your household income doubled tomorrow, name the one specific worry that would disappear. Now name the last time you asked God about that worry instead of waiting for money to solve it.
  • Verse 10 says "if riches increase, set not your heart upon them." Has your giving — fast offerings, help to a particular person — actually increased in the years your income did? The number is checkable. Go check it.

Meanwhile, 62:8: "pour out your heart before him." Pouring out is what you do with a container you intend to empty. Most adult prayer is a status update in complete sentences. Try one prayer this week that would be embarrassing if transcribed.

Psalm 49: Watching rich people and feeling the wrong thing

Psalm 49 is uncomfortably specific about a mundane sin: not greed exactly, but the observation of other people's prosperity and what it does to you. "Be not thou afraid when one is made rich, when the glory of his house is increased" (49:16). Notice the emotion he names. Not envy. Fear. He assumes that watching someone else do well makes you feel unsafe, as if their gain were evidence of your exposure.

He also names the fantasy underneath it: "Their inward thought is, that their houses shall continue for ever ... they call their lands after their own names" (49:11). Putting your name on things — the deal, the project, the remodel, the reputation in the ward. Then the flat verdict: "when he dieth he shall carry nothing away" (49:17).

This is not an argument that money is unimportant. It is an argument that you have misfiled the anxiety. The security you are trying to buy is precisely what money cannot buy — "None of them can by any means redeem his brother, nor give to God a ransom for him" (49:7) — and precisely what God has already committed to: "But God will redeem my soul from the power of the grave" (49:15).

Whose life do you look at most often and feel worse afterward? Name the person. Now decide whether you are going to keep looking, and if you are, be honest about why.

Psalm 51: What to do after the thing you cannot take back

The superscription puts Psalm 51 after Nathan came to David — after the adultery and the arranged killing of Uriah. This is not a psalm about feeling vaguely unworthy on a Tuesday. It is a psalm for someone whose life has a before and an after. Three things it does:

It names the sin and stops explaining it. "For I acknowledge my transgressions: and my sin is ever before me" (51:3). Not one word of context, mitigation, or blame — no "she was there," no "the war made it complicated." If your confessions, to God or a bishop or your spouse, cannot survive being said without the surrounding explanation, the explanation is doing work you should stop asking it to do.

It asks for something structural, not emotional. "Create in me a clean heart, O God; and renew a right spirit within me" (51:10). Create and renew are manufacturing words. He is not asking to feel better; he is asking to be a different apparatus. President Russell M. Nelson made the same point from the Greek word for repentance, metanoeo: "He is asking us to change the way we love, think, serve, spend our time, treat our wives, teach our children, and even care for our bodies" ("We Can Do Better and Be Better," April 2019 general conference). That is a checkable list. Pick the item you have never once considered part of repentance, and consider it this week.

It ends in usefulness, not in grief. "Then will I teach transgressors thy ways; and sinners shall be converted unto thee" (51:13). The endpoint of David's repentance is that he becomes useful to people who have done what he did. If your repentance has produced only self-loathing and no capacity to sit with somebody in their worst week, it is not finished — it is stuck.

One caution. Verse 4 says "Against thee, thee only, have I sinned," and people quote it to mean sin is only ever between them and God. Whatever David meant, that is plainly not the whole picture: Uriah was dead; Bathsheba's life was wrecked. The psalm does the God-facing work beautifully and the person-facing work not at all. If you are using 51:4 to avoid a conversation with somebody you actually hurt, you are using half a psalm.

And 51:17 — "a broken and a contrite heart" — is worth defining by contrast. Broken is not ashamed. Shame keeps the sin about you. A broken heart has stopped defending itself.

Psalm 77: For the person who cannot pray

If you have prayed for a year with nothing coming back, Psalm 77 was written for you, and it is unsparing: "my sore ran in the night, and ceased not: my soul refused to be comforted" (77:2). Refused. Not a failure to be comforted — a decision. Then: "I am so troubled that I cannot speak" (77:4).

First, he prays the complaint itself. Verses 7–9 are accusatory questions fired directly at God — "Will the Lord cast off for ever? ... Hath God forgotten to be gracious?" These were preserved, set to music, and sung in public worship. Whatever else that means, it means accusation is a permitted form of prayer. If the only prayer you can currently produce is a complaint, the Psalter's judgment is that a complaint is a prayer. Say it out loud this week rather than not praying.

Second, the pivot in verse 10 is not a feeling. "And I said, This is my infirmity: but I will remember the years of the right hand of the most High" (77:10–11). He never reports that God showed up. He reports a decision to rehearse specific past events — the sea, the cloud, Moses and Aaron (77:16–20). Memory is the tool he uses when consolation is unavailable.

Make that concrete: write down five things, with dates, that you believe God did — not five things you believe about God. "God loves me" does not qualify. "The February I lost the job and the rent came from somewhere" does. If you cannot produce five, produce two. Keep the list where you will find it at 3 a.m. Elder Jeffrey R. Holland, speaking to people in this condition, said, "Hope is never lost," and counseled that if the miracles "do not come soon or fully or seemingly at all," we drink the bitter cup and trust in happier days ahead ("Like a Broken Vessel," October 2013 general conference).

Psalm 69: Contempt, family, and the people who were not there

"I am become a stranger unto my brethren, and an alien unto my mother's children" (69:8). "Reproach hath broken my heart; and I am full of heaviness: and I looked for some to take pity, but there was none; and for comforters, but I found none" (69:20).

That second verse has two edges. If you are the one estranged — the family member somebody has stopped calling, the person whose religion has become a joke among people you love — this psalm does not tell you to pretend it does not hurt. It records the hurt at length before it records anything else.

But 69:20 is also a report on a community: somebody in that man's life could have been the comforter and was not. That is the ministering assignment this week, and it is not abstract — the person on your list who is exhausting to be around, whose situation has gone on so long everyone has quietly stopped asking. Go, and not with a lesson. The complaint is that nobody showed up, not that nobody taught well.

Then the part nobody reads in church: verses 22–28, where he asks God to darken his enemies' eyes and blot them out of the book of the living. Shocking, and instructive. He tells God exactly what he wants done to these people — and hands it over. He does not do it himself. If you are in a marriage that is not going well, or working for someone you resent, the distance between saying the ugly thing to God and enacting it in your kitchen or in a meeting may be the whole of your discipleship this week.

Psalm 50: The sins of the tongue, at home

Psalm 50 is God bringing charges, and the charges are startlingly domestic: "Thou givest thy mouth to evil, and thy tongue frameth deceit. Thou sittest and speakest against thy brother; thou slanderest thine own mother's son" (50:19–20). Not shouting — sitting. This is the conversation in the car on the way home, the sibling text thread, the lowered voice.

Then: "these things hast thou done, and I kept silence; thou thoughtest that I was altogether such an one as thyself" (50:21). You assumed God agreed with you about that person because he did not interrupt. So: who is the family member you speak about most often in a lowered voice, and how would you feel if a transcript of the last month were read back to you?

Psalms 63 and 78: The two schedules

Psalm 63 gives times of day: "early will I seek thee" (63:1) and "when I remember thee upon my bed, and meditate on thee in the night watches" (63:6). The two moments this psalmist reserves for God are, for most of us, the two most reliably given to a phone — the first minutes awake and the last before sleep. That does not require a new program. It requires a decision about where the phone spends the night.

Psalm 78 gives the longer schedule: tell the children "the praises of the Lord ... and his wonderful works that he hath done" (78:4), so "that they might set their hope in God" (78:7). Then it tells them a story in which their ancestors come off appallingly — forgetting, doubting, flattering God with their mouths while their hearts were elsewhere (78:36–37). The transmission model is not a highlight reel; it includes the failures. So what have your children actually heard from you about a time you were wrong, scared, or faithless, and what happened next?

And 78:34 deserves to be sat with: "When he slew them, then they sought him." Crisis-only religion, named by God as a pattern. If somebody plotted your prayer frequency against your stress level, would the two lines be the same line?

Psalm 66:16: Say it out loud

The verse the lesson is named for: "Come and hear, all ye that fear God, and I will declare what he hath done for my soul" (66:16).

Most people find this excruciating, and it is worth being precise about why. It is not modesty. A real declaration requires specifics — a date, an event, a detail — and specifics can be argued with, could have been coincidence, and might sound like a claim that God favors you more than the person listening. "I know the Church is true" commits you to nothing checkable. "In March, this happened" does.

But look at what surrounds verse 16: "Thou broughtest us into the net ... we went through fire and through water" (66:11–12). The man inviting everyone to come and hear was thoroughly wrecked and says so, and he assigns the wrecking to God rather than to bad luck. So the declaration this psalm asks for is not a report that things went well. It is a report of what God did in the fire and the water, from somebody not pretending the fire did not happen.

Then verse 14, the sharpest practical hook in the block: "which my lips have uttered, and my mouth hath spoken, when I was in trouble." He is paying vows he made while desperate. Everybody makes those — in the hospital corridor, in the parking lot after the diagnosis, at 3 a.m. before the meeting. What did you promise God during the worst week of the last two years? Have you done it? If you cannot remember, that is itself the answer, and this is the week to work out what it was.

The one thing, if you only do one

Psalm 86:11: "Teach me thy way, O Lord; I will walk in thy truth: unite my heart to fear thy name." A united heart is the opposite of what most of us are running — one part faithful, one part hedging, one part still keeping accounts against somebody.

If the week gets away from you, do this much: pick one person and tell them one specific thing God has done for you, with a date and a detail in it. That is Psalm 66:16, and it is the assignment. It will be uncomfortable, and that discomfort is worth examining rather than avoiding.
