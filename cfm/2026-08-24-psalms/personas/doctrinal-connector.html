What He Hath Done for My Soul: Psalms 49–86 and the Restoration

Written by an AI persona connecting the block to Restoration scripture and modern teaching — not a real person.

One word keeps surfacing in this week's block: soul. "God will redeem my soul from the power of the grave" (Psalm 49:15). "I will declare what he hath done for my soul" (Psalm 66:16). In Hebrew, nephesh is closer to "life" or "self" than to a disembodied spirit — but the Restoration hears that word and supplies a definition: "the spirit and the body are the soul of man" (D&C 88:15). That is the pattern to watch all week: the Psalms ask, and Restoration scripture answers in terms the psalmist did not have.

Psalm 49 and the plan behind the ransom

Psalm 49 is a wisdom poem in the shape of an argument about money, and its central move is a denial: "None of them can by any means redeem his brother, nor give to God a ransom for him" (49:7). Wealth cannot buy a body out of the grave. Then the reversal: "But God will redeem my soul from the power of the grave" (49:15).

The psalm never says how. Jacob takes up the identical problem — an escape from death no mortal power can arrange — and names the mechanism: "it must needs be an infinite atonement," because only that frees us from "that monster, death and hell, which I call the death of the body, and also the death of the spirit" (2 Nephi 9:7, 10). Alma supplies the anatomy: "The soul shall be restored to the body, and the body to the soul... all things shall be restored to their proper and perfect frame" (Alma 40:23). Psalm 49:15 says my soul; Alma explains that this means the reassembly of a person, not the rescue of a ghost. Nor is the link imported from outside — the footnote at Psalm 49:15 routes the reader to Mosiah 27:24–26, D&C 93:38, Moses 5:9, and Article of Faith 1:3.

Psalm 51 and the Book of Mormon's conversion narratives

Psalm 51 is the block's center of gravity, and the lesson points to Alma 36 alongside it. The parallel is stronger than shared topic — the two texts run the same sequence. David acknowledges ("my sin is ever before me," 51:3), asks to be cleansed ("wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow," 51:7), asks for a new interior ("Create in me a clean heart, O God," 51:10), asks for joy back (51:12) — and then makes the turn most readers skip, a vow to teach: "Then will I teach transgressors thy ways; and sinners shall be converted unto thee" (51:13). Alma runs the same course: total recall of sin ("I did remember all my sins and iniquities," Alma 36:13), the cry to Christ (36:18), the exchange of pain for joy ("my soul was filled with joy as exceeding as was my pain," 36:20), and then the identical vow in the identical narrative position: "I have labored without ceasing, that I might bring souls unto repentance; that I might bring them to taste of the exceeding joy of which I did taste" (36:24).

Both men end their repentance by turning outward — which is why the lesson pairs Psalm 51:13–15 with Psalm 66:5–20 and Alma 26. Ammon's rejoicing is Psalm 66:16 in Nephite idiom: "I will not boast of myself, but I will boast of my God" (Alma 26:12). Testimony is not private property; it is the last step of repentance.

Enos belongs here too — his "wrestle... before God, before I received a remission of my sins" (Enos 1:2) and his stunned "Lord, how is it done?" (1:7) sit exactly where Psalm 51:7–10 sits. Note the verb in 51:10: create. Not repair, not improve. The Hebrew bara' is the verb of Genesis 1:1, used in the Old Testament with God as its only subject. David is asking for an act of creation, and Mosiah 5:2 records that act performed on a whole congregation: "the Spirit of the Lord Omnipotent... has wrought a mighty change in us, or in our hearts, that we have no more disposition to do evil, but to do good continually." Ezekiel promised the same in nearly David's words (Ezekiel 36:26). Psalm 51:10 is the prayer; Mosiah 5:2 is the receipt.

President Russell M. Nelson explains why the cleansing and the change are one act rather than two. Unpacking the Greek metanoeo, he noted that "the prefix meta- means 'change'" and that the suffix relates to words meaning "'mind,' 'knowledge,' 'spirit,' and 'breath'" — then: "When we choose to repent, we choose to change!" ("We Can Do Better and Be Better," April 2019 general conference). Elder D. Todd Christofferson added the tone: "Repentance is a divine gift, and there should be a smile on our faces when we speak of it" ("The Divine Gift of Repentance," October 2011). Psalm 51 asks for that smile in advance — "Make me to hear joy and gladness" (51:8) — before the forgiveness arrives.

"In sin did my mother conceive me"

The lesson's Scripture Helps section raises this question, so it deserves a direct answer. Psalm 51:5 has long served as a proof text for inherited guilt. The Restoration reads it differently, and the sharpest evidence is that the Book of Moses uses the very same phrase and then defines it against original sin.

Moses 6:54: "the Son of God hath atoned for original guilt, wherein the sins of the parents cannot be answered upon the heads of the children, for they are whole from the foundation of the world." One verse later: "Inasmuch as thy children are conceived in sin, even so when they begin to grow up, sin conceiveth in their hearts, and they taste the bitter, that they may know to prize the good" (6:55). The phrase survives; the doctrine is inverted. "Conceived in sin" describes the fallen world a child is born into, not guilt a child is born with — as Article of Faith 1:2 states flatly and D&C 93:38 completes: "Every spirit of man was innocent in the beginning."

Read that way, 51:5 is not David blaming his mother or Adam — verse 4 rules that out in advance, "Against thee, thee only, have I sinned." It is a man saying his sin runs deeper than one incident.

The hardest verse: "take not thy holy spirit from me"

Psalm 51:11 asks two things: "Cast me not away from thy presence; and take not thy holy spirit from me." The LDS footnotes flag the stakes — "TG Death, Spiritual, First" and "TG Holy Ghost, Loss of." D&C 121:37 describes that withdrawal as a consequence rather than a punishment: "when we undertake to cover our sins, or to gratify our pride, our vain ambition, or to exercise control or dominion or compulsion upon the souls of the children of men, in any degree of unrighteousness, behold, the heavens withdraw themselves; the Spirit of the Lord is grieved." The list is uncomfortably close to what 2 Samuel 11 records of David — covering sin, exercising compulsion upon a soul. The section's promise runs the other way: "let virtue garnish thy thoughts unceasingly; then shall thy confidence wax strong in the presence of God... The Holy Ghost shall be thy constant companion" (121:45–46). Set beside Psalm 51:11, those verses describe one relationship from opposite ends.

Which raises the question this block cannot honestly avoid: was David's prayer answered? D&C 132:39 says that "in none of these things did he sin against me save in the case of Uriah and his wife; and, therefore he hath fallen from his exaltation." Peter, at Pentecost, quotes Psalm 16:10 — "thou wilt not leave my soul in hell" — and then declines to apply it to its author: David "is both dead and buried, and his sepulchre is with us unto this day"; "For David is not ascended into the heavens" (Acts 2:27, 29, 34). The Psalter's most personal cries about the soul's rescue turn out, on apostolic reading, to be about Christ.

The LDS edition makes this point itself, and quietly: the footnote at Psalm 86:13 — "thou hast delivered my soul from the lowest hell" — points to Acts 2:34, the verse saying David did not ascend. That is an editorial invitation to read David's confidence against Peter's correction. What holds both is the ordinary Restoration distinction between salvation and exaltation. D&C 132:39 does not say David is lost; it says he "hath fallen from his exaltation." Psalm 86:13 can be true of a soul delivered from hell without being true of a soul enthroned — which is precisely the lower and surer claim Alma makes in Mosiah 27:29: "My soul hath been redeemed from the gall of bitterness and bonds of iniquity." The pattern of David's prayer holds even where the petitioner's own outcome was limited, which is why the Church's lesson can still make Psalm 51 the week's primary repentance text. As Elder Peter F. Meurs put it, "the Savior's healing and redeeming power applies to accidental mistakes, poor decisions, challenges, and trials of every kind—as well as to our sins" ("He Could Heal Me!," April 2023 general conference).

Psalm 85:10 and Alma 42

"Mercy and truth are met together; righteousness and peace have kissed each other" (Psalm 85:10). This is the richest verse in the block, and Alma 42 is its commentary. Corianton had asked how God can be merciful without ceasing to be just. Alma refuses to soften either attribute: "mercy could not take effect except it should destroy the work of justice. Now the work of justice could not be destroyed; if so, God would cease to be God" (Alma 42:13). The resolution is not compromise but a place where both are paid in full: "God himself atoneth for the sins of the world, to bring about the plan of mercy, to appease the demands of justice, that God might be a perfect, just God, and a merciful God also" (42:15). Then the line that reads like an exegesis of the Psalm: "justice exerciseth all his demands, and also mercy claimeth all which is her own" (42:24). Psalm 85 personifies four attributes and stages a meeting; Alma personifies mercy and justice, stages a collision, and names the meeting place — the Atonement. Elder Jeffrey R. Holland's "Where Justice, Love, and Mercy Meet" (April 2015 general conference) carries the same image in its title. Read Psalm 85:10 and Alma 42:24 back to back: not merely compatible, but the same sentence in two grammars.

Then read the next verse. Psalm 85:11: "Truth shall spring out of the earth; and righteousness shall look down from heaven." Now Enoch, in Moses 7:62: "And righteousness will I send down out of heaven; and truth will I send forth out of the earth, to bear testimony of mine Only Begotten." Same pairing, same directions — truth from below, righteousness from above — with the purpose clause the Psalm lacks. The LDS chapter heading glosses 85:11 as the Book of Mormon; that heading is editorial, not scripture. Moses 7:62 is scripture, and it is what makes the reading defensible rather than devotional.

The rock, the remembering, and the reign

The rock. "Lead me to the rock that is higher than I" (Psalm 61:2); "He only is my rock and my salvation" (62:2, 6). The footnote at Psalm 71:3 points to Helaman 5:12: build "upon the rock of our Redeemer, who is Christ, the Son of God," because it is "a sure foundation, a foundation whereon if men build they cannot fall." But watch what changes. The Psalms use the rock as refuge, something you hide behind; Helaman converts it into foundation, something you stand on. Psalm 62:6's "I shall not be moved" is what Helaman means by "cannot fall."

The remembering. Psalm 78 is a covenant-memory text with a teaching mandate built in: parents must tell the next generation "the praises of the Lord... that they might set their hope in God, and not forget the works of God" (78:4, 7). Book of Mormon prophets adopt this as a rhetorical signature — Helaman's "remember, remember," Benjamin's "I would that ye should remember to retain the name written always in your hearts" (Mosiah 5:12) — and the Restoration institutionalizes it weekly, in sacrament prayers asking that participants "always remember him" (Moroni 4:3; 5:2; D&C 20:77, 79). As Elder Gerrit W. Gong observed, "each week, in partaking of the sacrament, we covenant to always remember Him" ("Always Remember Him," April 2016 general conference). Psalm 78 diagnoses forgetting as the mechanism of apostasy — "they kept not the covenant of God... and forgat his works" (78:10–11). The sacrament is the ordinance built against that exact failure.

The reign. Psalm 72's promises outrun any Israelite king: dominion "from sea to sea," "let the whole earth be filled with his glory" (72:8, 19). The footnotes route these to D&C 45:66, 88:104, and 65:2 — the kingdom rolling forth "until it has filled the whole earth." Then 72:20 signs off: "The prayers of David the son of Jesse are ended." The prayers ended; the reign has not begun.

Where I would not press the connection

Three places where a shared phrase is not a shared idea:

Psalm 69:21 — "They gave me also gall for my meat" — is genuinely messianic, and the footnotes to Matthew 27:34 and John 19:29 are sound. But the Book of Mormon's recurring "gall of bitterness" (Alma 36:18; Mosiah 27:29) is a different expression, drawn from Acts 8:23, naming an inward state rather than a drink at a crucifixion. Thematically resonant, lexically unrelated. Alma is not quoting Psalm 69.

Psalm 51:11's "holy spirit" is, in the Hebrew, ruach qodesh — God's own animating presence, not yet the distinct Third Member of the Godhead of D&C 130:22. Restoration doctrine illuminates the fear David expresses; it should not be read back into his vocabulary.

And the temple language of Psalms 61, 63, and 65 — "I will abide in thy tabernacle for ever" (61:4), "that he may dwell in thy courts... even of thy holy temple" (65:4) — is real temple devotion, but it is the Jerusalem temple, with its bullocks and its courts, not the endowment. What survives the translation is the longing, and the footnote at Psalm 65:4 catches it by pointing to Enos 1:27: "there is a place prepared for you in the mansions of my Father." President Nelson's teaching that "the temple lies at the center of strengthening our faith and spiritual fortitude because the Savior and His doctrine are the very heart of the temple" ("The Temple and Your Spiritual Foundation," October 2021 general conference) is continuous with Psalm 65:4 in desire, not in ritual.

That distinction protects what the block is about. Psalm 66:16 does not say come and hear what the ordinances accomplished. It says: "I will declare what he hath done for my soul." Every connection above is useful only insofar as it makes that sentence sayable.
