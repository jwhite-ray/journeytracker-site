A Guest at Your Table: Seventeen Psalms, Read from the Other Side of the Aisle

Written by an AI persona giving a Jewish reading in the voice of a rabbi — not a real rabbi.

I have been asked to read this week's psalms as a Jew reads them — an odd and generous thing to ask. These are the prayers my people say in the morning, at a funeral, on the night of Kol Nidre. You have invited someone in to tell you what the furniture is actually for. So I will, including the parts that may sting. A guest who only flatters the house is not much of a guest.

First, the verse numbers are wrong

Not wrong exactly — different. Open a Hebrew Bible beside your King James and for ten of this week's seventeen chapters the numbers do not line up. Those lines your edition prints in italics above the psalm — "To the chief Musician, A Psalm for the sons of Korah" — are not headings. In the Masoretic text they are scripture, and they are counted: sometimes standing as verse 1 on their own, sometimes folded into the opening line. The tally, checked against the Hebrew:

  • Hebrew runs one verse ahead of the KJV in Psalms 49, 61, 62, 63, 64, 65, 69, 70, 77, and 85.
  • Two verses ahead in Psalm 51, whose superscription fills two verses: "To the leader, a psalm of David," then "when Nathan the prophet came to him, after he had gone in to Bathsheba."
  • The numbering matches in Psalms 50, 66, 71, 72, 78, and 86, where the superscription is short (or, in Psalm 71, absent).

This is not pedantry. When the Talmud cites "Psalms 51:19," it means what your Bible calls 51:17 — "the sacrifices of God are a broken spirit." When it cites "Psalms 51:17," it means your 51:15, "O Lord, open thou my lips." Chase a rabbinic source and land on the wrong verse, and this is usually why.

And while we are counting: Selah is not a musical rest and not a scribal shrug. The school of Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov taught that wherever Scripture says netzaḥ, Selah, or va'ed, "the matter will never cease," proving Selah from Psalm 48:9 (Eruvin 54a). Whatever its origin, in Jewish ears Selah means and that will not stop. Read Psalm 49:15 with that in your mouth: God will redeem my life from the hand of Sheol — Selah.

Korah's sons, Asaph's sons, and a guild of singers

Two names govern this block. Psalms 49 and 85 belong to b'nei Koraḥ; Psalms 50, 77, and 78 to Asaph. Christian readers often treat these as bylines. Jewish tradition treats them as institutional.

The sons of Korah are a scandal and a wonder. Their father led the rebellion and the ground swallowed him — yet Numbers 26:11 says flatly, "The sons of Korah did not die." A baraita taught in the name of "our teacher" holds that "a place was fortified for them in Gehenna, and they sat upon it and recited songs of praise" (Sanhedrin 110a). The children of the arch-rebel, perched on a ledge above hell, singing. That guild gave us Psalm 49 on the futility of wealth and Psalm 85 on a nation begging to be turned back.

Asaph appears in Chronicles as a functionary: David set apart "the sons of Asaph, of Heman, and of Jeduthun, who prophesied to the accompaniment of lyres, harps, and cymbals" (1 Chronicles 25:1); Hezekiah later had the Levites praise God "in the words of David and Asaph the seer" (2 Chronicles 29:30). Jeduthun surfaces twice this week, atop Psalms 62 and 77. These headings are personnel files.

The Elohistic Psalter, caught in the act

Psalms 42–83 mostly prefer Elohim where the surrounding Psalter says YHWH — the Elohistic Psalter — and you can catch the substitution here. Psalm 70 duplicates Psalm 40:14–18 (KJV 40:13–17) with the Name swapped: Psalm 40:14 opens retzeh YHWH l'hatzileni, "Be pleased, O LORD, to deliver me," while Psalm 70:2 (KJV 70:1) opens Elohim l'hatzileni. The Name is handled, guarded, stepped around.

Psalm 51: teshuvah, and a fight the Sages never settled

The plain reading is the one your lesson assumes: David sinned with Bathsheba, Nathan confronted him, and Psalm 51 is the wreckage. Midrash Tehillim opens with Proverbs 18:21, "death and life are in the power of the tongue," and asks what earned David the World to Come. The answer: his mouth, which said ḥatati — "I have sinned" (Midrash Tehillim 51:1, on 2 Samuel 12:13). Two words. That is the engine of teshuvah.

But the Talmud also preserves a startling minority insistence. Rabbi Shmuel bar Naḥmani said in the name of Rabbi Yonatan: "Anyone who says that David sinned with Bathsheba is nothing other than mistaken" (Shabbat 56a). The argument is technical, and not really about the law. It is about not letting the king of Israel be reduced to his worst night.

And yet the same tradition needs David to have sinned. Avodah Zarah 5a: "If an individual has sinned, one says to him: go to that famous individual who sinned, King David, and learn from him that one can repent." The same Rabbi Yonatan there reads "the man raised on high" (2 Samuel 23:1) as David who raised up the yoke of repentance, teaching its power by his own example. So: David did not sin, and David is the master text on sinning. Jewish tradition holds both, unresolved, on purpose. A tradition that cannot bear its heroes' failures cannot teach anyone how to fail.

What teshuvah requires, Maimonides spells out: abandon the sin, remove it from your thoughts, resolve never to return, regret the past, and say it out loud — verbal confession is not optional (Mishneh Torah, Hilkhot Teshuvah 2:2). No mediator, no transaction. Resh Lakish went further: repentance from love converts deliberate sins into merits (Yoma 86b). Not erasure. Transmutation.

The psalm's most quoted line is load-bearing halakhically, not just poetically. Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi taught that one whose spirit is humble is credited "as though he had sacrificed all the offerings," since Scripture says zivḥei Elohim in the plural: "the sacrifices of God are a broken spirit" (Sanhedrin 43b, on Psalms 51:19 / KJV 51:17). Your verse is the rabbinic warrant for prayer replacing the altar after 70 CE — why there is still a Judaism.

"In sin did my mother conceive me"

Your lesson's "Scripture Helps" asks whether Psalm 51:5 (Hebrew 51:7) teaches original sin. Rashi answers before the question is asked. He glosses it as David saying, in effect, how could I not sin, given how creatures like me are made and out of what appetites — then adds that "there are many midrashim to this verse, but they do not fit the context of the psalm" (Rashi on Psalms 51:7).

No inherited guilt. A man confessing that he is built out of flammable material. Your own tradition lands in nearly the same place — "men will be punished for their own sins, and not for Adam's transgression" (Article of Faith 1:2). On this one we are on the same side of the room, both reading against Augustine.

Ruaḥ kodshecha is not the Holy Ghost

Psalm 51:11 (Hebrew 51:13): "take not thy holy spirit from me." In Hebrew, v'ruaḥ kodshecha — literally the spirit of Your holiness. It is a construct phrase describing something God possesses, not a name for someone God is.

The commentators are explicit about what it denotes. Radak explains that ruaḥ ha-kodesh was in David — it was by that spirit that he composed the psalms — and when he sinned it departed, so he prays not that it be taken forever but that it be restored (Radak on Psalms 51:13). Ibn Ezra says David feared falling from the rank of "the men of ruaḥ ha-kodesh," citing David's late claim that "the spirit of the LORD spoke in me," 2 Samuel 23:2 (Ibn Ezra on Psalms 51:13).

In rabbinic Hebrew, ruaḥ ha-kodesh is the capacity for divine inspiration — prophecy's quieter cousin. It is a grade, and it can lapse: "From the time when Haggai, Zechariah, and Malachi died, the Divine Spirit departed from Israel" (Sotah 48b). David is not praying that a Person of the Godhead not abandon him. He is praying not to be demoted — not to lose the thing that lets him write psalms. Christian readers who hear a third divine hypostasis here are hearing a word the Hebrew does not say.

What we say, and when we say it

Christians read psalms. Jews use them.

Every Amidah begins with Psalm 51:15. Rabbi Yoḥanan taught that before every prayer one recites "Lord, open my lips, that my mouth may declare Your glory" (Berakhot 4b, citing Psalms 51:17 in Hebrew numbering). Three times a day, a Jew opens the central prayer of the liturgy with a line from David's confession — as if to say: I have no standing here. Open my mouth anyway.

On the eve of Yom Kippur, this block is stitched into a single plea. In the Ashkenazi Maariv for Kol Nidre the congregation prays "Cast us not away from Your Presence, and Your holy spirit take not from us" (Psalm 51:13, KJV 51:11), then "Do not cast us off in time of old age, when our strength fails" (Psalm 71:9), then "Give us a sign of Your goodness that our enemies may see it and be ashamed" (Psalm 86:17). Three verses from this week's reading, pulled into the plural. David's private wreckage becomes the community's.

Psalm 49 is a psalm of the house of mourning, recited in Ashkenazi practice after Shacharit and Minḥah in a shiva house — except on days when Taḥanun is omitted and the calendar forbids public grief, when Psalm 16 is substituted. This meditation on how the rich man carries nothing away is what you say on a low chair, next to a covered mirror.

Psalm 86 is a Tefillah le-David, a title only Psalm 17 shares — odd, since Psalm 72 has just declared "the prayers of David the son of Jesse are ended" (72:20). Ibn Ezra canvasses explanations, one credited to Rabbi Yehudah HaLevi: a scribe's colophon closing a book (Ibn Ezra on Psalms 72:20). Then Psalm 86 arrives titled a prayer of David anyway.

Psalm 78 and the machinery of Jewish memory

Psalm 78 is not history. It is a maskil — a teaching piece — whose opening move is to command transmission: tell the children, so the next generation will tell theirs (78:5–6). Midrash Tehillim reads this into Deuteronomy 4:9–10, the Sinai command not to let what your eyes have seen depart from your heart (Midrash Tehillim 78:1).

To see the psalm at work, look at the Passover Haggadah. In Maggid, Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Akiva argue about how many plagues struck Egypt — and their whole dispute is a word-by-word parse of Psalm 78:49. Rabbi Eliezer counts four items in the verse and reaches forty plagues in Egypt and two hundred at the Sea; Rabbi Akiva counts five and reaches fifty and two hundred fifty (Pesaḥ Haggadah, Maggid, The Ten Plagues). Psalm 78's retelling of the Exodus has been folded back into the ritual retelling of it. The psalm does not describe Jewish memory-practice; it is a beam holding it up.

Psalm 69, said plainly

I would be a poor guest if I skipped this one. Psalm 69 has a history, and the history has a body count.

The New Testament quotes it heavily — 69:9 in John 2:17, 69:21 in the crucifixion accounts, 69:25 in Acts 1:20 against Judas, and, most consequentially, 69:22–23 in Romans 11:9–10: "Let their table become a snare... let their eyes be darkened." There the referent is shifted onto Israel, and "let their eyes be darkened" went on to a long career in Christian preaching about Jewish blindness. Jews did not meet Psalm 69 as devotional literature. We met it as evidence introduced against us.

Now hear how Rashi opens the psalm. On the superscription's al shoshanim — "upon lilies" — he writes that it concerns "Israel, who are like a rose among the thorns, pricked by the thorns, and he prayed for them" (Rashi on Psalms 69:1). He wrote that in eleventh-century Troyes, in the shadow of the First Crusade. In Jewish reading, Psalm 69 is a prayer for the Jews, spoken by a man drowning in mire whose enemies outnumber the hairs of his head. When Christians read it as testimony against the synagogue, they took Israel's own lament and handed it to Israel's persecutors. I say this not to make anyone squirm, but because you asked a rabbi, and you cannot read Psalm 69 honestly without knowing where it has been.

Psalm 72, and what a Jewish messiah is for

Psalm 72 is a coronation prayer — li-Shlomo, for Solomon. Rashi reads it that way: David prayed it for his son, foreseeing that Solomon would ask for "a heart to understand, to hear judgment" (Rashi on Psalms 72:1).

But the Sages also found the Messiah here. Discussing his name, the school of Rabbi Yannai said "Yinnon is his name," from "may his name endure forever; may his name continue [yinnon] as long as the sun" — Psalm 72:17 (Sanhedrin 98b). So yes, Jewish tradition reads this psalm messianically.

Notice what it asks a messiah to do. Judge the poor with justice. Break the oppressor. Deliver the needy who has no helper. Redeem their life from violence, "and precious shall their blood be in his sight" (72:4, 12–14). Not one word about forgiving sins or dying for anyone. The Jewish messiah is a king, a working sovereign who fixes the world's actual arrangements. Judaism did not fail to notice a spiritual messiah here. It read what the text says the man is hired for.

Psalm 85, and one last midrash

Psalm 85:10 (Hebrew 85:11): ḥesed ve-emet nifgashu, tzedek v'shalom nashaku — "mercy and truth are met together; righteousness and peace have kissed each other."

Genesis Rabbah builds a scene on it. When God came to create the first human, the ministering angels split into factions. Kindness said: create him, he does acts of kindness. Truth said: do not, he is full of lies. Righteousness said: create him. Peace said: do not, he is full of discord. What did the Holy One do? He seized Truth and hurled it to the ground. The angels protested — Truth is Your own seal. The answer comes from the next verse of our psalm: "Truth will spring from the earth" (Genesis Rabbah 8:5, on Psalms 85:11–12).

God overruled Truth in order to make us; Truth had to be buried before it could grow. Every one of these seventeen psalms — the wealth that redeems nobody, the broken bones, the mire, the drunkards' song, the manna and the rebellion — is Truth working its way back up through the dirt.

Thank you for the chair at your table.
