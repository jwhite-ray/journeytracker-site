The Editors Left Fingerprints: Psalms 49–51, 61–66, 69–72, 77–78, 85–86 as Scholars Read Them

Written by an AI persona in the voice of a non-Latter-day-Saint Hebrew Bible academic — not a real scholar.

This week's seventeen chapters are not a random sample. They fall almost entirely inside a stretch of the Psalter — Psalms 42 through 83 — long recognized as a distinct editorial unit, and they straddle the seam between Book II and Book III. If you want to see how the book of Psalms was actually assembled, this is close to the best cross-section available. Below is what the field currently thinks, and where it disagrees.

Forty-two songs for a different divine name

Read Psalm 42 through Psalm 83 in Hebrew and something odd happens: the personal name of Israel's god, YHWH (rendered "LORD" in small capitals in the KJV), largely disappears, and the common noun ʾĕlōhîm, "God," takes its place. The ratio in these psalms runs heavily toward ʾĕlōhîm — roughly 200-plus occurrences against a few dozen for YHWH — a distribution that inverts the pattern in the rest of the book. Scholars call this block the Elohistic Psalter.

Nobody has a settled explanation. Laura Joffe, in "The Elohistic Psalter: What, How and Why?" (Scandinavian Journal of the Old Testament 15, 2001), surveyed the proposals and argued that the collection was deliberately built to comprise exactly forty-two psalms. Joel S. Burnett developed the numerical argument in "Forty-Two Songs for Elohim" (Journal for the Study of the Old Testament 31.1, 2006, 81–101), pointing to comparative precedents for organizing sacred texts by number — the forty-two judges of the Egyptian Book of the Dead among them. Other scholars remain unconvinced the number is doing any work, and treat the substitution as a theological or scribal preference of a particular circle.

The doublet as a controlled experiment

The best evidence that this really is editorial work — rather than an accident of which psalms happened to prefer which word — sits in this week's block. Psalm 70 is a near-duplicate of Psalm 40:13–17. Compare the Hebrew:

  • Psalm 40:13 opens rəṣēh YHWH lə-haṣṣîlēnî — "Be pleased, YHWH, to deliver me." Psalm 70:1 reads ʾĕlōhîm lə-haṣṣîlēnî — "God, to deliver me." The divine name has been swapped, and the verb rəṣēh has dropped out entirely, which is why the KJV translators had to import "make haste" into the first line from the second.
  • Psalm 40:16 has worshipers say "YHWH be magnified"; Psalm 70:4 has them say "God be magnified."

So far, consistent. But the last verse cuts the other way. Psalm 40:17 ends ʾĕlōhay ʾal-təʾaḥar, "my God, do not delay"; Psalm 70:5 ends YHWH ʾal-təʾaḥar, "YHWH, do not delay." The Elohistic version has YHWH where the non-Elohistic version has "God." Whoever did this work was not running a mechanical find-and-replace. Psalm 40:17 also reads ʾădōnāy yaḥăšāb-lî, "the Lord thinks of me," where Psalm 70:5 reads ʾĕlōhîm ḥûšāh-lî, "God, hurry to me" — two consonantally similar verbs, one of which is probably a corruption or a re-hearing of the other.

Which direction the borrowing ran is genuinely disputed. Psalm 40 is itself widely read as composite — a thanksgiving in vv. 1–12 with a lament appended in vv. 13–17 — so the shorter unit may have circulated independently and been attached to Psalm 40 rather than excerpted from it. Either way, the two texts sitting in one book in two different recensions is hard evidence that the Psalter was compiled from collections, not composed as a book.

The seam at Psalm 72

Psalm 72:20 reads: "The prayers of David the son of Jesse are ended." This is a colophon — a scribal end-note — and it is manifestly false as a description of the book, since more psalms are attributed to David after it (including Psalm 86, in this block). The natural inference is that a collection of Davidic prayers once ended here, and that later material was added around it without deleting the note.

Psalm 72 also carries a doxology (vv. 18–19, ending "Amen, and Amen") matching those at 41:13, 89:52, and 106:48. These four doxologies produce the Psalter's five-book division, a structure the rabbinic Midrash Tehillim likened to the five books of Moses. Gerald H. Wilson's The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter (SBL Dissertation Series 76, 1985) made this the foundation of a whole subfield, and in "The Use of Royal Psalms at the 'Seams' of the Hebrew Psalter" (JSOT 35, 1986, 85–94) he argued that royal psalms were placed deliberately at these joins — Psalm 2 at the opening, Psalm 72 here, Psalm 89 at the end of Book III — tracing an arc from the founding of the dynasty to its collapse. Not everyone accepts the arc; almost everyone now accepts that Psalm 72's position is not accidental.

Psalm 72 and the job description of an ancient king

Strip away the Christian and Latter-day Saint readings of Psalm 72 as messianic prophecy and what remains is a recognizable ancient Near Eastern royal genre. The king is asked to "judge thy people with righteousness, and thy poor with judgment" (72:2), to "save the children of the needy" and "break in pieces the oppressor" (72:4), to rescue the one "that hath no helper" (72:12).

This is the standard advertisement of Mesopotamian kingship. The prologue and epilogue of the Laws of Hammurabi state that the laws were set up so that the strong should not oppress the weak and so that justice would be given to the orphan and the widow (see Martha T. Roth, Law Collections from Mesopotamia and Asia Minor, SBL Writings from the Ancient World, 1995). The same standard appears in Ugaritic narrative poetry: in the Kirta epic, Prince Yaṣṣib confronts his ailing father and tells him he has failed to judge the case of the widow and adjudicate the cause of the wretched — and therefore should step down (translations in Simon B. Parker, ed., Ugaritic Narrative Poetry, SBL Writings from the Ancient World 9, 1997). Psalm 72:8's "from sea to sea, and from the river unto the ends of the earth" belongs to the same idiom as the Assyrian royal boast of rule from the Upper Sea to the Lower Sea. A psalm modern readers hear as uniquely eschatological was, in its own century, an intelligible piece of court literature.

Asaph: a collection with a northern accent

Three psalms this week — 50, 77, 78 — carry the superscription "of Asaph." The Asaph psalms are Psalm 50 plus Psalms 73–83; Chronicles presents the Asaphites as a guild of temple singers (1 Chronicles 25). Harry P. Nasuti's Tradition History and the Psalms of Asaph (SBLDS 88, 1988) analyzed their shared vocabulary and concluded that the collection draws on traditions of a Northern or "Ephraimite" character — exodus and wilderness, Joseph, Shiloh, the divine shepherd — rather than the Zion-and-David complex dominant elsewhere.

You can feel it in Psalm 77:15, which speaks of "the sons of Jacob and Joseph" — an unusual pairing foregrounding the northern tribes. Psalm 77:16–20 then breaks into a storm theophany: waters that see God and writhe, thunder, arrows, a path through the sea. The passage is closely related to Habakkuk 3, and both are widely read as preserving old poetic material in which YHWH takes over the imagery of a West Semitic storm god confronting the sea — the pattern labeled Chaoskampf, from the Ugaritic Baal cycle where Baal fights Prince Yamm ("Sea") and Judge Nahar ("River"). Psalm 65:7, "which stilleth the noise of the seas," is the domesticated version of the same motif.

Psalm 50 as a lawsuit

Psalm 50 does not behave like a hymn. God summons heaven and earth (v. 4), convenes witnesses, states a charge, and delivers a verdict. Since Herbert B. Huffmon's "The Covenant Lawsuit in the Prophets" (Journal of Biblical Literature 78, 1959, 285–95), this pattern has been analyzed as the rîb — a covenant lawsuit form, with the deity acting as plaintiff and judge and the cosmos as jury, a structure with formal analogues in ancient treaty documents.

What God prosecutes is instructive: not failure to sacrifice but a misunderstanding of what sacrifice does. "If I were hungry, I would not tell thee" (50:12) strikes directly at the feeding model of offerings — which is precisely how sacrifice worked in much of Mesopotamian religion, where the gods ate. Psalm 51:16–17 pushes further: "thou desirest not sacrifice... the sacrifices of God are a broken spirit." This is the psalmic wing of a critique running through Amos 5:21–24, Hosea 6:6, Isaiah 1:11–17, and Micah 6:6–8.

And then Psalm 51:18–19 reverses it: rebuild Jerusalem's walls, and then "shall they offer bullocks upon thine altar." Most critical commentators, including Frank-Lothar Hossfeld and Erich Zenger in the Hermeneia volume Psalms 2: A Commentary on Psalms 51–100 (Fortress, 2005), treat these two verses as a later addition from the exilic or early post-exilic period, bringing a psalm suspicious of sacrifice back into line with the restored cult. A minority defends the psalm's unity and dates the whole of it to the exile. This is a live disagreement, not a settled point.

The superscriptions are the Bible's first commentary

Psalm 51's superscription places it after Nathan's confrontation over Bathsheba; Psalm 63's puts David "in the wilderness of Judah." Thirteen psalms carry such historical notes. Brevard S. Childs, in "Psalm Titles and Midrashic Exegesis" (Journal of Semitic Studies 16.2, 1971, 137–50), argued that these headings are a late interpretive layer — early midrash, essentially — produced by readers who matched psalm language to episodes in Samuel, and that their significance lies in showing how the psalms were being read, not in preserving authorship data.

Internal evidence supports the late dating here. Psalm 63:11 refers to "the king" in the third person, which sits awkwardly with a heading making the king its author. Psalm 51:11's plea "take not thy holy spirit from me" uses a phrase, rûaḥ qodšəkā, found in only one other place in the Hebrew Bible — Isaiah 63:10–11, from the exilic or post-exilic part of Isaiah. And Psalm 51:5, "in sin did my mother conceive me," reads in context as hyperbolic self-accusation of the sort common in penitential lament; the doctrine of original sin is a later Christian development read back onto the verse.

What the psalmists thought happened when you die

Psalm 49 is a wisdom poem, closer in temper to Ecclesiastes and Job than to the laments around it — it announces itself with the technical vocabulary of the sages, māšāl ("parable") and ḥîdāh ("dark saying," 49:4), and argues that wealth buys nothing at the grave.

Then verse 15: "But God will redeem my soul from the power of Sheol: for he shall receive me." The verb is lāqaḥ, "take" — the same verb used of Enoch in Genesis 5:24 and of Elijah in 2 Kings 2. Psalm 73:24 has the identical construction. Whether these lines assert a genuine hope of postmortem rescue or only rescue from premature death is one of the sharper divisions in the field. Philip S. Johnston's Shades of Sheol: Death and Afterlife in the Old Testament (Apollos/IVP, 2002) argues that Israelites were far less preoccupied with the underworld than some recent scholarship claims, and that a positive afterlife hope emerged late, out of the conviction that YHWH was the god of the living. On that reading, Psalm 86:13's "thou hast delivered my soul from the lowest Sheol" is a survivor's thanksgiving, not a report from beyond.

Where the Hebrew simply gives out

Parts of this block are textually broken, and English translations paper over it.

  • Psalm 49:11 in the Masoretic Text reads qirbām, "their inward part" — the KJV's "their inward thought is, that their houses shall continue for ever." The Septuagint, Targum, Vulgate, and Syriac all reflect qibrām, "their graves": their graves are their houses forever. Two consonants transposed. Ibn Ezra and Kimchi already noted it; most modern translations follow the versions.
  • Psalm 49:5's "the iniquity of my heels" (ʿăqēbay) is unintelligible as it stands; the LDS edition's gloss "persecutors" is one of a long list of guesses.
  • Psalm 65:1's "Praise waiteth for thee" translates ləkā dumiyyāh təhillāh, which more literally says "to you, silence is praise." The NIV flags the Hebrew as uncertain. Rashi took the paradox at face value: God's praises cannot be exhausted, so silence is the most eloquent form of them.

After Gunkel

Hermann Gunkel's Einleitung in die Psalmen (1933, completed by Joachim Begrich; English translation by James D. Nogalski, Introduction to Psalms: The Genres of the Religious Lyric of Israel, Mercer University Press, 1998) sorted the Psalter into genres — hymn, individual lament, communal lament, individual thanksgiving, royal psalm — each with a reconstructed setting in life. Sigmund Mowinckel then pushed the cultic side hard, proposing an annual autumn enthronement festival behind a large share of the collection. Both frameworks remain indispensable and both leak.

This week supplies the leaks. Psalm 66 is a communal hymn through v. 12 and then, without warning, switches to first-person singular vow-payment: "I will go into thy house with burnt offerings... Come and hear... and I will declare what he hath done for my soul" (66:13, 16). One psalm, two genres, one seam. Psalm 71 has no superscription at all — unusual here — and reads as an aged person's lament stitched from phrases found elsewhere (compare 71:1–3 with 31:1–3). Psalm 86 is the extreme case: a mosaic of borrowed lines, so much so that Franz Delitzsch's commentary observed that in it "almost everything is an echo of earlier language of the Psalms and of the Law." It is also the only psalm attributed to David in Book III — most likely a later insertion linking back to the Davidic collection that Psalm 72:20 declared closed.

Which returns us to Psalm 78, the block's longest and most contested text. Its climax is a polemic: God "refused the tabernacle of Joseph, and chose not the tribe of Ephraim: but chose the tribe of Judah, the mount Zion which he loved" (78:67–68), and then chose David. Richard J. Clifford, in "In Zion and David a New Beginning: An Interpretation of Psalm 78" (in Traditions in Transformation: Turning Points in Biblical Faith, ed. Baruch Halpern and Jon D. Levenson, Eisenbrauns, 1981), read the psalm as two parallel recitals of the national story converging on the claim that Zion and the Davidic house are the completion of the exodus. The dating is unresolved: Antony F. Campbell argued for the tenth century in "Psalm 78: A Contribution to the Theology of Tenth Century Israel" (Catholic Biblical Quarterly 41, 1979, 51–79); Philip D. Stern re-argued an eighth-century date in "The Eighth Century Dating of Psalm 78 Re-argued" (Hebrew Union College Annual 66, 1995, 41–65); others place its core under Hezekiah or Josiah, when a Judahite monarchy had reason to explain to northern refugees why Shiloh had fallen and Jerusalem stood. What is not in doubt is that Psalm 78 is an argument, made by someone, against someone, about who legitimately holds the sanctuary.
