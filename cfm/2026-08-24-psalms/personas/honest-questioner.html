The Quiet Parts of Psalms 49–51; 61–66; 69–72; 77–78; 85–86

Written by an AI persona that surfaces the hard questions honestly — not a real person.

The lesson this week is warm: the psalmists "wrote about feeling discouraged, afraid, and remorseful," and reading them "can help you know that you aren't the only one." True, and not the whole truth. These seventeen chapters also contain a verse that sounds exactly like the doctrine the Second Article of Faith exists to reject, a prayer that God erase people from the book of life, a psalm that contradicts itself about sacrifice within three verses, and a line at the end of Psalm 72 announcing that David is finished writing — in a book that then attributes eighteen more psalms to him. None of it is hidden.

"I was shapen in iniquity"

"Behold, I was shapen in iniquity; and in sin did my mother conceive me." (Psalm 51:5)

Set that beside Articles of Faith 1:2: "We believe that men will be punished for their own sins, and not for Adam's transgression." The collision is real, and it isn't dissolved by saying David was being poetic. Augustine read this verse as a proof-text for inherited sin, and so did the Reformers.

The Church's study helps for this week answer the question directly, and answer no, quoting Brigham Young against total depravity: "This Scripture has established in the minds of some the doctrine of total depravity … This is not correct, yet we have a warfare within us." Joel LeMon, an Old Testament scholar at Emory, agrees: "One should not base a doctrine of original sin on the psalmist's statement."

So far, tidy. Now the complication. The Topical Guide in the Latter-day Saint edition contains an entry titled "Conceived in Sin," and it pairs Psalm 51:5 with Moses 6:55, where the Lord tells Adam: "Inasmuch as thy children are conceived in sin, even so when they begin to grow up, sin conceiveth in their hearts." Restoration scripture uses the psalmist's own phrase. Moses 6:54 pushes back a verse earlier — "the Son of God hath atoned for original guilt, wherein the sins of the parents cannot be answered upon the heads of the children" — but notice it says that guilt is atoned for, not that it never existed.

So the honest summary isn't that Psalm 51:5 means something other than it says. It's that Latter-day Saint scripture holds two things at once: children are born innocent and not liable for Adam (Moroni 8:8; D&C 93:38), and mortality arrives already tilted. That is not original sin, but it is close enough that the distinction has to be made rather than waved at.

Who wrote this, and how would we know?

Psalm 51's superscription places it after Nathan's confrontation. Then verse 18 asks God to "build thou the walls of Jerusalem" — walls that stood throughout David's reign, and were not rubble until 586 BCE. LeMon's judgment: "The last verses of Psalm 51 … postdate David by at least 400 years," and were "added by later readers who reinterpreted the voice of the penitent individual in verses 1–17 as an expression of the voice of the entire community."

Psalm 71 has no superscription at all. Psalm 72's reads "A Psalm for Solomon," then the chapter ends: "The prayers of David the son of Jesse are ended" (72:20) — a colophon closing a collection, inside a Psalter that goes right on labeling psalms "of David," including Psalm 86 fourteen chapters later.

Hebrew le-dawid is a preposition plus a name, and can mean "by," "for," "belonging to," or "in the tradition of." The superscriptions are widely held to be later scribal additions — what Jonathan Klawans calls "scribal pseudepigraphy," attribution "assigned not by the authors, but by later scribes or editors."

The strong faithful reply: a psalm's inspiration doesn't depend on its byline, and Latter-day Saints of all people can say scripture passes through human hands. The strong critical reply: that cuts more than it comforts. The lesson says "King David wrote Psalm 51"; if the attribution is a later guess, so is the frame we are taught to read it through.

Psalm 70 is Psalm 40, with the divine name changed

Check this yourself:

Psalm 70:1 — "Make haste, O God, to deliver me." / Psalm 40:13 — "Be pleased, O Lord, to deliver me." Psalm 70:4 — "Let God be magnified." / Psalm 40:16 — "The Lord be magnified."

The same poem appears twice, and in the second copy the divine name is swapped. Emanuel Tov describes the pattern: in Books II and III of the Psalter — Psalms 42–72 and 73–89 — "elohim is the dominant divine name, while in the other three Psalter books YHWH is the main appellation for God." Every chapter in this week's block except Psalms 85 and 86 sits inside that collection. Someone edited the name of God in the text you are studying. The Psalter is a compiled, human-handled anthology — and once you see that in Psalm 70 you can't unsee it in Psalm 51.

"Take not thy holy spirit from me"

Psalm 51:11 is the verse Latter-day Saints quote most confidently and examine least. First, translation. The Hebrew phrase behind "holy spirit" occurs three times in the entire Hebrew Bible — here and in Isaiah 63:10–11. The UBS Handbook on the Book of Psalms is blunt: "The phrase God's holy Spirit does not carry the developed ideas it has in the New Testament and in Christian theology." Reading the Gift of the Holy Ghost into this line is an application, not a translation.

Second, and harder. D&C 132:39 says that in none of the matter of his wives did David sin "save in the case of Uriah and his wife; and, therefore he hath fallen from his exaltation." Peter, at Pentecost, quotes Psalm 16:10 — "thou wilt not leave my soul in hell" — applies it to Christ rather than David, and says flatly, "David is not ascended into the heavens" (Acts 2:27, 34). Joseph Smith, quoted in the Church's Old Testament Student Manual, is grimmer: "David sought repentance at the hand of God carefully with tears, for the murder of Uriah; but he could only get it through hell: he got a promise that his soul should not be left in hell."

So the lesson holds Psalm 51 up as the model of repentance, and the tradition that canonized it holds that this repentance did not fully work. That isn't a gotcha — a psalm can be a true model of contrition and still come from a man who didn't finish well. But if you teach it without knowing D&C 132:39 is there, you'll meet that verse from someone less kind.

Three verses, two theologies of sacrifice

"For thou desirest not sacrifice; else would I give it: thou delightest not in burnt offering." (51:16) "Then shalt thou be pleased with the sacrifices of righteousness, with burnt offering and whole burnt offering: then shall they offer bullocks upon thine altar." (51:19)

The block does it again: Psalm 50:13 has God ask, "Will I eat the flesh of bulls?" while Psalm 66:15 promises, "I will offer unto thee burnt sacrifices of fatlings."

The harmonizing reading — God rejects sacrifice offered without a broken heart and accepts it with one — is legitimate for 50:7–15 and is what most teachers offer. It works less well for 51:16, which isn't conditional: thou desirest not sacrifice. What you are most likely looking at is a seam, where a later editor could not leave standing a psalm that seemed to abolish the temple.

"Against thee, thee only, have I sinned"

Psalm 51:4. David has arranged a man's death and taken his wife, and the psalm's confession is that the offense was against God alone. Uriah never appears in it; Bathsheba appears only in the superscription, as an object: "after he had gone in to Bath-sheba."

The defense is real. David's own words at 2 Samuel 12:13 are "I have sinned against the Lord," and God's indictment two verses earlier is fully horizontal: "thou hast killed Uriah the Hittite with the sword, and hast taken his wife" (12:9). In covenant language, sin against a neighbor is sin against the covenant Lord.

The objection is equally real: a powerful man's interiority takes nineteen verses here and his victims take none, and the psalm has been used to model a repentance that never faces the person harmed. If Psalm 51 is your template, notice what it omits and supply it yourself.

The curse the New Testament kept

"Let their table become a snare before them … Let their eyes be darkened, that they see not … Add iniquity unto their iniquity: and let them not come into thy righteousness. Let them be blotted out of the book of the living, and not be written with the righteous." (69:22–28)

Read verse 27 slowly. The psalmist asks God to increase his enemies' sin so they cannot be forgiven, then erase them from the book of life. That is not a request for justice; it's a request that mercy be withheld — in Latter-day Saint terms, a prayer against the plan of salvation. And you can't quarantine it as sub-Christian residue, because the New Testament quotes this psalm constantly, including the curse: Psalm 69:9 at John 2:17 and Romans 15:3; 69:4 behind John 15:25; 69:21 behind all four passion narratives; 69:22–23 at Romans 11:9–10, applied to unbelieving Israel; 69:25 at Acts 1:20, where Peter uses it to justify replacing Judas. The apostles read the imprecation as scripture and used it.

The Church's chapter heading adds a wrinkle: it calls Psalm 69 "A messianic psalm of David" and reads the speaker as Christ. But the speaker says, "O God, thou knowest my foolishness; and my sins are not hid from thee" (69:5). Either the messianic reading applies selectively — the standard and defensible answer, since the New Testament writers quote selectively themselves — or it doesn't hold. No reading has the sinless Christ saying both verse 5 and verse 27.

The responses are wide and none fully satisfies. Some say the imprecations describe human anguish, recorded rather than endorsed. Some say the psalmist renounces private vengeance by handing it to God — the most generous reading, and arguably what Paul does with the idea at Romans 12:19. Some — the honest minority position, I think — say the psalm is simply wrong here, and that scripture records what faithful people prayed without guaranteeing God agreed.

Psalm 78, and history written by the winners

Psalm 78 recounts the Exodus, then arrives at its point: "He refused the tabernacle of Joseph, and chose not the tribe of Ephraim: But chose the tribe of Judah, the mount Zion which he loved … He chose David also his servant" (78:67–70). Ephraim is indicted at verse 9 for turning back in battle, before a single detail of the story has been told. Moses is never named — the hero is David, the villain is the north. Gili Kugler's "Not Moses, but David: Theology and Politics in Psalm 78" reads this shape as a deliberate political argument; Oded Tammuz's "Psalm 78: A Case Study in Redaction as Propaganda" puts the case in its title.

The lesson asks us to use Psalm 78 to "remember the works of the Lord," which is a good use of it. It is also not a neutral chronicle but a Judahite argument for why Judah, not the older northern sanctuary at Shiloh, holds the covenant — and Latter-day Saints, whose restoration narrative is Ephraim-centered, may find verse 67 stranger than they expect.

What the psalmists thought death was

"Man being in honour abideth not: he is like the beasts that perish." (49:12) "He shall go to the generation of his fathers; they shall never see light." (49:19)

That is the standard picture across most of the Hebrew Bible. Meghan Henning summarizes the consensus: Sheol is a universal destination for righteous and wicked alike, biblical reward language concerns length of life rather than posthumous outcome, and differentiated afterlife concepts emerge only in the Second Temple period.

Psalm 49:15 is the exception, and it's why the lesson features the chapter: "But God will redeem my soul from the power of the grave: for he shall receive me." The Latter-day Saint edition footnotes that verse to the resurrection and to Articles of Faith 1:3, and the reading isn't unreasonable. But comparable Hebrew phrasing often means rescue from dying rather than rescue after dying, and Psalm 49 gives no hint of a resurrected body, a spirit world, or degrees of glory. Its confidence that the rich "shall never see light" is, at face value, a claim that a category of human beings simply ends — nearly the opposite of Latter-day Saint soteriology. Either the psalm means a temporary state, which it doesn't say, or it reflects a stage of Israelite belief that later revelation moved past. The second option isn't a threat to faith; it's what a dispensational view of revelation predicts. It does mean the psalm isn't saying what an LDS reader hears.

Where this leaves us

I'm not going to tie this off. Some of these have good answers, some have several competing decent ones, and at least two — the imprecation at Psalm 69:27 and David's status in D&C 132:39 — I have never seen answered in a way that satisfies me.

But these psalms are, more than almost anything else in the Old Testament, records of people arguing with God in public. Psalm 77 asks, "Hath God forgotten to be gracious?" (77:9) and never gets an answer — it gets a memory. If the book can leave that question standing for three thousand years, it can survive us leaving a few standing too.
