Ezekiel in the Restoration Canon

Written by an AI persona connecting the block to Restoration scripture and modern teaching — not a real person.

Restoration scripture names Ezekiel and reads him directly. D&C 29:21 speaks of a judgment "spoken by the mouth of Ezekiel the prophet"; D&C 138:43 places him in the spirit world as the one "shown in vision the great valley of dry bones"; D&C 27:5 borrows his "stick of Ephraim." The Book of Mormon keeps returning to his shepherds, watchmen, new hearts, and gathered records. Sometimes the later scripture adds to Ezekiel; sometimes it repeats him almost word for word. Both kinds of connection are worth tracing, including where they are hard.

Two Prophets, One Decade, One Throne

The Book of Mormon opens a few years before Ezekiel 1, and the openings rhyme. Lehi lived in Jerusalem "in the commencement of the first year of the reign of Zedekiah," when "there came many prophets" (1 Nephi 1:4). He "saw the heavens open, and he thought he saw God sitting upon his throne" (1 Nephi 1:8). Ezekiel, deported with Jehoiachin and dating his vision to "the fifth year of king Jehoiachin's captivity" (Ezekiel 1:2), writes that "the heavens were opened, and I saw visions of God" (1:1), and later he sees "the likeness of a throne" with "the likeness as the appearance of a man above upon it" (1:26). Lehi's "he thought he saw" hedges the way Ezekiel's stacked "likeness" and "appearance" do: real, and beyond exact description.

Next comes the book. Lehi is handed one "and bade him that he should read," and its contents are "Wo, wo, unto Jerusalem" (1 Nephi 1:11–13). Ezekiel is handed "a roll of a book… written within and without: and there was written therein lamentations, and mourning, and woe" (Ezekiel 2:9–10), and he is told to eat it. It is "in my mouth as honey for sweetness" (3:3), and then he goes "in bitterness, in the heat of my spirit" (3:14). John repeats the pattern (Revelation 10:9–10), and the Doctrine and Covenants explains John's eaten book: "it was a mission, and an ordinance, for him to gather the tribes of Israel" (D&C 77:14).

Read back into Ezekiel 2–3, that definition makes the eaten scroll a commission, not only an experience: the prophet takes the message into his body before he takes it to Israel, and tastes woe as honey. Lehi, too, reads a book of woe and ends by praising God "because thou art merciful" (1 Nephi 1:14). Judgment and mercy come in the same book.

The Watchman and the Blood on the Garments

The watchman charge (3:17–21; 33:1–9) is one of the hardest passages in the block: if the watchman does not warn, "his blood will I require at thine hand" (3:18; 33:8). The Book of Mormon applies it to an office. Jacob says he and Joseph magnified their office by "answering the sins of the people upon our own heads if we did not teach them the word of God with all diligence; wherefore, by laboring with our might their blood might not come upon our garments" (Jacob 1:19).

Latter-day revelation spreads the duty beyond prophets: "it becometh every man who hath been warned to warn his neighbor. Therefore, they are left without excuse, and their sins are upon their own heads" (D&C 88:81–82). Ezekiel had one watchman for the house of Israel; here everyone warned becomes a watchman to someone else.

Elder Ezra Taft Benson opened his April 1973 address "Watchman, Warn the Wicked" with Ezekiel 3:17 and described Church leaders as "watchmen on the tower of Zion" whose duty is "to speak out against current evils," adding, "we must not permit fear of criticism to keep us from doing our duty." Yet the text also limits the watchman: he is responsible for the warning, not the response. If the wicked man "turn not from his wickedness," God tells the watchman, "thou hast delivered thy soul" (3:19). No one answers for another's agency, only for staying silent.

"Turn Ye, Turn Ye": Ezekiel's Logic in the Doctrine and Covenants

To the exiles' despair, "how should we then live?" (33:10), God answers with an oath and an invitation: "I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked… turn ye, turn ye from your evil ways; for why will ye die, O house of Israel?" (33:11). Then comes a principle the hearers call unfair ("The way of the Lord is not equal," 33:17): a righteous person's past righteousness "shall not be remembered" if he turns to iniquity (33:13). The wicked person who turns and "restore[s] the pledge" will find that "none of his sins that he hath committed shall be mentioned unto him" (33:15–16).

The Doctrine and Covenants restates both halves of this ruling. On forgiveness: "he who has repented of his sins, the same is forgiven, and I, the Lord, remember them no more" (D&C 58:42). On relapse: "unto that soul who sinneth shall the former sins return" (D&C 82:7). Together they are Ezekiel 33:12–16 compressed: God judges where a person now faces, not the sum of the past. And as Ezekiel's penitent must "restore the pledge," D&C 58:43 requires that sins be confessed and forsaken.

The standard is a changed person, not a balance sheet. Elder D. Todd Christofferson's "Born Again" (April 2008) adds a qualification Ezekiel's courtroom language lacks: "Being born again, unlike our physical birth, is more a process than an event." The "day that he turneth" (33:12) may be a direction held for years.

Shepherds Who Feed Themselves

Ezekiel 34 indicts Israel's rulers in specific terms: "Ye eat the fat, and ye clothe you with the wool… but ye feed not the flock" (34:3), and they have not healed the sick, bound the broken, or sought the lost (34:4). God takes back the job and reverses each failure: "I will seek that which was lost, and bring again that which was driven away, and will bind up that which was broken, and will strengthen that which was sick" (34:16).

Elder Gerrit W. Gong's "Ministering" (April 2023) asks ward councils, elders quorums, and Relief Societies to "hearken to the Good Shepherd and help Him" do exactly those four things, and his notes add that Ezekiel 34 "sternly warns the shepherds of Israel." Ezekiel's target was kings and priests; Gong's reading spreads the shepherd's duty to every member, as D&C 88:81 spreads the watchman's.

Then there is the promise of "one shepherd… even my servant David" (34:23; 37:24). In context this is a restored Davidic king over a reunited Israel ("one king shall be king to them all," 37:22). The Book of Mormon reads "one shepherd" as Christ. Nephi foresees records "established in one; for there is one God and one Shepherd over all the earth" (1 Nephi 13:41). He also says, "there shall be one fold and one shepherd; and he shall feed his sheep, and in him they shall find pasture" (1 Nephi 22:25), which echoes Ezekiel's "good pasture" (34:14). Alma adds the sharp edge of Ezekiel 34. The shepherd calls "in his own name… which is the name of Christ," and anyone who will not answer is asked, "of what fold are ye?" (Alma 5:38–39). A Davidic prince over a restored people and a Son of David over all the sheep can be read as one promise at two scales.

A Heart of Flesh

"Then will I sprinkle clean water upon you… A new heart also will I give you… and I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh… And I will put my spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes" (36:25–27). The order matters: water, then a new heart, then the Spirit, then obedience. God acts first.

The Book of Mormon gives this a name and a liturgy. King Benjamin's people report that the Spirit "has wrought a mighty change in us, or in our hearts, that we have no more disposition to do evil" (Mosiah 5:2). Alma asks the members of the Church, "Have ye experienced this mighty change in your hearts?" and then, "can ye feel so now?" (Alma 5:14, 26). The Pearl of Great Price maps the same elements onto the plan of salvation: Adam is told that "by the water ye keep the commandment; by the Spirit ye are justified, and by the blood ye are sanctified" (Moses 6:59–60). Ezekiel's sprinkled water and indwelling spirit become, in Restoration theology, baptism and the gift of the Holy Ghost.

The passage also has a hard frame. It opens with "I do not this for your sakes… but for mine holy name's sake" (36:22) and closes with "be ashamed and confounded for your own ways" (36:32). Restoration scripture does not soften this, but it relocates the name. After the mighty change, Benjamin's people are told, "ye shall be called the children of Christ… for ye say that your hearts are changed through faith on his name" (Mosiah 5:7). In Ezekiel, God restores Israel to vindicate his name among the nations; in Mosiah, the changed people receive the name themselves, and acting for the name's sake and for the people's sake become the same act.

Can These Bones Live?

Ezekiel 37:11 interprets its own vision: "these bones are the whole house of Israel: behold, they say, Our bones are dried, and our hope is lost." First, then, it is national revival, answering the despair of 33:10. Restoration scripture adds a literal reading. D&C 138:43 says Ezekiel saw bones "which were to be clothed upon with flesh, to come forth again in the resurrection of the dead, living souls." The Bible Dictionary entry "Ezekiel" holds both readings together: chapter 37 depicts the bones "coming together, bone to his bone, in the Resurrection, the restoration of Israel."

The Book of Mormon's resurrection language reads like a description of 37:7–8: "every limb and joint shall be restored to its body; yea, even a hair of the head shall not be lost" (Alma 40:23). Ezekiel's two-stage scene fits that body-and-spirit doctrine closely. First the bones, sinews, and skin come together, "but there was no breath in them" (37:8). Then the ruach comes "from the four winds" (37:9). In Jacob's terms, "the spirit and the body is restored to itself again" (2 Nephi 9:13).

Elder Russell M. Nelson's "The Gathering of Scattered Israel" (October 2006), which cites Ezekiel 37:21–22, frames the gathering as reaching "both sides of the veil." So "I will open your graves" (37:12) works on three levels: exiles brought home, the dead raised, and, for Latter-day Saints, the dead reached by temple ordinances.

The Two Sticks

No verses in the block carry more Latter-day Saint weight than 37:16–17. The Bible Dictionary entry "Ephraim, stick of" calls it "a prophetic reference to the Book of Mormon" which, joined to "the stick of Judah (the Bible)," forms "a unified, complementary testimony." D&C 27:5 speaks of Moroni, to whom the Lord "committed the keys of the record of the stick of Ephraim."

The JST appendix in the Latter-day Saint Bible reprints no passages from Ezekiel; the relevant Joseph Smith Translation material is Genesis 50, where Joseph of Egypt prophesies: "the fruit of thy loins shall write, and the fruit of the loins of Judah shall write; and that which shall be written… shall grow together, unto the confounding of false doctrines" (JST Genesis 50:31; repeated in 2 Nephi 3:12). Joseph supplies the two writings; Ezekiel supplies the image of them joined in one hand. The Nephite record makes the gathering of books and the gathering of people one promise: "my people… shall be gathered home… and my word also shall be gathered in one" (2 Nephi 29:14). President Nelson put it most strongly in his "Hope of Israel" youth devotional (June 3, 2018): "The Book of Mormon is central to the gathering of Israel. In fact, if there were no Book of Mormon, the promised gathering of Israel would not occur."

Two qualifications. First, the text's own explanation (37:21–22) is political: "they shall be no more two nations," north (Joseph/Ephraim) and south (Judah) reunited under one king. Second, the "writing board" reading the lesson mentions has a specific Latter-day Saint history. Keith Meservy argued in the Ensign ("Ezekiel's Sticks and the Gathering of Israel," February 1987, building on a September 1977 article) that Hebrew ʻets here means wooden writing boards. He pointed to Babylonian references to wooden tablets and to hinged wax-covered boards that Max Mallowan excavated in Assyria. Brian E. Keck answered in "Ezekiel 37, Sticks, and Babylonian Writing Boards: A Critical Reappraisal" (Dialogue 23, no. 1, 1990) and challenged that reading. The doctrinal claim does not hang on that dispute; it rests on Joseph's and Nephi's prophecies, through which Ezekiel's sign-act of reunion becomes a sign that Israel will be reunited partly through its records.

Water from Under the Threshold

The lesson points to Elder Dale G. Renlund's "Family History and Temple Work: Sealing and Healing" (April 2018), which quotes Ezekiel 47:8–9 and notes two features of the river: "though the small stream had no tributaries, it grew into a mighty river," like sealing blessings that grow "backward and forward through the generations," and "the river renewed everything that it touched." His central story reaches back to chapter 36 too: his former patient Rod knelt at a sealing altar in the St. George Utah Temple as proxy for Todd, the young man "whose heart was still beating inside Rod's chest." A replaced heart and a healing river meet in one act of vicarious temple work.

The Lord promises that "in the barren deserts there shall come forth pools of living water" (D&C 133:29). Nephi learns that "the fountain of living waters, or… the tree of life" represents "the love of God" (1 Nephi 11:25). That joins Ezekiel's river to Ezekiel's trees, "whose leaf shall not fade… and the leaf thereof for medicine" (47:12), which John later sets beside "the throne of God and of the Lamb" (Revelation 22:1–2). The keys of the gathering that Ezekiel 36–37 promises were restored inside a temple. In the Kirtland Temple, Moses "committed unto us the keys of the gathering of Israel from the four parts of the earth" (D&C 110:11).

Two details keep the vision from becoming sentimental. The "miry places… shall not be healed; they shall be given to salt" (47:11). Even this river does not heal ground that will not take it in, perhaps the same agency that limits the watchman. And the land is divided "to the strangers that sojourn among you… they shall have inheritance with you among the tribes of Israel" (47:22). That fits President Nelson's statement in "Hope of Israel" that "every one of our Heavenly Father's children, on both sides of the veil, deserves to hear the message of the restored gospel." The river runs out to the dead, to the stranger, and to the Dead Sea itself.
