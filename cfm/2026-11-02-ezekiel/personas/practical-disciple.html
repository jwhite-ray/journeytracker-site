Sit Where They Sit, Then Speak: Ezekiel for an Ordinary Week

Written by an AI persona on living the week's text — not a real person.

Few of us will see wheels full of eyes or eat a scroll. But this week's chapters keep handing the reader ordinary jobs: watching, warning, feeding, telling the truth, waiting through a dry stretch, wading in. Nearly every picture comes with a question you can take to work, to the dinner table, or to your ministering list.

Seven days of saying nothing

Before Ezekiel gets the watchman charge, he gets a week of silence. "I came to them of the captivity at Tel-abib... and I sat where they sat, and remained there astonished among them seven days" (3:15). Only "at the end of seven days" does the word of the LORD come (3:16).

The word translated "astonished," mashmim, comes from a root meaning to be desolated or appalled (Bible Hub interlinear, Ezekiel 3:15). The same root later describes the land itself, laid "most desolate" (33:28–29) and lying "desolate in the sight of all that passed by" (36:34). Before saying anything to the exiles, Ezekiel takes on their condition.

The order matters. A watchman who has never sat where the people sit will usually warn them about the wrong things, in the wrong tone. Whom am I trying to correct whom I have never simply sat with? When did I last spend time with a struggling family member or ministering assignment without steering the visit toward a lesson? Am I willing to be "astonished" by someone's situation, instead of arriving already sure what is wrong?

The watchman's two failures

The watchman passage appears twice (3:17–21; 33:1–9), and its logic is blunt. If the watchman "seeth the sword come, and blow not the trumpet... his blood will I require at the watchman's hand" (33:6). If he warns and the people ignore him, "thou hast delivered thy soul" (33:9).

The Restoration extends the charge to ordinary members: "it becometh every man who hath been warned to warn his neighbor" (D&C 88:81). There are two ways to fail, and most of us lean toward one.

The first is silence. A friend's drinking is getting worse. A coworker is rounding numbers in a way that will end in a lawsuit. You see the sword coming and do not blow the trumpet, because it would be awkward, because you might be wrong, because it is "a time of peace." That phrase is from the parable in D&C 101:44–54, where the servants stop building the watchtower and ask, "What need hath my lord of this tower, seeing this is a time of peace?" (101:48). The tower has to be built in quiet years, exactly when it feels least necessary.

The second failure is less discussed, and Ezekiel's own story addresses it. Right after his commission, God makes the prophet mute: "I will make thy tongue cleave to the roof of thy mouth, that thou shalt be dumb, and shalt not be to them a reprover... But when I speak with thee, I will open thy mouth" (3:26–27). The watchman is not a running commentary on everyone's faults. He speaks "from me" (3:17; 33:7), when the Lord opens his mouth. A parent who warns about everything is eventually heard about nothing.

The passage also holds a mercy for anxious people. The watchman is responsible for the warning, not the response: "If thou warn the wicked, and he turn not... thou hast delivered thy soul" (3:19). You cannot make your adult child choose well. You can say, clearly and in love, what you see, and then let go of an outcome you never controlled. Is there one warning I have owed someone for months that I could give this week, privately and once? Is there someone I warn so often that my voice has become noise?

"Restore the pledge"

The exiles think it is too late: "If our transgressions and our sins be upon us, and we pine away in them, how should we then live?" (33:10). God's answer is a plea: "I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked... turn ye, turn ye from your evil ways; for why will ye die, O house of Israel?" (33:11).

And turning, when Ezekiel describes it, is concrete: "If the wicked restore the pledge, give again that he had robbed, walk in the statutes of life... he shall surely live" (33:15). A pledge was collateral taken from a borrower. Repentance here means giving back what you took. Most of us hold things that are not ours: credit for someone else's work, hours billed and not worked, a borrowed sum that quietly became ours. What do I need to give back, and to whom?

The other half of the passage warns the long-faithful: if the righteous man "trust to his own righteousness, and commit iniquity, all his righteousnesses shall not be remembered" (33:13). One version is treating past service as a credit balance: thirty years of callings, so this shortcut at work or this grudge against my brother does not count. Ezekiel says it counts. Each person is judged "after his ways" (33:20), by where they are going now, not where they used to be.

The lovely song

The most uncomfortable verse this week may be 33:32: "thou art unto them as a very lovely song of one that hath a pleasant voice, and can play well on an instrument: for they hear thy words, but they do them not."

The people are not hostile. They are eager, saying to one another, "Come, I pray you, and hear what is the word that cometh forth from the LORD" (33:30). They "sit before thee as my people" (33:31). The Hebrew word behind the KJV's "much love" (33:31) and "very lovely song" (33:32) is ʻagabim, which occurs only in these two verses and carries the sense of sensual or amorous love, a love song (Bible Hub, Strong's 5690). Modern translations render 33:32 as "a sensual song" (NET) or "lustful songs" (ESV). The prophet has become entertainment, enjoyed like a well-sung ballad, and then everyone goes home and their "heart goeth after their covetousness" (33:31).

This is a temptation for anyone reading a weekly deep-study guide. It is possible to love scripture study the way one loves a good podcast: the insight, the Hebrew word, the clever connection. Ezekiel's audience had all of that and changed nothing. James puts it plainly: "be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving your own selves" (James 1:22; compare the two sons in Matthew 21:28–31). A simple test: before you close the chapter, write down one thing you will do differently by Saturday. If you cannot name one, you may have been listening to a lovely song.

Shepherds who feed themselves

Chapter 34 is addressed to Israel's leaders, but it fits anyone with a flock, from a family to a ministering list. The shepherds "eat the fat" and "clothe you with the wool" (34:3), taking what the flock produces. Then comes the list of what they did not do:

The diseased have ye not strengthened, neither have ye healed that which was sick, neither have ye bound up that which was broken, neither have ye brought again that which was driven away, neither have ye sought that which was lost; but with force and with cruelty have ye ruled them. (34:4)

The list describes different kinds of need. The lesson asks: "What's the difference between a sheep who is 'lost' and one who is 'driven away'?" (34:16). In Hebrew, the lost one (haʼobedet) is the one who wandered off or is perishing; the driven-away one (hanniddachat) is the one scattered or banished (Bible Hub interlinear, Ezekiel 34:16). One drifted; the other was pushed. A relative who quietly stopped coming to church and one who left after being shamed in a class will not come back the same way. The first may need to be found. The second may need someone to repair what was done.

The phrase "with force and with cruelty" also deserves a look. The word translated "cruelty," perek, occurs only six times in the Hebrew Bible. Twice it describes how Egypt made Israel serve "with rigour" (Exodus 1:13–14), and three times the law of Leviticus 25 forbids Israelites to rule over one another that way (Leviticus 25:43, 46, 53). Ezekiel 34:4 is the sixth (Bible Hub, Strong's 6531). So the charge is not only neglect. The shepherds treated God's people the way Pharaoh did. A parent or leader who rules by intimidation has, in Ezekiel's vocabulary, become a taskmaster.

Ezekiel's shepherds could probably have produced a fine report of visits. What they skipped was the five verbs: strengthen, heal, bind up, bring back, seek. That is a usable checklist for any ministering assignment, and the lesson pairs the chapter with Elder Gerrit W. Gong's "Ministering" (April 2023) and John 21:15–17.

Then the chapter turns to the flock itself: "Behold, I judge between cattle and cattle" (34:17). Some of the sheep are the problem. They eat the good pasture and "tread down with your feet the residue"; they drink the clear water and "foul the residue with your feet" (34:18). They "thrust with side and with shoulder, and pushed all the diseased with your horns" (34:21). Most of us are not bishops, but all of us are sheep. Do I dominate the conversation in class, so the quieter person never speaks? Do I share a grievance in the hall that fouls the water for someone newer? Do I push past the weak in the way I joke, compete, or schedule?

A heart that can feel

The lesson's title comes from 36:26: "A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you: and I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you an heart of flesh."

A stone heart is a heart that does not register what it should, that can hear a lovely song and stay unmoved. A heart of flesh can be hurt. That is the point, and it is not comfortable. Verse 31 describes what happens after the new heart arrives: "Then shall ye remember your own evil ways... and shall lothe yourselves in your own sight." In the text, that painful memory comes after the cleansing (36:25), not before it. For someone in the middle of repentance who is suddenly sick about the past, the pain may be a sign the new heart is working, not proof that they are beyond help.

Notice also who does the work. Every verb is God's: "I will sprinkle... I will give... I will take away... I will put my spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes" (36:25–27). And God insists twice that he is not doing it because Israel earned it: "I do not this for your sakes" (36:22, 32). Discipline matters, but no one performs his own heart transplant. The practical step is in 36:37: "I will yet for this be inquired of." Ask, specifically and repeatedly.

When the bones are very dry

The lesson reads the valley of dry bones (37:1–14) as the gathering of Israel on both sides of the veil. But verse 11 also records what the exiles felt: "Our bones are dried, and our hope is lost: we are cut off for our parts." Anyone who has been through depression or a faith crisis will recognize the language. Three details are worth carrying into a dry season.

First, Ezekiel's answer to "can these bones live?" is "O Lord GOD, thou knowest" (37:3). He does not pretend to optimism, and he does not say no. That is an honest prayer for a hard week.

Second, the revival happens in stages. Sinews, flesh, and skin cover the bones, "but there was no breath in them" (37:8). Many of us know this stage: you are back at church, reading again, going through the motions, but there is no breath yet. The vision suggests this is not failure. It is the middle.

Third, the breath comes when Ezekiel does what he is told: "Prophesy unto the wind" (37:9). The Hebrew ruach means breath, wind, and spirit, and the vision plays on all three. The disciple's part in a dry season is often small: keep speaking the word to bones that cannot yet hear it, including your own.

Ankle-deep first

The river from the temple (47:1–12) has a practical detail: the measuring. A man with a line measures "a thousand cubits," and the water is "to the ankles"; another thousand, "to the knees"; another, "to the loins"; another, and "it was a river that I could not pass over" (47:3–5). Ezekiel is walked through at each stage. He does not start in the deep part.

Elder Dale G. Renlund, in the talk the lesson cites, observed that "though the small stream had no tributaries, it grew into a mighty river," and that "the river renewed everything that it touched" (Renlund, "Family History and Temple Work: Sealing and Healing," April 2018). He applied it to temple and family history work: "Temple blessings can heal hearts and lives and families."

The measured stages are permission to start small. A family that has never done family history does not need a four-generation project. Find one name. Start ankle-deep. The water rises.

One verse is easy to read past: "But the miry places thereof and the marshes thereof shall not be healed; they shall be given to salt" (47:11). Some read this as judgment, some as provision; the nineteenth-century Pulpit Commentary records a reading, which it traces to the German scholar Smend, that these marshes are left alone "on account of the excellent salt which they furnished" (Pulpit Commentary on Ezekiel 47:11, via Bible Hub). Either way, the vision does not promise that every corner becomes fresh water. Not every problem in your family will be resolved this side of the veil, and that is no reason to stop wading in. The trees on the bank still bear "new fruit according to his months," with "the leaf thereof for medicine" (47:12): ordinary, recurring nourishment rather than one dramatic harvest, which is what discipleship looks like most weeks.
