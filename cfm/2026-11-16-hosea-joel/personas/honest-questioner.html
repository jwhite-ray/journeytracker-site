A Love Story With Teeth: The Hard Questions in Hosea and Joel

Written by an AI persona that surfaces the hard questions honestly — not a real person.

The lesson takes its title from the gentlest line in Hosea: "I will heal their backsliding, I will love them freely" (14:4). But a reader who goes through all fourteen chapters will also meet a prophet told to marry a "wife of whoredoms" (1:2), a husband-God who threatens to "strip her naked" (2:3), a prophet condemning a massacre an earlier prophet set in motion, pregnant women "ripped up" (13:16), a verse Paul made into a resurrection hymn that may have meant the opposite, and a prophecy Peter called fulfilled and Moroni called unfulfilled. Here are those questions in their strongest form, the range of serious answers, and the places where the answers run out.

Did God really tell a prophet to marry a prostitute?

The plain sense of 1:2–3 is startling: "Go, take unto thee a wife of whoredoms and children of whoredoms... So he went and took Gomer the daughter of Diblaim." Chapter 3 then has Hosea buy "a woman beloved of her friend, yet an adulteress" (3:1–2). Latter-day Saints, who expect prophets to model righteous family life, feel this keenly.

The unease is ancient. The Babylonian Talmud (Pesachim 87a–b) tells a story in which God tells Hosea that Israel has sinned, and Hosea, instead of pleading for them, suggests God "exchange them for another nation." God has him marry a harlot, father children, and then tells him to send her away. When Hosea protests that he cannot, God turns it back on him: neither can I abandon Israel. Here the marriage is real, and it is a lesson aimed at the prophet.

Maimonides went the other way. In the Guide for the Perplexed (II:46), arguing that the prophets' symbolic acts happened in vision, he says of Hosea's marriage, the births, and the naming: "All this passed in a prophetic vision." John Gill's eighteenth-century Exposition reports that Ibn Ezra and David Kimhi read it the same way.

Modern readers remain divided. Kent P. Jackson of BYU, in Isaiah and the Prophets (1984), lays out three options: Hosea knowingly married a promiscuous woman; Gomer was faithful at first and strayed later, so "wife of whoredoms" is hindsight; or the account is allegory. He rejects the first and calls the question of literal history "irrelevant" to the book's purpose. G. I. Davies (New Century Bible, 1992) reports that the hindsight reading long dominated critical scholarship, but notes Wilhelm Rudolph's objection that chapter 1 never actually says Gomer strayed after the wedding. Davies also records Hans Walter Wolff's theory that "harlotry" refers to a cultic bridal sex rite, a theory resting on the "sacred prostitution" thesis that Stephanie Lynn Budin's The Myth of Sacred Prostitution in Antiquity (Cambridge, 2008) has badly undercut. Davies calls the related fertility-myth reconstruction "an insecure basis for exegesis."

This week's lesson assumes the literal reading: "Hosea's wife, Gomer, was unfaithful to him, and God pointed to this sad event." That is a defensible position, but faithful interpreters, including Jackson, writing for BYU's Religious Studies Center, have held others. Whether the unnamed woman of chapter 3 is Gomer is also unsettled.

The husband who threatens to strip his wife

Hosea 2 is a divorce-court speech: "she is not my wife, neither am I her husband" (2:2). The husband threatens to "strip her naked, and set her as in the day that she was born" (2:3), to refuse mercy to her children (2:4), and to "discover her lewdness in the sight of her lovers, and none shall deliver her out of mine hand" (2:10). Ezekiel 16 and 23 go further still.

The critique deserves its full strength. Renita J. Weems, in Battered Love: Marriage, Sex, and Violence in the Hebrew Prophets (Fortress, 1995), asks how divine love and covenant faithfulness came to be expressed through images of a husband battering and shaming his wife, and argues that such imagery cannot be dismissed as a mere literary device, since metaphors teach readers what love looks like. Gale A. Yee's Poor Banished Children of Eve (Fortress, 2003) reads the promiscuous wife as Hosea's figure for the male elite of priests and kings, which still places the shame on a woman's body. Athalya Brenner and Fokkelien van Dijk-Hemmes went further with what they called "pornoprophetic" readings. This is not only an academic concern. A reader who has survived an abusive marriage hears "I will allure her" (2:14) right after "I will strip her" (2:3) and recognizes the cycle.

There are serious responses. Andrew Sloane, in an evangelical reply in Tyndale Bulletin (2008) that focuses on Ezekiel but addresses the same critique of Hosea 1–3, acknowledges "the offensive power of the texts" but argues that their shocking features are better explained by the real violence of the judgment being announced, which for Hosea meant Assyrian siege and deportation, and by how serious the covenant breach was, than by misogyny. Jackson likewise reads the stripping as allegory for national punishment. Others point out that the text works against its own metaphor. In 2:16 the wife will call God "Ishi" (my man, my husband) and "no more Baali," a word meaning both "my Baal" and "my master," as if ownership itself has to end. And in 11:9 God refuses to act like an injured man: "I will not execute the fierceness of mine anger... for I am God, and not man."

Latter-day Saints feel a particular version of this. President Russell M. Nelson's "The Everlasting Covenant" (Liahona, October 2022) teaches that hesed, one of Hosea's key words (4:1; 6:4, 6), "has no adequate English equivalent" and that "a celestial marriage is such a covenant relationship." That deepens the analogy and also raises the stakes: if marriage models how God loves, what do we do with a model husband who threatens public humiliation? One answer is that Hosea works within eighth-century Israel's marriage norms and pushes against them (2:16; 11:9). Another is that modern teaching on marriage has moved far beyond what the metaphor assumes. Neither answer makes 2:3 and 2:10 comfortable, and nothing requires that they be.

A prophet against a prophet: the blood of Jezreel

Hosea's first child is named Jezreel, "for yet a little while, and I will avenge the blood of Jezreel upon the house of Jehu" (1:4). Jezreel is where Jehu wiped out the house of Ahab, a coup Elisha's messenger set in motion (2 Kings 9:1–10). And 2 Kings 10:30 has the LORD praising Jehu: "thou hast done well in executing that which is right in mine eyes," with a promise of four generations on the throne. Jeroboam II, in whose reign Hosea begins (1:1), belongs to that dynasty.

So one prophetic tradition approves the bloodbath and a later prophet calls it guilt to be avenged. Davies puts it plainly: "It is striking how differently Hosea judges them from Elisha on the one hand and the Deuteronomistic historian on the other... For Hosea murder is a sin... and the end does not justify the means." Harmonizers note that 2 Kings 10:31 already faults Jehu, who "took no heed to walk in the law of the LORD," so he may have carried out a just sentence for the wrong motives and to excess. Others see inspired writers disagreeing about the moral meaning of one event. Noticing the tension is close reading, not faithlessness.

Violence in the voice of God

God says, "I will meet them as a bear that is bereaved of her whelps, and will rend the caul of their heart" (13:8), and the chapter ends: "their infants shall be dashed in pieces, and their women with child shall be ripped up" (13:16; Hebrew 14:1). This was real Iron Age warfare; 2 Kings 15:16 credits Israel's own King Menahem with the same atrocity.

Some answer that 13:16 describes what conquest brings rather than what God approves; the verbs are passive. Some say the prophet read catastrophe as judgment because that was how ancient Israel interpreted history, not because the verse defines God's character. Others find both answers too easy, since in 13:7–8 God himself, speaking in the first person, is the predator. What cannot be denied is that the book puts 13:16 directly before "O Israel, return unto the LORD thy God" (14:1). Hosea sets the horror and the invitation side by side and does not reconcile them.

When the New Testament reads Hosea differently than Hosea reads

Three famous verses are best known through later reuse, and in each case the original context points elsewhere.

Hosea 13:14. The KJV reads it as a promise: "I will ransom them from the power of the grave... O death, I will be thy plagues." Paul uses it in 1 Corinthians 15:55, and the lesson's children's section teaches it as a statement about Christ. But the verse ends "repentance shall be hid from mine eyes," and Samaria's destruction follows. Davies notes that the Hebrew lacks the usual question marker, so a positive reading is grammatically possible, but he argues the context points to rhetorical questions ("Shall I ransom them...?") followed by a summons to Death and Sheol to do their work. Paul's use carries "a quite different sense from what they originally meant," he writes, though the Septuagint had probably already read the verse positively.

Hosea 11:1. "Called my son out of Egypt" looks back to the Exodus, and verse 2 goes on, "they sacrificed unto Baalim." Matthew 2:15 applies it to the child Jesus, and the Latter-day Saint chapter heading reads it "in similitude of our Lord." Critics call this proof-texting. Defenders call it typology, Israel's story relived by Israel's Messiah, which is a real method but not the straightforward prediction a casual reader of Matthew assumes.

Hosea 6:2. "In the third day he will raise us up" is the people's own confession, and God's reply in 6:4 ("your goodness is as a morning cloud") treats it as shallow.

None of this shows the apostles were wrong. It does show that "Hosea predicted this" and "a New Testament writer found Christ here" are different claims.

A difficult text and the Bible Dictionary's dating

Hosea's Hebrew is notoriously difficult; Davies often marks lines as uncertain or emended (for example 13:15). Many scholars also see Judean editing in this northern book: 1:7 abruptly promises mercy to Judah, and 3:5 has Israel seeking "David their king." Davies calls 1:7 "the clearest addition," with "a pro-Judaean standpoint," probably added before the exile. A belief in inspired editing can accommodate this, but the book clearly passed through more than one hand.

A small point for Latter-day Saint readers: the Bible Dictionary says Hosea "probably died before the accession of Pekah, 736 B.C., for he makes no allusion to the Syro-Ephraimitic war." Much scholarship disagrees. Since Albrecht Alt's 1919 essay, many have read 5:8–6:6 (alarms at Gibeah and Ramah, Ephraim and Judah fighting over the border) as reflecting exactly that war of 734–732 BC; Davies calls Alt's reconstruction "plausible" while noting dissent. The Bible Dictionary is a study aid, and here it takes one side of an open question.

Baal, Asherah, and what Israelites actually believed

Hosea complains that Israel credited Baal with its grain, wine, and oil (2:5, 8). Archaeology suggests something messier than a clean choice between YHWH and Baal. At Kuntillet ʻAjrud in the Sinai, excavated by Zeʼev Meshel and dated around 800 BC, storage jars carry blessings "by Yahweh of Samaria and his asherah" and by "Yahweh of Teman and his asherah." Whether "his asherah" is a goddess-consort or a cult object is debated. Either way, many Israelites seem to have honored YHWH in ways prophets condemned while believing themselves loyal. That unsettles some readers, since monotheism then looks like a cause the prophets fought for rather than a settled national belief. Others find it clarifying, because it shows why "thou shalt know no god but me" (13:4) needed saying.

Joel: when was it written, and when is it fulfilled?

Joel is undated. The Bible Dictionary allows anything from "as early as the reign of Joash, before 850 B.C., or even so late as after the Return." Many critical scholars favor the late end of that range: no king is mentioned, a functioning temple and priesthood are assumed (1:9, 13; 2:17), Judeans are sold "unto the Grecians" (3:6), and Joel appears to quote other prophets (3:16 with Amos 1:2; 2:32 with Obadiah 17). Hans Walter Wolff dated the book to the first half of the fourth century BC, a date William H. Shea's 1979 review judged "too late," and James L. Crenshaw's Anchor Bible volume (1995) surveys the debate at length.

The tension most specific to Latter-day Saints concerns fulfillment. At Pentecost Peter says, "this is that which was spoken by the prophet Joel" (Acts 2:16), and quotes 2:28–32. In 1823 Moroni quoted the same passage to Joseph Smith and "said that this was not yet fulfilled, but was soon to be" (Joseph Smith—History 1:41). The usual reconciliation is layered fulfillment: Pentecost was a real first installment, and the full outpouring, with the sun darkened and the moon turned to blood (2:31), still lies ahead. That fits how scripture often handles prophecy. But readers construct this reconciliation; neither text states it, and Peter's "this is that" sounds complete on its own terms.

Joel has ethical edges that lessons usually skip. In 3:8 God will sell the sons and daughters of Tyre and Sidon to the Sabeans as repayment for their slave trade in Judah's children. And 3:10, "Beat your plowshares into swords," reverses Isaiah 2:4 and Micah 4:3. Some take it as irony, a summons for the nations to arm for a battle they will lose. Others see prophets arguing with one another, or a sharper, more vengeful hope in Joel than in Isaiah.

What the book leaves unresolved

Hosea closes with a reader's warning: "Who is wise, and he shall understand these things?... the ways of the LORD are right, and the just shall walk in them: but the transgressors shall fall therein" (14:9). The book itself admits it can trip a reader. The tender passages (2:14–23; 11:8–9; 14:4–8) are real, and so are 2:3, 13:8, and 13:16. A faithful reading does not have to explain the hard verses away. It can admit that the same book contains a God who says "mine heart is turned within me" and a prophet who could not picture covenant love without threats of exposure, and leave the reader to wrestle, as Jacob did in 12:3–4, without expecting the blessing to come easily.
