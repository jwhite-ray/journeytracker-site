Rain, Grain, and a Dying Kingdom: Hosea and Joel as Historians Read Them

Written by an AI persona in the voice of a non-Latter-day-Saint Hebrew Bible academic — not a real scholar.

A northern voice in a southern book

Hosea is the only book among the Latter Prophets whose speaker plainly belongs to the northern kingdom of Israel. He calls the nation "Ephraim," he knows its shrines (Gilgal, Mizpah, Tabor, and Bethel, which he mocks as Beth-aven, "house of iniquity," 4:15; 10:5), and he watches its kings fall. Yet the superscription in 1:1 dates him first by four kings of Judah and only then by one northern king, Jeroboam son of Joash. Most critical scholars think Hosea's sayings were carried south after Samaria fell to Assyria in 722 BCE and were edited in Judah for Judean readers. Hans Walter Wolff's Hermeneia commentary (1974) and A. A. Macintosh's International Critical Commentary (1997) both treat this Judean layer at length. Its likeliest marks are the lines that set Judah apart for favor ("But I will have mercy upon the house of Judah," 1:7) and the hope that Israel will return to "David their king" (3:5). A northern prophet had little reason to look to the Davidic dynasty. A Judean editor after 722 had every reason to.

The editing did not stop there. In the Hebrew canon the twelve "minor" prophets form one scroll, the Book of the Twelve. James Nogalski's paired monographs (1993) argued that the scroll was deliberately stitched together with catchwords. One example sits in this week's reading: Joel 3:16, "The LORD also shall roar out of Zion, and utter his voice from Jerusalem," is almost word for word the opening line of Amos (Amos 1:2), the book that follows Joel in the Hebrew order. Nogalski's detailed reconstruction is disputed, but the Twelve were read as a unit in antiquity.

Hosea's Hebrew is among the most obscure poetry in the Bible, full of rare words and lines that may be corrupt or may reflect a northern dialect we know poorly. Francis I. Andersen and David Noel Freedman's Anchor Bible volume (1980) runs to nearly seven hundred pages largely because so many verses allow several defensible translations.

Eighth-century Israel: from boom to collapse

Hosea's career seems to begin in the last prosperous years of Jeroboam II (died about 750 BCE) and to run into the chaos that followed. Second Kings 15 records four kings assassinated within a generation. Hosea's lines "her king is cut off as the foam upon the water" (10:7) and "I gave thee a king in mine anger, and took him away in my wrath" (13:11) fit this run of coups.

Assyrian royal inscriptions confirm the outline. Tiglath-pileser III lists "Menahem of Samaria" among his tributaries, and one of his summary inscriptions boasts that the Israelites killed Pekah, "their king," and that he himself placed Hoshea on the throne and took gold and silver as tribute. (The texts are edited by Hayim Tadmor and Shigeo Yamada, 2011.) Hosea's complaint that Ephraim "went... to the Assyrian, and sent to king Jareb" (5:13; also 10:6) probably refers to this tribute diplomacy. No Assyrian king named Jareb is known, and many scholars divide the Hebrew consonants differently to get malki rab, "the great king," a standard Assyrian royal title. Hosea 5:8–6:6, with its alarm trumpets at Gibeah and Ramah on the Benjaminite border, has long been read against the Syro-Ephraimite war of about 734–732 BCE, when Israel and Damascus attacked Judah (2 Kings 16:5–9; Isaiah 7).

The marriage: biography, vision, or political allegory?

"Go, take unto thee a wife of whoredoms and children of whoredoms" (1:2). The Babylonian Talmud (Pesachim 87a–b) turns it into a lesson for the prophet himself. When God tells Hosea that Israel has sinned, Hosea answers that God should exchange Israel for another nation, so God orders him to marry a prostitute and then asks whether he could send her away. Maimonides, in the Guide of the Perplexed (II:46), rejected the literal reading outright. He held that the command, the births, and the naming of the children all happened in a prophetic vision, just as Jeremiah's girdle at the Euphrates did.

Modern scholarship asks different questions. The phrase ʼeshet zenunim (literally "a woman of fornications") uses an abstract plural, and many commentators take it to describe a disposition rather than a profession, perhaps named in hindsight after the woman proved unfaithful. For much of the twentieth century a popular theory held that Gomer had taken part in a Canaanite rite of "sacred prostitution." That theory has largely collapsed. Stephanie Lynn Budin's The Myth of Sacred Prostitution in Antiquity (2008) reexamined the ancient Near Eastern, Greek, and early Christian evidence and concluded that the institution, as classically described, did not exist. What remains is Hosea's own rhetoric in 4:13–14, which describes worship "upon the tops of the mountains... under oaks and poplars" in sexual terms. Sexual language for religious disloyalty is metaphor, not evidence of a ritual.

Two other readings now get a serious hearing. Gale A. Yee, in Poor Banished Children of Eve (2003), argues that the promiscuous wife stands for the male elite of priests and kings. On her reading Hosea's target is their economic and political conduct, not women's religion. Brad E. Kelle's Hosea 2: Metaphor and Rhetoric in Historical Perspective (2005) notes that ancient Near Eastern texts often personify capital cities as women and argues that the wife of chapter 2 is Samaria itself during the Syro-Ephraimite crisis. On either view the "lovers" of 2:5 are foreign powers and their gods as much as fertility deities.

Chapter 3, told in the first person, does not name the woman Hosea buys back, so whether she is Gomer cannot be settled from the Hebrew. But the price, "fifteen pieces of silver, and... an homer and an half of barley" (3:2), has the feel of a real transaction, one reason many scholars think some biography lies behind the symbolism.

The children's names carry the politics. "Jezreel" (1:4) points to Jehu's massacre of the house of Ahab (2 Kings 9–10), the coup that founded Jeroboam II's dynasty. In 2 Kings 10:30 that purge has divine approval. Hosea condemns it: "I will avenge the blood of Jezreel upon the house of Jehu." Here one prophetic voice judges an act that another biblical tradition endorses.

Who sends the rain? Baal, Asherah, and Hosea's polemic

At its center chapter 2 is an argument about agriculture. The wife says her lovers "give me my bread and my water, my wool and my flax, mine oil and my drink" (2:5), "for she did not know that I gave her corn, and wine, and oil" (2:8). In the Ugaritic Baal Cycle from Late Bronze Age Syria (Mark S. Smith began a multivolume edition and commentary in 1994), Baal is the storm god whose rains bring fertility; when he is swallowed by Mot, "Death," the land withers. Hosea insists that YHWH, not Baal, controls the rain: he "shall come unto us as the rain, as the latter and former rain" (6:3), and "I will be as the dew unto Israel" (14:5).

The polemic also works through a pun. Hebrew baʻal means "master" or "husband" as well as the god's name: "Thou shalt call me Ishi [my husband]; and shalt call me no more Baali [my master/my Baal]" (2:16).

Archaeology shows that Israelite religion in Hosea's century was more varied than his rhetoric allows. At Kuntillet ʻAjrud, a waystation in northeastern Sinai occupied in the early eighth century, inscriptions on storage jars invoke blessings by "YHWH of Samaria and his asherah" and "YHWH of Teman and his asherah." The final report, edited by Zeʼev Meshel (2012), argues that "asherah" here means a cultic object such as a sacred tree or pole; other scholars read it as the goddess Asherah, YHWH's consort. Either way, people in Hosea's world combined devotion to YHWH with practices he condemned, and they invoked the name of his own capital to do it.

Hosea 6:1–3 ("after two days will he revive us: in the third day he will raise us up") was once explained as borrowed from the cult of a "dying and rising" fertility god, a category Mark S. Smith's 1998 article "The Death of 'Dying and Rising Gods' in the Biblical World" argued fits the ancient evidence poorly. Most commentators now read "two days... third day" as a numerical idiom for "soon," and the "raising" as recovery from the illness and wounds described in 5:13. The reading of the verse as a reference to resurrection is a later Christian one.

The problem of the stripped wife

Chapter 2 also threatens violence: "Lest I strip her naked, and set her as in the day that she was born... and slay her with thirst" (2:3); "now will I discover her lewdness in the sight of her lovers" (2:10). Renita J. Weems's Battered Love: Marriage, Sex, and Violence in the Hebrew Prophets (1995) brought this into the center of the field. She argued that the metaphor persuades by drawing on its audience's assumptions about a husband's right to punish, so that divine love and male violence become entangled.

Where the stripping comes from is debated. Delbert Hillers, in Treaty-Curses and the Old Testament Prophets (1964), proposed a parallel in the eighth-century Aramaic Sefire treaty, which he restored as a curse that the vassal's wives be stripped "just as the prostitute is stripped." Ryan C. Hanley's 2017 article "The Background and Purpose of Stripping the Adulteress in Hosea 2" points out that the key words in Sefire are reconstructed and that no ancient law clearly prescribes stripping an adulteress. He argues that the curse lists of Deuteronomy 28 are the closer background. Either way, the speech of 2:2–13 is a covenant curse turned into a marriage quarrel.

Then the chapter turns sharply, as Hosea often does: "I will allure her, and bring her into the wilderness, and speak comfortably unto her" (2:14), literally "speak to her heart," a phrase used elsewhere for courtship and reconciliation (Genesis 34:3; Ruth 2:13). The betrothal of 2:19–20 lists the bride-price God pays, "in righteousness, and in judgment, and in lovingkindness, and in mercies... in faithfulness." The text does not resolve the tension between threat and tenderness, and an honest reading should not resolve it either.

Hosea's vocabulary

Three terms run through the book. Hesed (4:1; 6:4, 6; 10:12; 12:6) is loyalty within a relationship. The KJV's "mercy," "goodness," and "lovingkindness" all miss its sense of obligation. Daʻat ʼelohim, "knowledge of God" (4:1, 6; 6:6), is not doctrinal information but acknowledgment, the way a vassal "knows" an overlord by keeping faith with him. Shuv, "turn/return," is Hosea's key verb. It can mean repentance (6:1; 14:1), refusal (11:5, "they refused to return"), or a return to Egypt as captivity. The famous line "I desired mercy [hesed], and not sacrifice" (6:6) is not a rejection of ritual in principle. Its second half, "more than burnt offerings," shows that the idiom means "rather than."

Two well-known verses read differently in Hebrew than in the KJV. In 6:7, "they like men [keʼadam] have transgressed the covenant" can mean "like Adam," but the next words, "there have they dealt treacherously," point to a place. Many modern translations therefore read "at Adam," the Jordan town of Joshua 3:16. In 13:14, unpunctuated Hebrew allows the lines to be questions: "Shall I ransom them from the power of Sheol? Shall I redeem them from death? O Death, where are your plagues?" The verse is surrounded by threats (13:12–16) and ends with "repentance [or compassion] shall be hid from mine eyes." Read that way, it summons Death to do its work. Paul's use of the verse in 1 Corinthians 15:55 follows a Greek text and turns it in the opposite direction.

The most remarkable passage theologically is 11:1–9, where God is a parent rather than a husband: "When Israel was a child, then I loved him... I taught Ephraim also to go, taking them by their arms" (11:1, 3). Then God argues with himself: "How shall I give thee up, Ephraim?... how shall I make thee as Admah?" Admah and Zeboim were destroyed with Sodom (Deuteronomy 29:23). The resolution, "for I am God, and not man" (11:9), sets divine holiness against human vengefulness. Chapter 12, by contrast, turns Jacob into an accusation (12:3–4, 12), in a version that differs from Genesis: in 12:4 Jacob weeps and pleads with the angel, a detail Genesis 32 lacks.

Joel: a locust plague and its interpretation

Joel gives no king, no datable event, and nothing about the prophet beyond his father's name. Proposed dates range from the ninth century to the Persian period; Wolff's Joel and Amos (1977) argued for a late date, and James L. Crenshaw's Anchor Bible Joel (1995) weighs the options. The case for a date after the exile rests on several features: no king is mentioned, and elders and priests lead the community (1:13–14; 2:16–17); there is a functioning temple and a city wall (2:9); Judeans have been sold to the "Grecians" (yevanim, 3:6); and the book seems to cite earlier prophets. Joel 2:32, "in mount Zion and in Jerusalem shall be deliverance, as the LORD hath said," echoes Obadiah 17 and marks itself as a quotation. Joel 3:10, "Beat your plowshares into swords," reverses Isaiah 2:4 and Micah 4:3. No single argument is decisive, but most scholars now place Joel after the exile.

The four insect names in 1:4 are probably terms for locusts at different stages or simply different names for them, not four species. John A. Thompson's 1955 article "Joel's Locusts in the Light of Near Eastern Parallels" collected ancient comparisons and supported the view that a real plague lies behind chapter 1. Chapter 2 is harder. Its "army" that runs like horses, climbs walls, and enters windows (2:4–9) accurately describes a locust swarm, but it is also YHWH's "army" (2:11), and 2:20 calls it "the northern army." Many interpreters think the book moves from a real plague to the "day of the LORD" (1:15; 2:1, 11, 31; 3:14), using the plague as a picture of final judgment.

Joel's call to "rend your heart, and not your garments... for he is gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness" (2:13) quotes the formula of Exodus 34:6, which recurs in Jonah 4:2. Joel's next question, "Who knoweth if he will return and repent" (2:14), has a close parallel in Jonah 3:9. The promise "I will pour out my spirit upon all flesh" (2:28–32; Hebrew 3:1–5) is narrower in context than its later Christian use. "All flesh" is "your sons and your daughters," the community of Judah, set against the nations judged in the next chapter in the "valley of Jehoshaphat," a symbolic name meaning "YHWH judges" (3:2, 12). Joel ends on a nationalist note, with Egypt and Edom made desolate while "Judah shall dwell for ever" (3:19–20). The book itself leaves open how that sits with the gift of the spirit.
