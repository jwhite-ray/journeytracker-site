One Prophecy, Six Ways of Seeing: Joel 2:28–32

Written by an AI persona applying Donald W. Parry's six-outlooks framework. Professor Parry did not write, review, or endorse this essay, and no words are attributed to him that are not cited to a published source.

Of all the passages in this week's fourteen chapters, Joel 2:28–32 has the busiest afterlife. A prophet of uncertain date promises that God "will pour out my spirit upon all flesh"; centuries later Peter stands up on the day of Pentecost and says, in effect, this is that (Acts 2:16); about eighteen hundred years after Peter, an angel quotes the same five verses to a seventeen-year-old in upstate New York and says they are "not yet fulfilled, but [were] soon to be" (Joseph Smith—History 1:41). A text that is fulfilled, and then fulfilled again, and then declared not yet fulfilled, is a text that forces a reader to ask what kind of reading is going on.

Donald W. Parry, a professor of Hebrew Bible at Brigham Young University, offers a useful tool for that question. In a chapter titled "Ways to Read Isaiah: Six Outlooks," published in Search Diligently the Words of Isaiah (Religious Studies Center/Deseret Book, pp. 159–68), he lays out six distinct ways a reader can approach a prophetic passage: reading in historical context (a "near view"); reading prophecy (a "distant" view); reading for dual or multiple fulfillment; likening the words to ourselves and others; reading for personal reasons; and reading to understand doctrine. Parry built the scheme for Isaiah, but nothing about it is Isaiah-specific, and he himself reaches outside Isaiah for one of his examples: Joel 2:28, by way of a talk by Elder Dallin H. Oaks. That makes Joel 2:28–32 an unusually fitting place to try the whole framework out.

A note on what Parry does and does not claim. He does not rank the six outlooks or name one as the most important. He does suggest a starting point, advising that readers "begin by examining the book in its immediate context." So the walk-through below starts there, and treats the six as lenses to be held up one at a time, not as a ladder.

1. The near view: a drought, a locust swarm, and "afterward"

Parry's first outlook asks what a passage meant in and for the prophet's own time. For Joel, that question is harder than for Hosea, because Joel gives no date. Hosea 1:1 names four kings of Judah and one of Israel; Joel 1:1 names only his father, Pethuel. Scholars have consequently placed the book anywhere from the ninth century BC to the Maccabean period. The Bible Gateway Encyclopedia of the Bible entry on Joel lays out the competing arguments: a priestly, temple-centered community with no king in view, and no mention of the northern kingdom or Samaria, push many toward a date after the exile; others find that the same evidence fits earlier periods of Judah's history.

What is not in doubt is the shape of the book. Joel 1 is a lament over a real agricultural catastrophe: "That which the palmerworm hath left hath the locust eaten" (1:4), the field wasted, "the new wine is dried up, the oil languisheth" (1:10). Joel 2:1–11 turns the swarm into an army that runs "like mighty men" and climbs "the wall like men of war" (2:7). Then comes the call to "rend your heart, and not your garments" (2:13), and a turn: "Then will the LORD be jealous for his land, and pity his people" (2:18). The promises that follow are concrete and agricultural: "corn, and wine, and oil" (2:19), "the former rain, and the latter rain" (2:23), floors "full of wheat" (2:24), and "I will restore to you the years that the locust hath eaten" (2:25).

Our passage begins only after that: "And it shall come to pass afterward." The Hebrew ʼaharei-khen, "after this," marks a seam. (In Hebrew Bibles this passage is a separate chapter, Joel 3:1–5, which reinforces the sense that a new movement begins here.) In its near context, then, the outpouring of the Spirit is the second stage of a restoration. First the land is healed; then the people are filled. Read this way, "all flesh" is addressed in the first instance to the same audience Joel has been speaking to all along, the "children of Zion" of 2:23, which is why the verse immediately specifies "your sons and your daughters," "your old men," "your young men." What is startling in the near view is not the geography but the social reach: the Spirit falls on the old and the young, the sons and the daughters, and "also upon the servants and upon the handmaids" (2:29). Prophecy, in Israel's experience, came to a few named individuals. Moses had once wished aloud, "would God that all the LORD's people were prophets, and that the LORD would put his spirit upon them!" (Numbers 11:29). Joel announces that wish granted.

2. The distant view: "the great and the terrible day of the LORD"

Parry's second outlook reads prophecy as reaching "centuries or even millennia after" the prophet's lifetime. Joel's own text pushes in that direction. Verses 30–31 widen the frame from a people to a cosmos: "wonders in the heavens and in the earth, blood, and fire, and pillars of smoke. The sun shall be turned into darkness, and the moon into blood, before the great and the terrible day of the LORD come." Joel had already used darkened sun and moon to describe the locust-army (2:10), so the imagery has a near anchor. But here it is attached to "the day of the LORD" as a final event, and Joel 3 goes on to gather "all nations" into "the valley of Jehoshaphat" for judgment (3:2, 12) and to describe "multitudes, multitudes in the valley of decision" (3:14).

The distant view does not erase the near one. It notices that the prophecy's horizon is larger than any single harvest.

3. Dual and multiple fulfillment: Joel, Peter, Moroni

This is where Joel 2:28–32 becomes almost a textbook case, and where Parry's chapter touches Joel directly. Parry defines dual- or multiple-fulfillment prophecies as those fulfilled "two or more times," and among his examples he cites Elder Oaks, who wrote in the January 1995 Ensign that "many of the prophecies and doctrinal passages in the scriptures have multiple meanings." Oaks's illustration is this very passage: on the day of Pentecost, Peter declared that what the crowd had witnessed was "spoken by the prophet Joel"; "eighteen hundred years later, the angel Moroni quoted this same prophecy and said that 'this was not yet fulfilled, but was soon to be.'"

Peter's use in Acts 2 is not a passive quotation. Where Joel says "afterward," Peter says "in the last days" (Acts 2:17). The Hebrew text and the Greek Septuagint both read "after these things," so the change is Peter's (or Luke's) interpretive framing; it announces that the age Joel looked toward has begun. Peter adds "and they shall prophesy" after the servants and handmaids (Acts 2:18), and pairs Joel's "wonders in the heavens" with "signs in the earth beneath" (Acts 2:19). He quotes Joel through "whosoever shall call on the name of the Lord shall be saved" (Acts 2:21) and stops mid-verse. Then, at the end of his sermon, he seems to pick up the thread he left: "the promise is unto you, and to your children, and to all that are afar off, even as many as the Lord our God shall call" (Acts 2:39). Joel's verse ended with "the remnant whom the LORD shall call" (Joel 2:32). And Peter's "all that are afar off" pushes "all flesh" well past Zion's sons and daughters. Paul would later quote the same line, "whosoever shall call upon the name of the Lord shall be saved," in a passage whose point is that "there is no difference between the Jew and the Greek" (Romans 10:12–13).

Moroni's use in 1823 adds a third layer. Joseph Smith records that Moroni "quoted the second chapter of Joel, from the twenty-eighth verse to the last," said it "was not yet fulfilled, but was soon to be," and added "that the fulness of the Gentiles was soon to come in" (Joseph Smith—History 1:41). Read alongside Acts 2, that statement is not a contradiction of Peter. It is the logic of multiple fulfillment stated plainly: a prophecy can be truly fulfilled at Pentecost and still have a fuller fulfillment ahead.

So the passage now has at least three horizons: Joel's restored community after the locusts; the church at Pentecost; and the latter-day dispensation Moroni announced. Nothing in the multiple-fulfillment outlook requires choosing among them.

4. Likening: from Nephi to the pesher

Parry's fourth outlook, likening, takes its language from the Book of Mormon. He cites Nephi: "I will liken his words unto my people, . . . and ye may liken them unto you and unto all men" (2 Nephi 11:8; see also 1 Nephi 19:23–24), and calls likening "a productive, profitable, and sacred exercise." Likening is different from the third outlook. Multiple fulfillment claims the prophecy itself had more than one target; likening takes a text whose target may be quite specific and asks what it means when held against our own situation.

Ancient Jewish readers had a striking version of this. The Dead Sea Scrolls include commentaries called pesharim (singular pesher, "interpretation"). As Devorah Dimant explains in her Encyclopedia of Religion entry on pesher, these works quote a prophet line by line and then apply each line to the community's own history and expected end times. The Habakkuk commentary from Qumran Cave 1 even claims that God told Habakkuk to write about the last generation but did not reveal to him when it would be fulfilled, and that the meaning was made known instead to the community's Teacher of Righteousness, "to whom God made known all the mysteries" of the prophets (1QpHab 7). Pesher is not the same as Nephi's likening, and Parry does not mention it; the comparison is mine. But it shows how old the instinct is, and Peter's "this is that" in Acts 2:16 has a family resemblance to it.

Likening Joel 2:28–29 in a Latter-day Saint congregation is not hard. The lesson for this week suggests asking children to put each other's names in place of "your sons" and "your daughters." The adult version is to notice who Joel names: the young and the old, women and men, servants and handmaids. Joel's list has no clergy in it. If the Spirit is promised to every one of those categories, then a ward, a family, or a quorum in which revelation is assumed to come only to the person at the front of the room has not yet taken Joel at his word. Scripture already supplied precedents for the "daughters": Miriam (Exodus 15:20), Deborah (Judges 4:4), and Huldah (2 Kings 22:14) are each called a prophetess.

5. Personal reading: what the Lord wishes to say today

The fifth outlook is the least controllable and, for that reason, the one most worth naming. Parry describes reading scripture for "personal instruction, spiritual guidance, devotionalistic purposes": for comfort, for an increase of the Spirit, for personal revelation. Here he leans again on Elder Oaks, who wrote that "a scripture is not limited to what it meant when it was written" and that scripture reading "may also lead to current revelation on whatever else the Lord wishes to communicate to the reader at that time."

That phrase, "whatever else," is the key. Neither Parry nor Oaks says it in so many words, but the implication is that the personal outlook is not bound to the passage's own subject. A reader working through Joel 2 might come away with a prompting about a strained relationship, a job decision, or a person to visit, none of which has anything to do with locusts, Pentecost, or the day of the Lord. On this outlook, that is not a misreading. It is the passage functioning as a place where revelation happens rather than as the content of the revelation.

Joel 2:28–29 is an apt text for this outlook because it is itself a promise of personal revelation: dreams, visions, prophecy, given to ordinary people. The lesson pairs it with President Russell M. Nelson's warning that "in coming days, it will not be possible to survive spiritually without the guiding, directing, comforting, and constant influence of the Holy Ghost" ("Revelation for the Church, Revelation for Our Lives," April 2018 general conference). A reader who opens Joel asking for exactly that kind of guidance is, in a small way, testing whether Joel's promise holds.

The discipline this outlook needs is honesty about which outlook is in play. A personal impression received while reading Joel 2 is real and may be precious, but it should not be offered to a class as what Joel meant.

6. Doctrine: what the passage teaches

Parry's sixth outlook reads for doctrine, noting that Isaiah presents "scores and hundreds of significant truths and doctrines." Joel 2:28–32 is compact, but it carries several.

The first is that the Spirit is God's to give and is given lavishly. The verb is "pour out," not drip or dispense; the lesson's children's activity contrasts a pour with a trickle for exactly this reason. The second is that access to revelation is broad. Peter's Pentecost sermon ties the promise to repentance, baptism, and "the gift of the Holy Ghost" (Acts 2:38), the same sequence Latter-day Saints confess in the fourth article of faith, and the seventh article of faith claims the very gifts Joel lists: "prophecy, revelation, visions." The third is that deliverance comes by calling on the name of the Lord: "whosoever shall call on the name of the LORD shall be delivered" (Joel 2:32), a line both Peter and Paul read as an invitation that reaches beyond Israel. The fourth is that the outpouring of the Spirit and "the great and the terrible day of the LORD" belong together; Joel places the gift before the day (2:31), as preparation, not as an afterthought.

Why keep the six apart

The value of Parry's framework is that it separates readings a careful reader already senses at once, so each can be held to its own standard. The near view is answerable to the Hebrew text and to history. The distant and multiple-fulfillment views are answerable to the way later scripture, Acts and Joseph Smith—History, actually uses the passage. Likening and personal reading are answerable to the Spirit and to the reader's honesty. Doctrine is answerable to the whole canon. Joel 2:28–32 rewards all six, and reading it once through each lens is a good way to see how a single promise about the Spirit could be fulfilled at Pentecost, declared "soon to be" fulfilled in 1823, and still be addressed, this week, to someone's sons and daughters.
