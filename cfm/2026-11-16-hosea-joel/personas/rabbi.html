Return as Far as the Throne: A Jewish Reading of Hosea and Joel

Written by an AI persona giving a Jewish reading in the voice of a rabbi — not a real rabbi.

I write as an openly fictional AI persona trained to read as a rabbi reads, not as any real rabbi. Most Jews do not meet Hosea as a book read straight through. They meet it in pieces, worked into prayer. Every weekday morning, a man winding his tefillin strap around his middle finger says Hosea 2:19–20 (Hebrew 2:21–22). On the Shabbat between Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur the synagogue reads Hosea 14:1–9 (Hebrew 14:2–10) as the haftarah. Ashkenazim add Joel 2:15–27; Sephardim add Micah 7:18–20. That Sabbath takes its name, Shabbat Shuvah, from the haftarah's first word, shuvah, "return." In the morning service, a prayer in the sacrificial readings asks God to accept "the bulls of our lips" (14:2; Hebrew 14:3) in place of the daily offering.

So the Hosea that Jews carry around is a book of betrothal and return. The stripping and the lion and the bear of chapters 2 and 13 are in the scroll, but the liturgy did not choose them. That choice is itself a way of reading the book.

The rabbis were uneasy too

The hard question about Hosea's marriage is not a modern one. The Babylonian Talmud (Pesachim 87a–b) tells a story that turns Hosea 1 inside out. God says to Hosea, "Your sons have sinned." Hosea should have pleaded that they are the children of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Instead he answers that the whole world is God's, so exchange Israel for another nation. So God tells him to marry a prostitute and have children by her, intending later to tell him to send her away: if he can, I will send Israel away. After three children are born, God does tell Hosea to separate from her. Hosea answers that he cannot. Then the turn: you do not even know whether these children are yours, God says, and still you will not let them go. How could I let go of Israel? Hosea starts to pray for himself. God tells him to pray for Israel first, because three decrees, the names Jezreel, Lo-ruhamah, and Lo-ammi, have gone out on his account. Hosea prays, the decree is annulled, and the blessing of 1:10 (Hebrew 2:1) follows: "in the place where it was said unto them, Ye are not my people, there it shall be said unto them, Ye are the sons of the living God."

In the sages' reading the marriage is not mainly God teaching Israel. It is God teaching the prophet, who had forgotten that a prophet is supposed to be Israel's advocate. The children's terrible names are the price of his lack of love, and the book's first comfort comes when he finally intercedes.

The medieval commentators split. Rashi, on 1:2, follows the sages: the command means what it says, and he points to Pesachim. He also reports that the Targum turned the verse into a prophecy against "the inhabitants of the cities of idolatry," so already in antiquity some readers would not let it stand literally. Abraham ibn Ezra is blunt. "Heaven forbid," he writes, that God would command a man to marry a harlot and father children of harlotry. He holds that the prophet saw all of it in a night vision, as with Isaiah walking naked (Isaiah 20). Maimonides (Guide of the Perplexed II:46) says the same: the command to Hosea, the births, and the naming: "All this passed in a prophetic vision."

I will be honest about the limits of this tradition. Ibn Ezra's "Heaven forbid" protects the prophet's holiness, not Gomer's dignity. None of these sources asks what chapter 2 sounds like from the woman's side. That question is pressed hard in recent scholarship, for example in Renita J. Weems's Battered Love (1995), which asks what it costs to make a husband's threats to strip and expose his wife (2:3, 10) a picture of God. Neither the Talmud nor Ibn Ezra answers it. What the tradition did instead was choose: it built its daily practice on 2:19–20 and its fall season on chapter 14, and it read the Ishi and Baali verse in a way that pushes the metaphor away from domination.

"Thou shalt call me Ishi"

Hosea 2:16 (Hebrew 2:18) is a pun. The word baʻal names the storm god whom Israel credited with "my bread and my water, my wool and my flax" (2:5). It is also the ordinary Hebrew word for "husband" in the sense of "owner" or "master." By contrast ish is simply "man, husband." Rashi explains: "You shall worship Me out of love and not out of fear." The word ishi speaks of marriage and young love; baʻali speaks of mastery and fear. The Talmud (Ketubot 71b) gives Rabbi Yohanan's image: she will be like a bride already living in her husband's household, at ease enough to call him her partner, and not like a betrothed girl still in her father's house who calls her groom "my master."

That is the right frame for what comes next, the verses on the tefillin strap. "I will betroth thee unto me for ever; yea, I will betroth thee unto me in righteousness, and in judgment, and in lovingkindness, and in mercies. I will even betroth thee unto me in faithfulness: and thou shalt know the LORD" (2:19–20). The verb is ʼeres, betroth, the first stage of a Jewish marriage. Hosea does not say God will take back a divorced wife. He says God will start the marriage over, as though for the first time. Rashi, on Hebrew 2:21, notices that the bride-price in this verse goes both ways. Righteousness and justice are "which you practice." Lovingkindness and mercy "will come to you from Me because of them." Abraham was loved because he taught his children "to do justice and judgment" (Genesis 18:19), and God answers with hesed and rahamim. The covenant is reciprocal even when it is given freely.

So when a Jew winds the strap three times around the finger like a ring and says these words, he is not acting out a forgiven adulteress. He is renewing a betrothal. The last clause, "and thou shalt know the LORD," answers the book's diagnosis that there is no "knowledge of God in the land" (4:1), and the Hebrew daʻat can carry the knowledge of intimacy (Genesis 4:1).

Hesed and not sacrifice

Christian readers know Hosea 6:6 mostly through Jesus' use of it in Matthew 9:13 and 12:7. For Jews it is the founding text of life after the Temple. Avot de-Rabbi Natan (chapter 4) tells how Rabban Yohanan ben Zakkai was leaving Jerusalem with Rabbi Joshua and saw the Temple in ruins. "Woe to us," said Rabbi Joshua, "for this house... the place where atonement was made for the sins of Israel!" Rabban Yohanan answered that they had another means of atonement just as effective, acts of lovingkindness, gemilut hasadim, "as it is stated, For I desire lovingkindness and not sacrifice."

This week's lesson sums up 6:6 and Joel 2:13 as "Devotion to God must be felt inwardly, not just expressed outwardly." A Jewish reader would push back a little. In Hosea, hesed is not a feeling. It is loyal action, the kind of kindness that keeps a covenant. Rabban Yohanan did not replace the altar with a mood. He replaced it with deeds. The Mishnah makes the same point about Joel. On a public fast for drought, with the ark carried into the town square and covered with ashes, the eldest member of the community reminded the people that scripture does not say God saw Nineveh's sackcloth and fasting, but that God "saw their deeds, that they had turned from their evil way" (Jonah 3:10; Mishnah Taʻanit 2:1). Then he quoted Joel: "rend your heart, and not your garments" (2:13). In the Mishnah, rending the heart means changing what you do. Fasting and prayer without that are not enough.

Two verses Christians hear one way

"After two days will he revive us: in the third day he will raise us up" (6:2). Christian readers hear the resurrection, and that hearing is not foreign to Judaism. The Aramaic Targum Jonathan renders the verse: "He will give us life in the days of consolation that are to come; on the day of the resurrection of the dead He will raise us up." That is the resurrection of the nation and of all the dead, at the end. Rashi reads it historically: the "two days" are the two Temples destroyed, and on the third day God will set us up with the third Temple. The Talmud (Sanhedrin 97a) has Abaye take each "day" as a thousand years of desolation. None of these is one individual's third day.

Hosea 13:14 is the harder case. Paul's "O death, where is thy sting? O grave, where is thy victory?" (1 Corinthians 15:55) turns it into a taunt over a defeated death. Rashi reads it as a threat. His paraphrase: I am the One who would ransom them from the grave and redeem them from death, but now I will speak words of death against you. "Repentance shall be hid from mine eyes" means, for him, "I will not regret this evil." Given the bereaved bear of 13:8 and the east wind of 13:15, his reading is hard to dismiss. Christians are free to read the verse through Easter. They should know that in Hosea's own sequence the promise of chapter 14 has not yet come.

"For I am God, and not man"

The emotional center of the book is 11:8–9: "How shall I give thee up, Ephraim?... mine heart is turned within me, my repentings are kindled together. I will not execute the fierceness of mine anger... for I am God, and not man; the Holy One in the midst of thee: and I will not enter into the city." Rashi takes "God, and not man" as constancy: "God Who keeps His favorable word, and I do not retract the good." The Talmud reads the last obscure clause (Taʻanit 5a) through Rabbi Yohanan: God says, "I shall not enter Jerusalem above until I enter Jerusalem below." God refuses to be at home in heaven while His people are in exile.

The great modern Jewish reading of this chapter is Abraham Joshua Heschel's The Prophets (1962). Heschel argues that the prophets knew God not as an unmoved mover but through divine pathos: God is moved by and involved in what human beings do. In his chapter on Hosea he reads the marriage as Hosea's way of learning, in his own life, to share in God's grief. Eliezer Berkovits, in "Dr. A. J. Heschel's Theology of Pathos" (Tradition, 1964), objected that this attributes human passions to God, against the Maimonidean philosophical tradition; the argument still runs in Tradition's pages. Latter-day Saint readers, who do not share Maimonides' discomfort with a God who feels, may find Heschel congenial. But notice that Hosea's own verse keeps both sides. God's heart turns over, and the reason given for mercy is "for I am God, and not man."

Return as far as the Throne

Now the haftarah of Shabbat Shuvah. "O Israel, return unto the LORD thy God" (14:1; Hebrew 14:2). The Hebrew preposition is ʻad, literally "as far as." In the Talmud (Yoma 86a), Rabbi Levi draws the lesson: "Great is repentance, as it reaches the heavenly throne." Reish Lakish (Yoma 86b) notices the second half of the verse: "thou hast fallen by thine iniquity." The verb is kashalta, "you stumbled." Iniquity, ʻavon, is deliberate sin, yet the prophet calls it stumbling: for one who returns, deliberate sins count as accidents, and, the Gemara adds, when one returns out of love, even as merits.

The same page reads the next verses as a ladder. "Take with you words" (14:2): with a human being you have wronged, you cannot know whether words will be enough, but God is appeased by words. "So will we render the calves of our lips": the Gemara says Scripture counts it as though one had offered bulls. The Targum turns it into a prayer: may "the words of our lips be accepted before You like bulls" on the altar. Then the lesson's title verse: "I will heal their backsliding, I will love them freely" (14:4; Hebrew 14:5). "Freely" is nedavah, the word for a freewill offering. The Gemara reads it as saying that repentance counts as more than an obligatory sacrifice; it counts as a voluntary gift. Rabbi Meir adds a remarkable note on the end of the verse: "mine anger is turned away from him," singular. Because one person returns, the whole world is forgiven.

Maimonides builds his laws of repentance on this chapter. His definition of true repentance (Hilkhot Teshuvah 2:2) uses 14:3 (Hebrew 14:4), "neither will we say any more to the work of our hands, Ye are our gods," as the test: the one who knows hidden things can testify that this person will not go back. In 7:6 he cites "Return, O Israel" to show that repentance brings a person near to the Divine Presence. Then he turns to Hosea 1:10 (Hebrew 2:1): the language that pushed the sinner away, "Ye are not my people," becomes the language that draws him near, "Ye are the sons of the living God." The verse that closes the Talmud's story of Hosea's marriage is the verse that opens Maimonides' description of return.

Joel: the attributes, and all flesh

Joel 2:13 puts the Thirteen Attributes of Mercy (Exodus 34:6–7) back into a prophet's mouth: "gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness." Jews recite the Exodus formula over and over in the penitential prayers and on Yom Kippur. Joel, like Jonah 4:2, adds a clause that is not in Exodus: "and repenteth him of the evil." Exodus says God does not clear the guilty. Joel and Jonah say God turns back from harm. The Ashkenazi haftarah of Shabbat Shuvah carries this passage into the week before Yom Kippur, ending at 2:27, "my people shall never be ashamed," just before the outpouring of the spirit.

Peter quotes that outpouring (2:28–32; Hebrew 3:1–5) in Acts 2, and Moroni quoted it to Joseph Smith as "not yet fulfilled, but was soon to be" (Joseph Smith—History 1:41). A midrash on the seventy elders who received a share of Moses' spirit (Numbers 11) ends with Joel: "In this world, individuals prophesied. But in the World to Come, all of Israel will become prophets" (Bamidbar Rabbah 15:25). The commentators disagree on who "all flesh" is. Radak limits it to those in Israel worthy of the holy spirit and notes that Joel does not say all will prophesy, only "your sons and your daughters." Rashi is far more generous: "all flesh" is "anyone whose heart becomes soft as flesh," and he cites Ezekiel 36:26, "I will give you an heart of flesh." On Rashi's reading the condition for receiving the spirit is not birth but a softened heart. That is Joel 2:13 again, the heart rent rather than the garment.
