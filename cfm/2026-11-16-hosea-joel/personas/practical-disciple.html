Take With You Words: Hosea and Joel for an Ordinary Week

Written by an AI persona on living the week's text — not a real person.

Hosea opens with a prophet told to marry "a wife of whoredoms" and to name his children "Not pitied" and "Not my people" (Hosea 1:2, 6, 9). Joel opens with a crop failure so complete that "joy is withered away from the sons of men" (Joel 1:12). Neither reads like a family home evening lesson. But underneath the strangeness both books keep asking plain questions: Who do you credit for what you have? Is your goodness the kind that lasts until noon? When you have drifted, what do you actually say on the way back? And what will you do with the people you have privately written off?

The person you would rather exchange

Start with the hard part, because the lesson builds on it. Hosea marries Gomer, three children are born, and each child's name is a sentence of judgment. In chapter 3 he is told to "go yet, love a woman beloved of her friend, yet an adulteress," and he pays "fifteen pieces of silver, and... an homer of barley, and an half homer of barley" to bring her home (3:1–2).

Readers have never agreed on what happened. Maimonides argued that the marriage, births, and naming all took place in a prophetic vision rather than in Hosea's household (Guide for the Perplexed II:46). Others read it as biography. You do not need to settle the question to be taught by it: God chose the most intimate covenant people know, and the most painful way it breaks, to show how He experiences Israel.

The Babylonian Talmud tells a story that turns the book into a mirror. When God told Hosea that Israel had sinned, the rabbis say, Hosea did not plead for them; he suggested God "exchange them for another nation." So God had him marry a prostitute and have children by her, then told him to send her away. Hosea answered that he had children by her and could not. God's reply: as you cannot let them go, neither can I let Israel go (Pesachim 87a–b). This is rabbinic storytelling, not history, but it names something true. Hosea learns mercy by being bound to someone he cannot simply replace.

Most of us have someone we would quietly exchange if we could: a sibling who keeps relapsing, an adult child who left the Church and is sharp about it, a spouse who disappointed us in a way we have stopped talking about, a ward member we no longer bother to visit. Hosea 3:1 does not say "tolerate" her. It says "love" her, "according to the love of the LORD toward the children of Israel, who look to other gods." Whom have I mentally traded away? What would it cost me, in time, in pride, in convenience, to go buy them back?

What chapter 2 does not license

Then comes Hosea 2, and an honest reader has to slow down. The husband threatens to "strip her naked" (2:3), to "discover her lewdness in the sight of her lovers" (2:10), to "hedge up thy way with thorns" so she cannot leave (2:6), and to withhold mercy from the children (2:4). Renita J. Weems, in Battered Love: Marriage, Sex, and Violence in the Hebrew Prophets (1995), pressed exactly this problem: the prophets' marriage metaphor uses sexual humiliation as the image of divine judgment, and a reader who absorbs it uncritically may absorb a picture of marriage in which a wronged husband may punish, isolate, and expose. In a church that puts marriage at the center of the plan of salvation, that critique deserves a hearing.

Here is the practical line. Hosea 2 is a metaphor about God and a nation, and the book itself marks the difference: "I am God, and not man" (11:9). Nothing here gives a spouse permission to control money, movement, or a partner's body as punishment. If someone in your home is being hedged in or threatened with exposure, that is not a Hosea story; it is abuse, and it calls for help, not patience.

And notice where the chapter goes. After every threat comes the turn: "Therefore, behold, I will allure her, and bring her into the wilderness, and speak comfortably unto her" (2:14). The Hebrew behind "speak comfortably" is to speak "to her heart." Punishment does not win her back; tenderness does. The valley of Achor, the place of Achan's trouble in Joshua 7, becomes "a door of hope" (2:15). Then: "thou shalt call me Ishi; and shalt call me no more Baali" (2:16). Both words can mean "my husband," but baʻal also means "master" or "owner." The Lord wants to be the husband of companionship, not the husband who owns.

That is a real self-examination for marriages. In my home, am I more ʼishi or more baʻali? When I am hurt, do I reach for leverage (silence, money, withdrawal, airing my spouse's faults to family) or do I speak to their heart? The betrothal of 2:19–20 makes a good checklist: "in righteousness, and in judgment, and in lovingkindness, and in mercies... in faithfulness." Pick the one you have been weakest in this year and do one thing about it before Sunday.

Who gave you the grain?

Israel's sin in chapter 2 is misattribution. She says, "I will go after my lovers, that give me my bread and my water" (2:5), "for she did not know that I gave her corn, and wine, and oil, and multiplied her silver and gold, which they prepared for Baal" (2:8). Israel did not stop worshipping the LORD; she credited her paycheck to someone else.

The modern version is rarely a carved image. It is the quiet conviction that the job, the market, or my own hustle really feeds the family, and God is for Sundays. Hosea 12 makes it a workplace sin: "He is a merchant, the balances of deceit are in his hand... And Ephraim said, Yet I am become rich... in all my labours they shall find none iniquity in me" (12:7–8). That is the voice of someone whose books are technically clean and whose conscience has been outsourced to the books. Is there a place where my scales are slightly weighted: an expense report, a billable hour, a product description, a reference letter? When I do well, whom do I actually thank, and does my giving show it? Hosea's diagnosis of prosperity is blunt: "they were filled, and their heart was exalted; therefore have they forgotten me" (13:6).

Goodness that burns off by noon

The Lord's most famous complaint in Hosea is also his most domestic: "your goodness is as a morning cloud, and as the early dew it goeth away" (6:4). "Goodness" translates hesed, the same word rendered "mercy" two verses later: "For I desired mercy, and not sacrifice; and the knowledge of God more than burnt offerings" (6:6). President Russell M. Nelson called hesed "a covenant relationship in which both parties are bound to be loyal and faithful to each other" ("The Everlasting Covenant," Liahona, Oct. 2022). Hosea's charge is that Israel's side of that loyalty evaporates.

Read 6:1–3 and 6:4 together and the problem sharpens. The people say the right things: "Come, and let us return unto the LORD: for he hath torn, and he will heal us." It sounds like repentance. But the next line is God's sigh: "O Ephraim, what shall I do unto thee?" The words came easily; the loyalty did not last. Most of us know the resolve after a powerful sacrament meeting that is gone by Tuesday.

Jesus used Hosea 6:6 twice, both times against people whose observance had become an excuse for unkindness: when Pharisees objected to his eating with publicans and sinners ("go ye and learn what that meaneth," Matthew 9:13), and when they condemned his hungry disciples for plucking grain on the Sabbath (Matthew 12:7). Apply that to ministering. Am I keeping the forms, the reported contact, while avoiding the person who actually needs mercy? Hosea 4:1 lists what was missing in the land, "no truth, nor mercy, nor knowledge of God," and 4:2 shows what fills the vacuum: "swearing, and lying, and killing, and stealing." Honesty and mercy are not add-ons to worship; they are what worship was supposed to produce.

Returning with actual words

Hosea's master verb is shuv, "return," and the book ends with a script for doing it: "O Israel, return unto the LORD thy God... Take with you words, and turn to the LORD: say unto him, Take away all iniquity, and receive us graciously: so will we render the calves of our lips" (14:1–2). The returning person brings sentences instead of a bull. And the sentences are specific: "Asshur shall not save us; we will not ride upon horses: neither will we say any more to the work of our hands, Ye are our gods" (14:3). Israel names the exact alliances and idols it is giving up.

That is the difference between 6:1–3 and 14:1–3: the first is general and confident, the second concrete and humble. If you have drifted, from prayer, from church, from a temple covenant, from a neglected relationship, do what 14:2 literally says. Write out the words. Name your Asshur, the thing you have been counting on instead of God. Name the "work of your hands" you have treated as security. Then say it, in prayer, and if another person is involved, to them.

God's answer is the lesson's title: "I will heal their backsliding, I will love them freely: for mine anger is turned away from him" (14:4). Elder Jeffrey R. Holland put the same truth pastorally: "It is never too late so long as the Master of the vineyard says there is time" ("The Laborers in the Vineyard," April 2012). Hosea's image of the result is not dramatic. It is dew, a lily, an olive tree, roots like Lebanon (14:5–7): slow, rooted growth, not one emotional morning.

Two more verses help someone tired of starting over. Hosea 10:12: "break up your fallow ground: for it is time to seek the LORD." Fallow ground is not ruined ground; it has been left alone until it crusted over. Breaking it up is the work of a season, not a miracle. And 11:3–4 shows the patience on the other side: "I taught Ephraim also to go, taking them by their arms... I drew them with cords of a man, with bands of love." That is a parent teaching a toddler to walk, who falls constantly while the parent keeps reaching. If you suspect God is done with you, read 11:8 slowly: "How shall I give thee up, Ephraim?... mine heart is turned within me."

Not skipping the brutal verses

The same book says "their infants shall be dashed in pieces, and their women with child shall be ripped up" (13:16), "the mother was dashed in pieces upon her children" (10:14), and pictures God as a bereaved bear who will "rend the caul of their heart" (13:8). These verses describe what conquest actually did to Samaria's people, and the prophet frames that catastrophe as the consequence of a broken covenant. What you can do is refuse to read selectively. Choose carefully what you teach children, but as an adult do not let the lovely verses of chapter 14 make you forget that Hosea wrote to people about to lose everything. Even 13:9 holds both together: "O Israel, thou hast destroyed thyself; but in me is thine help."

Joel: rend the heart, gather the children

Joel begins with loss no one caused by a single sin: four waves of insects, each eating what the last left (1:4), then drought and fire (1:19–20). Joel's first instruction is not to explain the disaster but to pass it on honestly: "Tell ye your children of it, and let your children tell their children" (1:3).

Then the turning point: "rend your heart, and not your garments, and turn unto the LORD your God: for he is gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness" (2:13), echoing Exodus 34:6. Torn clothing was the visible sign of grief. The modern equivalent may be the public post, the visible fast, the announced resolution. None is wrong. The question is whether anything underneath has actually torn.

Joel insists that turning is communal: "gather the children, and those that suck the breasts: let the bridegroom go forth of his chamber, and the bride out of her closet" (2:16). No one is exempt, not even newlyweds. When your family fasts or prays over a real problem, include the children by name, and include whoever seems least interested.

Then the promise: "I will restore to you the years that the locust hath eaten" (2:25). It does not say the years were not lost. It promises restoration afterward. For someone returning after a decade of drift, that is the honest hope: not that the decade did not happen, but that God can make it yield something.

Finally: "I will pour out my spirit upon all flesh; and your sons and your daughters shall prophesy, your old men shall dream dreams, your young men shall see visions: and also upon the servants and upon the handmaids" (2:28–29). Moroni told Joseph Smith this was "not yet fulfilled, but was soon to be" (Joseph Smith—History 1:41). Revelation here is spread across ages, sexes, and social ranks. Do I take my daughter's spiritual impressions as seriously as my own? Whose dreams in my ward am I not hearing because of who they are? The lesson quotes Elder David A. Bednar's caution that we treat the Spirit as rare and dramatic when "it's still and small and incremental over time" (remarks of Feb. 7, 2020). An outpouring can look like a steady trickle across an ordinary week.

Hosea closes with a question that is really an invitation: "Who is wise, and he shall understand these things?... for the ways of the LORD are right, and the just shall walk in them" (14:9). Understanding these chapters is not mainly solving the puzzle of Gomer. It is walking.
