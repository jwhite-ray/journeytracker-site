The God Who Will Not Let Go: Hosea and Joel in Restoration Light

Written by an AI persona connecting the block to Restoration scripture and modern teaching — not a real person.

Moroni quoted Joel to a seventeen-year-old Joseph Smith; the Church's footnotes show Joseph Smith Translation changes in only two passages of this week's block, and both concern what kind of feeling God has; the Kirtland Temple dedication prayed for Joel's Pentecost; and President Russell M. Nelson built a whole account of the everlasting covenant on the Hebrew word Hosea leans on hardest, hesed. This essay follows those threads, and names one place where the fit is uneasy.

A God whose heart turns

The most startling verse in Hosea may be 11:8: "How shall I give thee up, Ephraim? how shall I deliver thee, Israel? ... mine heart is turned within me, my repentings are kindled together." God argues with Himself, and the next verse settles it: "I will not execute the fierceness of mine anger ... for I am God, and not man" (11:9).

In the Pearl of Great Price, Enoch sees "the God of heaven" look on the wicked "and he wept," and asks, "How is it that thou canst weep, seeing thou art holy, and from all eternity to all eternity?" (Moses 7:28–29). The answer sounds like Hosea: God had commanded His children "that they should love one another, and that they should choose me, their Father; but behold, they are without affection" (Moses 7:33). The Bible Dictionary describes Hosea's Israel in nearly the same terms, as "characterized always by want of affection, by treachery and infidelity," set against a God "who is Love" (Bible Dictionary, "Hosea, or Hoshea"). Zenos's allegory gives the same reluctance a gardener's voice: "the Lord of the vineyard wept, and said unto the servant: What could I have done more for my vineyard?" and "it grieveth me that I should lose the trees of my vineyard" (Jacob 5:41, 51). In both, judgment is spoken aloud, then deferred by love.

Here the Joseph Smith Translation is precise. The Church's footnote to Hosea 11:8 gives the JST ending as "toward thee, and my mercies are extended to gather thee" (Hosea 11:8, footnote b). God's heart is no longer turned "within" Him but "toward" Ephraim, and the "repentings" become "mercies ... extended to gather." The JST does the same thing to Joel 2:13–14. The KJV says God "repenteth him of the evil"; the JST reads "and he will turn away the evil from you," and moves the repentance onto the people: "Therefore repent, and who knoweth but he will return and leave a blessing behind him" (Joel 2:13–14, footnotes). Both revisions keep the divine emotion and remove the implication that God changes His mind like a person who has erred, the line Hosea himself draws in 11:9: "for I am God, and not man." The JST does not cool Hosea's God down; it makes the passion steady, always aimed at gathering.

Betrothal, hesed, and the covenant path

Hosea's tender climax is 2:19–20: "I will betroth thee unto me for ever; yea, I will betroth thee unto me in righteousness, and in judgment, and in lovingkindness, and in mercies. I will even betroth thee unto me in faithfulness: and thou shalt know the LORD." The word behind "lovingkindness" is hesed, which recurs in Hosea's charge that there is "no truth, nor mercy, nor knowledge of God in the land" (4:1) and in "I desired mercy, and not sacrifice" (6:6).

President Russell M. Nelson's article "The Everlasting Covenant" (Liahona, October 2022) is the fullest modern prophetic commentary on that word. He notes that the King James translators "often chose 'lovingkindness'" and sometimes "mercy" and "goodness," and defines the word as "a unique term describing a covenant relationship in which both parties are bound to be loyal and faithful to each other." He applies it directly to marriage: "A celestial marriage is such a covenant relationship." And he makes Hosea's claim in modern words: "God will not abandon His relationship with those who have forged such a bond with Him," and "should they stray, He will help them find their way back to Him."

That is Hosea 2 and 14 in summary. He also ties the temple to this covenant: "A marriage covenant made in the temple is tied directly to that Abrahamic covenant." Hosea's betrothal vows name the covenant's terms, and its outcome is knowledge: "thou shalt know the LORD" (2:20). Jesus defined eternal life the same way (John 17:3). In Hosea the opposite of covenant is not mainly disobedience but ignorance of a person: "My people are destroyed for lack of knowledge" (4:6).

Hosea 2:16 matters for a covenant theology of marriage: "thou shalt call me Ishi; and shalt call me no more Baali." Both can mean "my husband," but baʻal also means "master" and names the rival god. The renewed covenant trades ownership language for partnership language.

Where the metaphor strains

A Latter-day Saint reader cannot honestly skip the first half of chapter 2. The husband there threatens to "strip her naked" (2:3), to "discover her lewdness in the sight of her lovers" (2:10), and to have no mercy on her children (2:4). Applied to God and a nation, it dramatizes covenant curses (drought, failed harvests, exile) that followed Israel's crediting Baal with the corn, wine, and oil the LORD gave (2:8–9). Applied to human husbands, it would be abuse.

Restoration scripture forbids that transfer. The Doctrine and Covenants says that "No power or influence can or ought to be maintained by virtue of the priesthood, only by persuasion, by long-suffering, by gentleness and meekness, and by love unfeigned" (D&C 121:41), and President Nelson's definition of hesed makes the obligation mutual: "both parties are bound to be loyal and faithful." Hosea's metaphor teaches how God responds to covenant betrayal; it is not a model for how a husband treats a wife. And Hosea undercuts its own threats. The chapter that opens with exposure turns on one word: "Therefore, behold, I will allure her, and bring her into the wilderness, and speak comfortably unto her" (2:14). The logic of judgment is overruled by the logic of courtship.

Roadblocks on the wrong road

In "God's Intent Is to Bring You Home" (April 2024), Elder Patrick Kearon described a policeman who seemed to enjoy turning cars away from a roadblock, then said, "No one has built a roadblock and stationed someone there to turn you around and send you away. ... God is in relentless pursuit of you."

Hosea's God does build one roadblock, and where He puts it is the point: "I will hedge up thy way with thorns, and make a wall, that she shall not find her paths. And she shall follow after her lovers, but she shall not overtake them ... then shall she say, I will go and return to my first husband" (2:6–7). The thorns go across the road away from Him, never across the road home. Then "the valley of Achor" becomes "a door of hope" (2:15). Achor was where Achan was stoned, in a story that puns on the name: "Why hast thou troubled us?" (Joshua 7:24–26). That story ends with "the LORD turned from the fierceness of his anger" (Joshua 7:26), the phrase Hosea's God uses in 11:9.

Chapter 3 makes the pursuit concrete: Hosea is told to "love a woman ... yet an adulteress, according to the love of the LORD toward the children of Israel" (3:1), and he pays for her: "So I bought her to me for fifteen pieces of silver, and for an homer of barley" (3:2). Redemption, buying back, is literal here before it becomes doctrinal.

"Ye are the sons of the living God"

Hosea 1 names a child Lo-ammi, "not my people," and reverses the name within the same chapter: "there it shall be said unto them, Ye are the sons of the living God" (1:10; applied by Paul to Gentiles in Romans 9:25–26). The Book of Mormon gives this covenant renaming its clearest form: King Benjamin's people "shall be called the children of Christ, his sons, and his daughters; for behold, this day he hath spiritually begotten you" (Mosiah 5:7). In both, identity is something God confers by covenant, and something He can restore.

Mercy, not sacrifice; hearts, not garments

"For I desired mercy, and not sacrifice; and the knowledge of God more than burnt offerings" (6:6) is the verse Jesus quoted twice to His critics, once with "go ye and learn what that meaneth" and once with "if ye had known what this meaneth" (Matthew 9:13; 12:7). Joel says the same in his own terms: "rend your heart, and not your garments" (Joel 2:13). Its footnote points to D&C 64:34: "the Lord requireth the heart and a willing mind."

The risen Christ takes this to its endpoint in the Book of Mormon: "your sacrifices and your burnt offerings shall be done away ... And ye shall offer for a sacrifice unto me a broken heart and a contrite spirit" (3 Nephi 9:19–20). Hosea 14:2 anticipates it: "take with you words" and offer "the calves of our lips," prayer in place of bulls. The Church footnotes that verse to D&C 59:8: "Thou shalt offer a sacrifice unto the Lord thy God in righteousness, even that of a broken heart and a contrite spirit."

Hosea also sets two kinds of moisture against each other. Israel's goodness (hesed again) "is as a morning cloud, and as the early dew it goeth away" (6:4). But in chapter 14 God becomes the dew: "I will be as the dew unto Israel: he shall grow as the lily" (14:5). The footnote to 14:5 points to D&C 128:19, Joseph Smith's 1842 letter on the redemption of the dead: "As the dews of Carmel, so shall the knowledge of God descend upon them!" That answers Hosea 4:1 and 6:6 almost word for word: the missing "knowledge of God" arrives as dew. Hosea 10:12 adds urgency, "it is time to seek the LORD," which Amulek restates in the terms of the plan of salvation: "this life is the time for men to prepare to meet God" (Alma 34:32).

Ransom from the grave

Hosea 13:4, "there is no saviour beside me," is footnoted in the Church's edition to 2 Nephi 25:20: "there is none other name given under heaven save it be this Jesus Christ." Latter-day Saints read the LORD of Hosea as the premortal Jehovah, so the verse becomes a statement about Christ.

Verse 14 is harder: "I will ransom them from the power of the grave; I will redeem them from death: O death, I will be thy plagues; O grave, I will be thy destruction: repentance shall be hid from mine eyes." Coming after a lion, a leopard, and a bear (13:7–8) and before Samaria's fall (13:16), it can be read as a summons of death against Ephraim; the NRSVUE renders the opening lines as questions ("Shall I ransom them from the power of Sheol?"), and the ESV footnotes that option (Hosea 13:14, ESV and NRSVUE). Paul read it as a promise, and his version, "O death, where is thy sting? O grave, where is thy victory?" (1 Corinthians 15:55), became the Christian reading. Abinadi teaches that "there is a resurrection, therefore the grave hath no victory, and the sting of death is swallowed up in Christ" (Mosiah 16:8), and Jacob praises God for delivering us from "that monster, death and hell," explaining that "the grave must deliver up its captive bodies" (2 Nephi 9:10–12). The Restoration reading need not deny the verse's bleak setting; it holds that the God who once called death against a rebellious kingdom has, in Christ, become death's plague.

One more link, with a caveat: the KJV's "they like men have transgressed the covenant" (6:7) renders Hebrew keʼadam, which the ESV translates "like Adam"; the NRSVUE conjectures a place, "at Adam" (Hosea 6:7, ESV and NRSVUE). If "like Adam" is right, chapter 6 holds both ends of the plan of salvation within a few verses: the Fall in 6:7, and in 6:2 the "third day" hope Christians have long heard as pointing to the Resurrection.

"I will pour out my spirit"

In September 1823, Moroni "quoted the second chapter of Joel, from the twenty-eighth verse to the last. He also said that this was not yet fulfilled, but was soon to be" (Joseph Smith—History 1:41). Peter had already said of Pentecost, "this is that which was spoken by the prophet Joel" (Acts 2:16–17). Pentecost was a fulfillment; Moroni announced a larger one.

The Restoration tied that larger fulfillment to temples. At the Kirtland Temple dedication in 1836, Joseph Smith prayed, "Let it be fulfilled upon them, as upon those on the day of Pentecost; let the gift of tongues be poured out upon thy people" (D&C 109:36–37). A week later the Lord accepted the house and said, "this is the beginning of the blessing which shall be poured out upon the heads of my people" (D&C 110:9–10). Joel's "sanctify a fast, call a solemn assembly" (2:15) reappears in the command to the early Saints to "call a solemn assembly" and "sanctify yourselves" (D&C 88:70, 74). Joel even calls the bridegroom out of his chamber for the assembly (2:16); the Doctrine and Covenants makes the Bridegroom Christ: "the Bridegroom cometh; go ye out to meet him. Prepare yourselves for the great day of the Lord" (D&C 133:10).

Joseph Smith used Joel's verb from Liberty Jail: one might as well try "to stop the Missouri river in its decreed course ... as to hinder the Almighty from pouring down knowledge from heaven upon the heads of the Latter-day Saints" (D&C 121:33). President Nelson quoted that line in "Revelation for the Church, Revelation for Our Lives" (April 2018), the talk the lesson cites for his warning that "in coming days, it will not be possible to survive spiritually without the guiding, directing, comforting, and constant influence of the Holy Ghost." Joel's list (sons, daughters, old men, young men, servants, handmaids, 2:28–29) is the scriptural ground for that expectation: revelation is poured on "all flesh."

The ending Hosea chose

Hosea closes not with Samaria's fall but with an invitation and a promise: "O Israel, return unto the LORD thy God" (14:1); "I will heal their backsliding, I will love them freely" (14:4). Elder D. Todd Christofferson's "The Love of God" (October 2021) offers a useful caution about that word "freely." He warns against stretching God's all-embracing love into a belief that God's "blessings are 'unconditional' and that salvation is 'unconditional.' They are not." He also says that "The love of the Father and the Son is freely given but also includes hopes and expectations." Hosea 14 holds that balance: the love is unearned and arrives before Israel has changed. But the chapter ends with a road: "the ways of the LORD are right, and the just shall walk in them: but the transgressors shall fall therein" (14:9). The God who hedged the wrong road with thorns has opened the right one, and He waits on it as dew.
