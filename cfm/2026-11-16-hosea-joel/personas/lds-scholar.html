Samaria's Prophet and Zion's Spirit: Hosea and Joel Through Critical Eyes and Restoration Questions

Written by an AI persona in the voice of a Latter-day Saint academic — not a real scholar.

What kind of book we are holding

Hosea is the only writing prophet who came from the northern kingdom and spoke to it, and his book is the place where Israel's covenant theology is most raw. The superscription dates him by four Judean kings, "Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah," but by only one Israelite king, "Jeroboam the son of Joash" (Hosea 1:1). That lopsided list is the first clue to how the book reached us. A northern prophet's oracles survived the fall of Samaria in 722 BC because someone carried them south, and the Judean dating frame is best read as the work of those who preserved them. Many scholars detect the same hands in lines that turn toward Judah: "But I will have mercy upon the house of Judah" (1:7), and the hope that Israel will "seek the LORD their God, and David their king" (3:5), a striking phrase in the mouth of a man whose audience had rejected the Davidic house two centuries earlier.

The Hebrew of Hosea is notoriously difficult. Modern commentators divide over how much of that difficulty is textual damage and how much is dialect and compressed poetry. Francis I. Andersen and David Noel Freedman, in their Anchor Bible commentary (1980), took the minority position that the received text is "basically sound," as one reviewer summarized it, and translated it with conspicuous fidelity even where the result is awkward English. A. A. Macintosh's International Critical Commentary volume (1997) works the same problem through philology and medieval Jewish lexicography. Hans Walter Wolff's Hermeneia commentary (English 1974) remains the standard form-critical treatment. A Latter-day Saint reader should find nothing threatening in any of this. The eighth article of faith already assumes the Bible is a transmitted text, and the Restoration's own scripture includes the report that Moroni quoted Malachi "with a little variation from the way it reads in our Bibles" (Joseph Smith—History 1:36). Taking the editorial history seriously is a way of taking the book seriously.

The marriage: biography, vision, or parable?

"Go, take unto thee a wife of whoredoms and children of whoredoms" (1:2). Readers have argued over this command for two thousand years. Maimonides, in the Guide of the Perplexed II:46, held that the whole account of Hosea's marriage, the births, and the naming of the children took place in a prophetic vision, part of his general rule that the prophets' strange symbolic acts are told within their visions. Many modern scholars read the account as real events, but they disagree about Gomer. Was she a prostitute when Hosea married her? A woman later unfaithful, described in hindsight? A participant in fertility rites?

The last option, the "sacred prostitution" thesis, dominated twentieth-century commentary and should now be handled with real caution. Stephanie Lynn Budin's The Myth of Sacred Prostitution in Antiquity (Cambridge, 2008) argues that the institution is a scholarly construct built on misread sources. Her conclusion about the ancient Near East is blunt: there were no sacred prostitutes there. Not every specialist accepts her full case, but the burden of proof has shifted, and a reader should not take it for granted that Gomer served at a Baal shrine.

Latter-day Saint scholarship has generally avoided the most literal reading. Kent P. Jackson, writing for BYU's Religious Studies Center in 1984, judged it unlikely that God commanded Hosea to marry a woman already immoral. He preferred either later infidelity, with "wife of whoredoms" as anticipation, or allegory, and he insisted that "the main issue in Hosea's prophecy is not his relationship with his wife but God's relationship with Israel." Aaron P. Schade's more recent essay in Approaching Holiness (2021) likewise reads the family as "a metaphor of his message to the people." Chapter 3 is told in the first person, and its unnamed "woman beloved of her friend, yet an adulteress" (3:1) may be Gomer or may be someone else. The price, "fifteen pieces of silver, and... an homer and an half homer of barley" (3:2), sounds like a real transaction, and that is about as far as the evidence goes.

The children's names deserve more attention than the marriage. Jezreel, Lo-ruhamah ("not pitied"), and Lo-ammi ("not my people") function as walking oracles. The first carries a historical problem. Hosea 1:4 announces, "I will avenge the blood of Jezreel upon the house of Jehu." Yet in 2 Kings 10:30 the LORD commends Jehu for that same purge: "thou hast done well in executing that which is right in mine eyes," and promises his sons the throne to the fourth generation. Elisha's circle had anointed Jehu (2 Kings 9:1–10). A century later, another prophet calls the founding bloodshed of the dynasty a crime. Harmonizations exist, such as the claim that Jehu exceeded his commission, but the plain reading is that one prophetic voice judged an earlier prophetically sanctioned act more severely. For a tradition that teaches continuing revelation, that is not an embarrassment. It shows the prophetic tradition reassessing its own past, which is what living prophecy does.

The metaphor's dark side

The lesson asks how covenant is like marriage. Hosea 2 shows the analogy's dangers. The husband threatens to "strip her naked, and set her as in the day that she was born" (2:3), to "discover her lewdness in the sight of her lovers" (2:10), and to "hedge up thy way with thorns" (2:6). Renita J. Weems's Battered Love: Marriage, Sex, and Violence in the Hebrew Prophets (Fortress, 1995) argued that metaphors like this, here and in Jeremiah and Ezekiel 16 and 23, lend divine authority to a pattern of control, public shaming, and reconciliation that looks uncomfortably like domestic abuse. The critique is serious. Hosea 2:14 begins with "Therefore." The wooing comes after the punishment, in the same speech.

Two observations help, without making the problem go away. First, the metaphor runs in one direction. The divine husband is always faithful and the human wife is always guilty, because the image was built to indict a nation, not to describe human marriage. A Latter-day Saint, for whom eternal marriage stands at the center of the plan of salvation, should be careful not to reverse the analogy and draw from Hosea 2 a model for how husbands treat wives. Restoration teaching on marriage points the other way: "No power or influence can or ought to be maintained by virtue of the priesthood, only by persuasion, by long-suffering, by gentleness and meekness, and by love unfeigned" (Doctrine and Covenants 121:41). Second, the chapter itself undermines the logic of ownership. "Thou shalt call me Ishi; and shalt call me no more Baali" (2:16) is a pun on two Hebrew words for husband. ʼIshi means "my man"; baʻali means "my lord, my owner," and is also the name of the rival god. The renewed covenant is described as giving up the language of mastery.

Baal, Asherah, and an eighth-century jar

Hosea's central charge is theological misattribution: "she did not know that I gave her corn, and wine, and oil" (2:8). Israel credited the storm god Baal with the rain and the harvest. Mark S. Smith's The Early History of God (2nd ed., Eerdmans, 2002) traces how Baal, once an accepted presence in Israelite religion, became Yahweh's rival during the monarchy. On this account, Hosea's polemic is a late and decisive stage in that separation.

Archaeology shows how mixed northern religion actually was. At Kuntillet ʻAjrud, a remote way station in eastern Sinai, excavators under Zeʼev Meshel found storage jars with Hebrew blessings. One reads "by YHWH of Samaria and his asherah." The final excavation report (Israel Exploration Society, 2012) dates the site to the first half of the eighth century BC, Hosea's century, and notes a strong influence from the northern kingdom. Scholars still debate whether "his asherah" names a goddess consort or a sacred pole or tree, and the drawings once taken as Yahweh and his consort are now widely identified as the Egyptian god Bes (see Hershel Shanks, "The Persisting Uncertainties of Kuntillet ʻAjrud," Biblical Archaeology Review, 2012). Either way, the jar records the kind of popular Yahwism Hosea attacked: the LORD worshipped "of Samaria," at local sanctuaries, alongside a feminine cult symbol.

This is where scholarship meets a Restoration concern. Some Latter-day Saint scholars have used the Asherah evidence constructively. Daniel C. Peterson's "Nephi and His Asherah" (Journal of Book of Mormon Studies, 2000) reads Nephi's tree-and-virgin vision (1 Nephi 11) against Asherah's links to sacred trees and motherhood, and the article is often brought up in discussions of a Mother in Heaven. That work is interesting, but a reader of Hosea should see its limits. Hosea does not present the asherah-and-Baal complex as a lost truth. He presents it as infidelity: "My people ask counsel at their stocks" (4:12), "Ephraim is joined to idols" (4:17). Whatever Latter-day Saints believe about a divine Mother rests on modern revelation and teaching, not on rehabilitating the cult that Hosea, the prophet closest to it in time and place, condemned.

Hesed and a covenant broken "like Adam"

The words of Hosea 4:1 are the book's thesis: "there is no truth, nor mercy, nor knowledge of God in the land." The word translated "mercy" is hesed. It appears again in the betrothal ("in lovingkindness," 2:19), in the complaint "your goodness is as a morning cloud" (6:4), and in the verse Jesus quoted twice, "I desired mercy, and not sacrifice" (6:6; Matthew 9:13; 12:7). The KJV renders one Hebrew word three different ways in these verses, which shows how hard it is to translate. Jackson observed that "there is no English word that conveys the meaning of hesed accurately." President Russell M. Nelson made the same word the center of his October 2022 Liahona article "The Everlasting Covenant," defining it as "a special kind of love and mercy that God feels for and extends to those who have made a covenant with Him." Nelson did not cite Hosea, but no book of the Old Testament illustrates his definition better. In Hosea, hesed is covenant loyalty that keeps going after the other party has broken faith.

Hosea 6:7 shows how text criticism and Restoration theology can meet. The Hebrew keʼadam can be translated "like men" (KJV), "like Adam," or, with a one-letter emendation to beʼadam, "at Adam," a place. A town named Adam beside the Jordan appears in Joshua 3:16, and the next verses name Gilead and its bloodshed (6:8). English translations divide among all three readings. A Latter-day Saint will be drawn to "like Adam." Restoration scripture develops an Adamic covenant and priesthood more fully than the Bible does (Moses 5–6), and a pre-Sinai covenant with the first man would fit that reading. Scholarly honesty requires saying that the geographic reading has strong support, and that this verse cannot carry much doctrinal weight alone.

Texts that change in later hands

Hosea is also a case study in how later scripture reuses earlier scripture. "Out of Egypt have I called my son" (11:1) looks back to the Exodus. Matthew 2:15 applies it to the infant Jesus. The verse's own context, "they sacrificed unto Baalim" (11:2), shows Matthew was reading typologically rather than predictively. "In the third day he will raise us up" (6:2) is in context the people's too-easy confidence, which God immediately answers with "your goodness is as a morning cloud" (6:4).

Hosea 13:14 is the sharpest case. The KJV prints a promise: "I will ransom them from the power of the grave." But the Hebrew clauses can be read as questions ("Shall I ransom them?"), and the line ends "repentance [or compassion] shall be hid from mine eyes," which fits a threat. The verse that follows is the east wind drying Ephraim's spring, and 13:16 is the most violent line in the book. Many commentators therefore read 13:14 as God calling death's plagues down on Ephraim. Paul quotes it, through a Greek form close to the Septuagint, as a taunt at defeated death: "O death, where is thy sting?" (1 Corinthians 15:55). Latter-day Saints already accept that inspired reuse can carry a text past its original meaning. Moroni's 1823 quotations to Joseph Smith are presented as the same kind of authoritative reapplication. Seeing both meanings is more honest than letting Paul's use hide Hosea's.

Joel: an undated book and a promise "not yet fulfilled"

Joel has no royal dating at all. Estimates range from the ninth century to the Persian period. James L. Crenshaw's Anchor Bible commentary (1995) defends a postexilic date, as does much current scholarship. The evidence includes the absence of any king, a functioning temple run by priests and elders (1:13–14; 2:17), Judeans sold "unto the Grecians" (3:6), and the silence about Assyria and Babylon. Joel also quotes earlier prophets openly. "The LORD also shall roar out of Zion, and utter his voice from Jerusalem" (3:16) repeats Amos 1:2 word for word. "In mount Zion and in Jerusalem shall be deliverance, as the LORD hath said" (2:32) cites Obadiah 17 and marks the citation. "Beat your plowshares into swords" (3:10) deliberately reverses Isaiah 2:4 and Micah 4:3. Joel is already reading a body of prophecy.

The locusts of chapter 1 read as a real agricultural disaster, with four named stages of insects (1:4), withered vines and figs, and the grain and drink offerings "cut off from the house of the LORD" (1:9). In chapter 2 they become an army with "the appearance of horses" (2:4) and are called "my great army which I sent among you" (2:25). The response is liturgical: "rend your heart, and not your garments" (2:13). The confession that follows, "gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness [hesed]," draws on Exodus 34:6, the same formula Jonah resents in Jonah 4:2.

Then comes the passage that matters most to the Restoration (Joel 2:28–32; Hebrew 3:1–5). "I will pour out my spirit upon all flesh; and your sons and your daughters shall prophesy" (2:28), extended to "the servants and... the handmaids" (2:29). It answers Moses' wish, "would God that all the LORD's people were prophets" (Numbers 11:29). Peter declared at Pentecost "this is that which was spoken by the prophet Joel" (Acts 2:16), changing Joel's vague "afterward" to "in the last days" (Acts 2:17). Eighteen centuries later Moroni quoted the same verses to Joseph Smith and said "that this was not yet fulfilled, but was soon to be" (Joseph Smith—History 1:41). The two claims do not cancel each other. They show that a single prophecy can be fulfilled more than once, and that apostles and angels have treated Joel's promise as open-ended. The text gives the prophetic gift to daughters and to slaves. A Latter-day Saint reader who takes Moroni seriously should take that breadth seriously too.
