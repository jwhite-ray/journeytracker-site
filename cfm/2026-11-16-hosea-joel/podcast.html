Context

NATHAN: The panel's LDS scholar points out that Hosea is the only writing prophet who came from the northern kingdom and spoke to it. He calls the nation "Ephraim." He knows its shrines. The academic scholar notes he mocks Bethel, "house of God," as Beth-aven, "house of iniquity."

ELI: A northerner talking to northerners.

NATHAN: And yet the first verse dates him "in the days of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, kings of Judah, and in the days of Jeroboam the son of Joash, king of Israel." Four southern kings, one northern. The LDS scholar calls that lopsided list "the first clue to how the book reached us." Samaria fell to Assyria in 722 BC. A northern prophet's words survived because someone carried them south, and most critical scholars think they were edited there.

THE QUESTIONER: You can see the seams. Hosea 1:7 suddenly says, "But I will have mercy upon the house of Judah." Chapter 3 has Israel seeking "the LORD their God, and David their king." The academic scholar puts it well: a northern prophet had little reason to look to the Davidic dynasty, and a Judean editor after 722 had every reason to.

ELI: What was the north like in those years?

NATHAN: Hosea starts in the last prosperous years of Jeroboam II, who died about 750. Then it falls apart. Second Kings 15 records four kings assassinated within a generation. You can hear it in Hosea: "As for Samaria, her king is cut off as the foam upon the water." Or 13:11: "I gave thee a king in mine anger, and took him away in my wrath."

ELI: (dryly) Kings coming and going like foam.

NATHAN: And the Assyrians confirm it. The academic scholar cites the inscriptions of Tiglath-pileser III. He lists "Menahem of Samaria" among his tributaries, and one inscription boasts that the Israelites killed Pekah, "their king," and that he himself put Hoshea on the throne.

ELI: The Assyrian king bragging about installing Israel's king.

In the Hebrew canon, the twelve "minor" prophets are one scroll, the Book of the Twelve. James Nogalski argued it was stitched together with catchwords, and there's an example in our reading. Joel 3:16, "The LORD also shall roar out of Zion, and utter his voice from Jerusalem," is almost word for word the opening of Amos, which follows Joel in the Hebrew order.

ELI: So Joel hands off to Amos.

NATHAN: The details are disputed, but the Twelve were read as a unit in antiquity. And Joel himself is a mystery. Joel 1:1 names his father, Pethuel, and nothing else. No king, no datable event. Proposals run from the ninth century BC to the Persian period.

The text

Hosea insists the LORD sends it.

NATHAN: Then a pun in verse 16: "thou shalt call me Ishi; and shalt call me no more Baali." Both can mean "my husband," but baʻal also means "master" or "owner," and it's the rival god's name. Partner, not owner.

The hard ground

ELI: Questioner, start where everyone starts. Did God really tell a prophet to marry a prostitute?

THE QUESTIONER: The plain sense is startling, and the unease is ancient. The Babylonian Talmud, Pesachim 87, tells a story. God tells Hosea that Israel has sinned, and Hosea, instead of pleading for them, suggests God "exchange them for another nation."

ELI: (wincing) That's a bad answer for a prophet.

THE QUESTIONER: That's the sages' point. So God has him marry a harlot, have children, then tells him to send her away. Hosea says he can't. And God turns it back on him: neither can I abandon Israel. There the marriage is real, and the lesson is aimed at the prophet.

NATHAN: And Maimonides went the other way.

THE QUESTIONER: In the Guide for the Perplexed, he says of the marriage, the births, and the naming: "All this passed in a prophetic vision." Then Kent P. Jackson, writing for BYU's Religious Studies Center in 1984, laid out three options. Hosea knowingly married a promiscuous woman. Or Gomer was faithful at first and strayed later, so "wife of whoredoms" is hindsight. Or it's allegory. He rejects the first and calls the question of literal history "irrelevant" to the book's purpose.

NATHAN: And the hindsight reading dominated critical scholarship for a long time.

THE QUESTIONER: It did, though Wilhelm Rudolph objected that chapter 1 never actually says Gomer strayed after the wedding. And the old theory that she took part in a cultic sex rite, so-called sacred prostitution, was badly undercut by Stephanie Lynn Budin's 2008 book. This week's lesson assumes the literal reading: "Hosea's wife, Gomer, was unfaithful to him." That's defensible. But faithful interpreters, Jackson included, have held others. You're not choosing between faith and doubt here. You're choosing among readings faithful people have held for centuries.

ELI: The practical disciple said you don't have to settle it to be taught by it.

THE QUESTIONER: I agree.

NATHAN: Then chapter 2. The stripping.

THE QUESTIONER: This is the one I most want to steel-man, because it isn't only academic. The husband threatens to "strip her naked, and set her as in the day that she was born," to refuse mercy to her children, to "discover her lewdness in the sight of her lovers." Renita J. Weems, in Battered Love, asks how divine love came to be expressed through images of a husband battering and shaming his wife, and argues it can't be dismissed as a literary device, because metaphors teach readers what love looks like.

ELI: And the order matters.

THE QUESTIONER: The order is the hardest part. A reader who has survived an abusive marriage hears "I will allure her" right after "strip her" and recognizes the cycle. The LDS scholar makes the same observation: verse 14 begins with "Therefore." The wooing comes after the punishment, in the same speech.

NATHAN: So what are the serious responses?

THE QUESTIONER: Andrew Sloane, in an evangelical reply, acknowledges "the offensive power of the texts" but argues the shocking features are better explained by the real violence coming, Assyrian siege and deportation, and the seriousness of the breach, than by misogyny. And the text works against its own metaphor: Ishi, not Baali, as if ownership itself has to end. And 11:9: "I am God, and not man."

NATHAN: The LDS scholar adds that the metaphor runs one direction.

THE QUESTIONER: The divine husband is always faithful and the wife always guilty, because the image was built to indict a nation, not to describe human marriage. So don't reverse it into a model for husbands. The LDS scholar and the doctrinal connector both quote Doctrine and Covenants 121:41: "No power or influence can or ought to be maintained by virtue of the priesthood, only by persuasion, by long-suffering, by gentleness and meekness, and by love unfeigned."

ELI: And the practical disciple is very direct, and I want to say it because someone listening may need it. If someone in your home is being hedged in or threatened with exposure, that is not a Hosea story. It's abuse, and it calls for help, not patience.

THE QUESTIONER: I'm glad you said that. There's also a Latter-day Saint version of the problem. President Nelson teaches that hesed "has no adequate English equivalent" and that "a celestial marriage is such a covenant relationship." That deepens the analogy, and raises the stakes. If marriage models how God loves, what do we do with a model husband who threatens public humiliation? One answer: Hosea works within eighth-century marriage norms and pushes against them. Another: modern teaching on marriage has moved far beyond what the metaphor assumes. Neither makes 2:3 and 2:10 comfortable. And nothing requires that they be.

ELI: (pause) Okay. Jezreel.

THE QUESTIONER: The first son is named Jezreel, "for yet a little while, and I will avenge the blood of Jezreel upon the house of Jehu." Jezreel is where Jehu wiped out the house of Ahab, a coup Elisha's messenger set in motion. And in 2 Kings 10:30 the LORD praises Jehu: "thou hast done well in executing that which is right in mine eyes."

NATHAN: One prophetic tradition approves the bloodbath.

THE QUESTIONER: And a later prophet calls it guilt to be avenged. G. I. Davies: "For Hosea murder is a sin... and the end does not justify the means." Harmonizers note that 2 Kings 10:31 already faults Jehu, so maybe he carried out a just sentence for the wrong motives. Others see inspired writers disagreeing. And the LDS scholar's take is striking: for a tradition that teaches continuing revelation, this isn't an embarrassment. It shows the prophetic tradition reassessing its own past, which is what living prophecy does.

ELI: And 13:16. I almost don't want to read it.

THE QUESTIONER: "Samaria shall become desolate; for she hath rebelled against her God: they shall fall by the sword: their infants shall be dashed in pieces, and their women with child shall be ripped up." And just before, God says, "I will meet them as a bear that is bereaved of her whelps." This was real Iron Age warfare; 2 Kings 15:16 credits Israel's own King Menahem with the same atrocity. Some answer that 13:16 describes what conquest brings, not what God approves. The verbs are passive. Others find both answers too easy, because in 13:7 and 8 God himself, in the first person, is the predator.

ELI: What do you do with that?

THE QUESTIONER: I notice where the book puts it. 13:16 sits directly before "O Israel, return unto the LORD thy God." Hosea sets the horror and the invitation side by side and doesn't reconcile them. And the practical disciple's counsel is wise: choose carefully what you teach children, but as an adult, don't let the lovely verses of chapter 14 make you forget Hosea wrote to people about to lose everything.

NATHAN: Even 13:9 holds both: "O Israel, thou hast destroyed thyself; but in me is thine help."

ELI: Now Matthew's use of 11:1.

THE QUESTIONER: "Called my son out of Egypt" looks back to the Exodus, and the next verse says "they sacrificed unto Baalim." Matthew 2:15 applies it to the child Jesus, and our chapter heading reads it "in similitude of our Lord." Critics call that proof-texting. Defenders call it typology, Israel's story relived by Israel's Messiah. That's a real method. But it isn't the straightforward prediction a casual reader of Matthew assumes.

NATHAN: And 13:14 is sharper.

THE QUESTIONER: The King James prints a promise: "I will ransom them from the power of the grave; I will redeem them from death: O death, I will be thy plagues; O grave, I will be thy destruction." Paul uses it in 1 Corinthians 15, and the lesson teaches it to children as a statement about Christ. But the verse ends "repentance shall be hid from mine eyes," and Samaria's destruction follows. Davies argues the context points to questions, "Shall I ransom them?", then a summons to Death. The rabbi reports Rashi reads it as a threat too.

NATHAN: The doctrinal connector's Restoration reading?

THE QUESTIONER: Honest, I think. It doesn't deny the bleak setting. It holds that the God who once called death against a rebellious kingdom has, in Christ, become death's plague. Same with 6:2, the "third day," which in context is the people's own shallow confession. None of this shows the apostles were wrong. It shows that "Hosea predicted this" and "a New Testament writer found Christ here" are different claims.

ELI: And Joel's date. You promised.

THE QUESTIONER: (laughing) I did. The Bible Dictionary allows anything from before 850 BC to after the return from exile. Many critical scholars favor the late end: no king, a functioning temple and priesthood, Judeans sold "unto the Grecians," and Joel apparently quoting Amos and Obadiah. Hans Walter Wolff put it in the fourth century BC; William Shea's review judged that "too late." Joel 3:10, "Beat your plowshares into swords," even reverses Isaiah and Micah.

NATHAN: And Peter versus Moroni.

THE QUESTIONER: The tension most specific to Latter-day Saints. Peter says "this is that." Moroni says "not yet fulfilled, but was soon to be." The usual reconciliation is layered fulfillment: Pentecost was a real first installment, and the full outpouring, the sun darkened and the moon turned to blood, still lies ahead. Elder Oaks, as the six-outlooks voice notes, makes that case. But readers construct this reconciliation. Neither text states it. And Peter's "this is that" sounds complete on its own terms.

ELI: (gently) So where does that leave somebody who feels a bit shaken?

THE QUESTIONER: Hosea ends with a warning to readers: "Who is wise, and he shall understand these things? prudent, and he shall know them? for the ways of the LORD are right, and the just shall walk in them: but the transgressors shall fall therein." The book admits it can trip a reader. The tender passages are real. So are 2:3, 13:8, and 13:16. A faithful reading doesn't have to explain the hard verses away. You can admit the same book holds a God who says "mine heart is turned within me" and a prophet who couldn't picture covenant love without threats of exposure. And you can wrestle with that, the way Jacob does in chapter 12, without expecting the blessing to come easily.

ELI: (after a beat) Thank you. Truly.

THE QUESTIONER: Thank you both.
