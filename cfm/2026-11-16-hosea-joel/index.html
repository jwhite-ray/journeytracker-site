I Will Love Them Freely: Hosea's Marriage, Joel's Locusts, and a God Who Will Not Let Go

≈30 min · listen or read · the week's introduction — not one of the five sessions

Nathan and Eli open the week with Israel's morning-cloud goodness, then walk Hosea's marriage, the wilderness wooing, "mercy, and not sacrifice," a Father who cannot give Ephraim up, and Joel's locusts and outpoured Spirit. Then the hard ground with the panel's honest questioner: what God asked of Hosea, the threats of Hosea 2, Jezreel, Matthew's use of 11:1, and when Joel was written.

This week's reading puts two very different prophets side by side. Hosea preached in the northern kingdom of Israel in the eighth century before Christ, in the last prosperous years of Jeroboam II and the bloody decades that followed, until Assyria swallowed Samaria in 722. He was told to "take unto thee a wife of whoredoms" (1:2), to give his children names that were sentences of judgment, and then to "go yet, love a woman beloved of her friend, yet an adulteress" (3:1). His book swings without warning between threats and tenderness, between a husband in a divorce court, a lion that tears, a father teaching a toddler to walk, and dew on a lily. Joel gives no date at all. He begins with a locust plague so complete that "joy is withered away from the sons of men" (Joel 1:12), calls the people to "rend your heart, and not your garments" (2:13), and promises that God "will pour out my spirit upon all flesh" (2:28). Hosea 7–9 are not assigned this week; the reading jumps from chapter 6 to chapter 10.

The panel reads these chapters from different places. The practical disciple hears a set of plain questions under all the strangeness, among them: Is your goodness the kind that lasts until noon? The rabbi points out that Jews meet Hosea mostly in pieces worked into prayer, and concludes, So the Hosea that Jews carry around is a book of betrothal and return. The honest questioner lists the hard verses, from the stripped wife to the "ripped up" women of 13:16, and promises those questions in their strongest form, the range of serious answers, and the places where the answers run out. The academic scholar begins with geography: Hosea is the only book among the Latter Prophets whose speaker plainly belongs to the northern kingdom of Israel. The LDS scholar walks through the book's editing and difficult Hebrew and concludes that taking the editorial history seriously is a way of taking the book seriously. The doctrinal connector follows Hosea and Joel into the Joseph Smith Translation, the Kirtland Temple, and modern prophetic teaching on covenant love, and names one place where the fit is uneasy. And the six-outlooks essay takes the passage with the longest reach, Joel 2:28–32, because of all the passages in this week's fourteen chapters, Joel 2:28–32 has the busiest afterlife.

The lesson's title comes from the last chapter of Hosea: "I will heal their backsliding, I will love them freely: for mine anger is turned away from him" (14:4). The book takes thirteen chapters to get there, and it does not pretend the road was gentle. The question running under all five sessions is how the same God can say "I will strip her naked" (2:3) and "How shall I give thee up, Ephraim?" (11:8), and what kind of love can hold both.

Session 1 (Hosea 1–3) meets Hosea's marriage and children, wrestles with whether God told a prophet to marry a prostitute, with a prophet who condemns what an earlier prophet approved, and with a husband who threatens to strip his wife, then follows the turn to "I will allure her" and the betrothal of 2:19–20. Session 2 (Hosea 4–6) hears the LORD's lawsuit against a land with "no truth, nor mercy, nor knowledge of God," digs into an eighth-century jar inscribed "YHWH of Samaria and his asherah," and wrestles with a third-day resurrection and with "like Adam." Session 3 (Hosea 10–13) watches a father teach his son to walk, hears God argue with himself in 11:8–9, and faces the book's most violent verses and the riddle of "O death, I will be thy plagues." Session 4 (Hosea 14 and Joel 1) takes words back to the LORD with the Jewish Sabbath of Return, hears "I will love them freely," and opens an undated book with a locust plague. Session 5 (Joel 2 and 3) rends hearts, gets back the years the locust ate, wrestles with Peter's "this is that" and Moroni's "not yet fulfilled," and ends with a capstone that reads Joel 2:28–32 six ways.

The questions at the end of each session are not a quiz. Most have more than one defensible answer, and several ask about your own week rather than the text.

25–30 minSession 1: A Wife, Three Names, and a Door of Hope

Reading assignment: Hosea 1, 2, 3

A northern prophet in a southern book (1:1)

Start with the first verse, because it tells you how the book reached you. Hosea prophesied "in the days of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, kings of Judah, and in the days of Jeroboam the son of Joash, king of Israel" (1:1). Four southern kings and one northern king, for a northern prophet speaking to the north. That lopsided list is the first clue to how the book reached us. Most critical scholars think Hosea's sayings were carried to Judah after Samaria fell and were edited there. The likeliest marks of that Judean hand are the lines that single out Judah for mercy (1:7) and the hope that Israel will return to "David their king" (3:5). A northern prophet had little reason to look to the Davidic dynasty. A Judean editor after 722 had every reason to. The commentator G. I. Davies, as the honest questioner reports, calls 1:7 "the clearest addition," with "a pro-Judaean standpoint," probably added before the exile. A belief in inspired editing can accommodate this, but the book clearly passed through more than one hand.

Expect the language to be hard going. Hosea's Hebrew is among the most obscure poetry in the Bible, full of rare words and lines that may be corrupt or may reflect a northern dialect we know poorly. The LDS scholar notes that the eighth article of faith already assumes a transmitted Bible, and that Moroni quoted Malachi "with a little variation" from the King James text (Joseph Smith—History 1:36). A Latter-day Saint reader should find nothing threatening in any of this.

Wrestle: did God tell a prophet to marry a prostitute? (1:2–3)

"Go, take unto thee a wife of whoredoms and children of whoredoms: for the land hath committed great whoredom, departing from the LORD. So he went and took Gomer the daughter of Diblaim" (1:2–3). Latter-day Saints, who expect prophets to model righteous family life, feel this keenly. So did ancient readers. There are four main ways of reading the command, and faithful people have held each of them.

A real marriage, aimed at the prophet. The Babylonian Talmud (Pesachim 87a–b) tells a story that four of the essays draw on. God tells Hosea that Israel has sinned, and Hosea, instead of pleading for them, suggests God trade them for another nation. So God has him marry a prostitute and father three children, then tells him to send her away. Hosea says he cannot. God answers that he cannot let Israel go either. The rabbi draws out the point: In the sages' reading the marriage is not mainly God teaching Israel. It is God teaching the prophet, who had forgotten that a prophet is supposed to be Israel's advocate. The children's terrible names are the price of his lack of love, and the book's first comfort comes when he finally intercedes. The practical disciple, who calls the story rabbinic storytelling rather than history, still finds it true to life: Hosea learns mercy by being bound to someone he cannot simply replace. Rashi follows the sages and takes the command literally, though he also reports that the Targum turned the verse into a prophecy against "the inhabitants of the cities of idolatry," so already in antiquity some readers would not let it stand literally.

A vision. Maimonides, in the Guide of the Perplexed II:46, held that the whole account of Hosea's marriage, the births, and the naming of the children took place in a prophetic vision, part of his general rule that the prophets' strange symbolic acts are told within their visions. Before him, Abraham ibn Ezra is blunt. "Heaven forbid," he writes, that God would command such a marriage. The rabbi adds a caution about his own tradition: Ibn Ezra's "Heaven forbid" protects the prophet's holiness, not Gomer's dignity.

A woman named in hindsight. The Hebrew phrase is unusual. The phrase ʼeshet zenunim (literally "a woman of fornications") uses an abstract plural, and many commentators take it to describe a disposition rather than a profession, perhaps named in hindsight after the woman proved unfaithful. That reading has a strong objection against it: G. I. Davies (New Century Bible, 1992) reports that the hindsight reading long dominated critical scholarship, but notes Wilhelm Rudolph's objection that chapter 1 never actually says Gomer strayed after the wedding.

An allegory, or a political figure. Kent P. Jackson, writing for BYU in 1984, laid out the options and set aside the most literal one. He rejects the first and calls the question of literal history "irrelevant" to the book's purpose. As the LDS scholar quotes him, he insisted that "the main issue in Hosea's prophecy is not his relationship with his wife but God's relationship with Israel." Aaron P. Schade's more recent essay in Approaching Holiness (2021) likewise reads the family as "a metaphor of his message to the people." Some academic readers make the wife a political figure. Gale A. Yee, in Poor Banished Children of Eve (2003), argues that the promiscuous wife stands for the male elite of priests and kings. And Brad E. Kelle's Hosea 2: Metaphor and Rhetoric in Historical Perspective (2005) notes that ancient Near Eastern texts often personify capital cities as women and argues that the wife of chapter 2 is Samaria itself during the Syro-Ephraimite crisis.

One older theory should be set aside. Twentieth-century commentators often said Gomer took part in "sacred prostitution" at a Baal shrine. Stephanie Lynn Budin's 2008 study took that institution apart, and her conclusion about the ancient Near East is blunt: there were no sacred prostitutes there. Not every specialist accepts her full case, but the burden of proof has shifted, and a reader should not take it for granted that Gomer served at a Baal shrine. The academic scholar draws the line clearly: Sexual language for religious disloyalty is metaphor, not evidence of a ritual.

The lesson itself takes the plain reading: Gomer "was unfaithful to him, and God pointed to this sad event." That is a defensible position, but faithful interpreters, including Jackson, writing for BYU's Religious Studies Center, have held others. The practical disciple suggests you do not have to decide before you learn from it: You do not need to settle the question to be taught by it: God chose the most intimate covenant people know, and the most painful way it breaks, to show how He experiences Israel.

Three children, three sentences (1:3–2:1)

Gomer bears a son, Jezreel; a daughter, Lo-ruhamah, "for I will no more have mercy upon the house of Israel"; and a son, Lo-ammi, "for ye are not my people, and I will not be your God" (1:4–9). The LDS scholar thinks these deserve more attention than the marriage does. Jezreel, Lo-ruhamah ("not pitied"), and Lo-ammi ("not my people") function as walking oracles.

Then, within the same chapter, the last name is reversed: "in the place where it was said unto them, Ye are not my people, there it shall be said unto them, Ye are the sons of the living God" (1:10). And 2:1 renames the other two: "Say ye unto your brethren, Ammi; and to your sisters, Ruhamah." The doctrinal connector sets this next to Paul, who applied the verse to Gentiles (Romans 9:25–26), and to King Benjamin's people, who "shall be called the children of Christ, his sons, and his daughters" (Mosiah 5:7). In both, identity is something God confers by covenant, and something He can restore. In the Talmud's story, this verse is the blessing that follows when Hosea finally prays for Israel.

Wrestle: a prophet against a prophet (1:4)

The first child's name carries a historical charge. "Call his name Jezreel; for yet a little while, and I will avenge the blood of Jezreel upon the house of Jehu" (1:4). Jezreel was where Jehu wiped out the house of Ahab, in a coup that began when a messenger of Elisha anointed him (2 Kings 9:1–10). And in 2 Kings 10:30 the LORD tells Jehu, "thou hast done well in executing that which is right in mine eyes," and promises his sons the throne to the fourth generation. Jeroboam II, the king on the throne when Hosea began, was of that dynasty. The children's names carry the politics.

So one prophetic tradition approves the bloodbath and a later prophet calls it guilt to be avenged. The essays handle this three ways. Harmonizers point out that Kings itself has reservations: Harmonizers note that 2 Kings 10:31 already faults Jehu, who "took no heed to walk in the law of the LORD," so he may have carried out a just sentence for the wrong motives and to excess. The academic scholar states the plain result: Here one prophetic voice judges an act that another biblical tradition endorses. And the LDS scholar finds that result congenial to Latter-day Saint belief rather than threatening: For a tradition that teaches continuing revelation, that is not an embarrassment. It shows the prophetic tradition reassessing its own past, which is what living prophecy does. The honest questioner's summary: Noticing the tension is close reading, not faithlessness.

The wife in the dock (2:2–13)

Chapter 2 opens in a courtroom. "Plead with your mother, plead: for she is not my wife, neither am I her husband" (2:2). Hosea 2 is a divorce-court speech: "she is not my wife, neither am I her husband" (2:2).

The charge is surprising. At its center chapter 2 is an argument about agriculture. The wife says, "I will go after my lovers, that give me my bread and my water, my wool and my flax, mine oil and my drink" (2:5). God answers: "she did not know that I gave her corn, and wine, and oil, and multiplied her silver and gold, which they prepared for Baal" (2:8). In the Canaanite stories, Baal was the storm god who brought rain and harvest. Hosea's central charge is theological misattribution: "she did not know that I gave her corn, and wine, and oil" (2:8). The practical disciple puts it in modern terms: Israel did not stop worshipping the LORD; she credited her paycheck to someone else. The modern version is rarely a carved image. It is the quiet assumption that the job, the market, or our own effort is what really feeds the family.

Look closely at the barrier in 2:6–7: "I will hedge up thy way with thorns, and make a wall, that she shall not find her paths. And she shall follow after her lovers, but she shall not overtake them... then shall she say, I will go and return to my first husband." The doctrinal connector sets this beside a talk in which Elder Patrick Kearon described a policeman who seemed to enjoy turning cars away from a roadblock and then said that no one has built a roadblock to turn you away from God. Hosea's God does build one, the connector notes, but look where. The thorns go across the road away from Him, never across the road home.

Wrestle: the husband who threatens to strip his wife (2:3, 10)

The same speech threatens: "Lest I strip her naked, and set her as in the day that she was born, and make her as a wilderness, and set her like a dry land, and slay her with thirst" (2:3). "And now will I discover her lewdness in the sight of her lovers, and none shall deliver her out of mine hand" (2:10). The children will receive no mercy (2:4). Every essay but the six-outlooks essay, which does not treat Hosea, faces these verses.

The critique. The critique deserves its full strength. Renita J. Weems's Battered Love (1995) put this problem at the center of the field. She argued that the metaphor persuades by drawing on its audience's assumptions about a husband's right to punish, so that divine love and male violence become entangled. Others went further: Athalya Brenner and Fokkelien van Dijk-Hemmes went further with what they called "pornoprophetic" readings. Even the political reading does not escape: Yee's reading still places the shame on a woman's body. And the problem is not only on paper. A reader who has survived an abusive marriage hears "I will allure her" (2:14) right after "I will strip her" (2:3) and recognizes the cycle. The LDS scholar agrees and notices the same sequence: The critique is serious. Hosea 2:14 begins with "Therefore." The wooing comes after the punishment, in the same speech.

Where the imagery comes from. Scholars have looked for the source of the stripping in ancient treaty curses. Delbert Hillers pointed to an Aramaic treaty from Sefire; Ryan C. Hanley notes that the key words there are reconstructed, and he argues that the curse lists of Deuteronomy 28 are the closer background. Either way, the speech of 2:2–13 is a covenant curse turned into a marriage quarrel.

The responses. There are serious responses. Andrew Sloane, in an evangelical reply in Tyndale Bulletin (2008) that focuses on Ezekiel but addresses the same critique of Hosea 1–3, acknowledges "the offensive power of the texts" but argues that their shocking features are better explained by the real violence of the judgment being announced, which for Hosea meant Assyrian siege and deportation, and by how serious the covenant breach was, than by misogyny. The LDS scholar's first point is about direction. First, the metaphor runs in one direction. God is always the faithful husband and the nation always the guilty wife, because the image was built to indict a people, not to describe a marriage. So a Latter-day Saint, for whom eternal marriage stands at the center of the plan of salvation, should be careful not to reverse the analogy and draw from Hosea 2 a model for how husbands treat wives. The doctrinal connector says it more bluntly. Applied to human husbands, it would be abuse. Doctrine and Covenants 121:41 rules out any power kept "by virtue of the priesthood" except by "persuasion," "long-suffering," "gentleness and meekness," and "love unfeigned." Hosea's metaphor teaches how God responds to covenant betrayal; it is not a model for how a husband treats a wife.

The practical disciple turns that into a line nobody in a family should miss. Nothing here gives a spouse permission to control money, movement, or a partner's body as punishment. If someone in your home is being hedged in or threatened with exposure, that is not a Hosea story; it is abuse, and it calls for help, not patience.

What remains. The honest questioner notes that Latter-day Saints feel this more sharply than most, because President Russell M. Nelson has taught that "a celestial marriage is such a covenant relationship." That deepens the analogy and also raises the stakes: if marriage models how God loves, what do we do with a model husband who threatens public humiliation? One answer is that Hosea pushes against his own era's marriage norms; another is that modern teaching has moved far past them. Neither answer makes 2:3 and 2:10 comfortable, and nothing requires that they be. The rabbi admits his tradition never really asked the question: None of these sources asks what chapter 2 sounds like from the woman's side. What the tradition did instead was choose: it built its daily practice on 2:19–20 and its fall season on chapter 14, and it read the Ishi and Baali verse in a way that pushes the metaphor away from domination. And the academic scholar refuses to tidy it up: The text does not resolve the tension between threat and tenderness, and an honest reading should not resolve it either.

"I will allure her" (2:14–15)

"Therefore, behold, I will allure her, and bring her into the wilderness, and speak comfortably unto her" (2:14). The Hebrew behind "speak comfortably" is to speak "to her heart." The wilderness is where Israel's marriage began, after Egypt, and God takes her back there to start again. Punishment does not win her back; tenderness does. The doctrinal connector agrees: The logic of judgment is overruled by the logic of courtship.

Then "the valley of Achor for a door of hope" (2:15). Achor, "trouble," is where Achan was stoned after Jericho (Joshua 7). That story ends with "the LORD turned from the fierceness of his anger" (Joshua 7:26), the phrase Hosea's God uses in 11:9. The place named for Israel's worst trouble becomes the entrance to hope.

"Thou shalt call me Ishi" (2:16–17)

"And it shall be at that day, saith the LORD, that thou shalt call me Ishi; and shalt call me no more Baali" (2:16). The polemic also works through a pun. Baʻal is the storm god's name and also the ordinary word for a husband as owner or master; ʼish simply means "man" or "husband." So the line does two things at once: it takes Baal's name out of Israel's mouth, and it changes what kind of husband God will be. The renewed covenant is described as giving up the language of mastery. The renewed covenant trades ownership language for partnership language.

The rabbi brings two Jewish readings. Rashi explains: "You shall worship Me out of love and not out of fear." And the Talmud (Ketubot 71b) gives Rabbi Yohanan's image: she will be like a bride already living in her husband's household, at ease enough to call him her partner, and not like a betrothed girl still in her father's house who calls her groom "my master." The honest questioner counts this verse among the answers to the stripping problem: Others point out that the text works against its own metaphor.

The practical disciple brings it home: The Lord wants to be the husband of companionship, not the husband who owns. In my home, am I more ʼishi or more baʻali? When hurt, do I reach for leverage, or do I speak to the heart?

"I will betroth thee unto me for ever" (2:18–23)

"And I will betroth thee unto me for ever; yea, I will betroth thee unto me in righteousness, and in judgment, and in lovingkindness, and in mercies. I will even betroth thee unto me in faithfulness: and thou shalt know the LORD" (2:19–20).

These verses are worn on the body. Every weekday morning, a man winding his tefillin strap around his middle finger says Hosea 2:19–20 (Hebrew 2:21–22). The rabbi points to the verb. It is "betroth," the first stage of a Jewish marriage, not "take back." Hosea does not say God will take back a divorced wife. He says God will start the marriage over, as though for the first time. Rashi notices that the five gifts run in two directions: righteousness and justice are what Israel practices, and lovingkindness and mercy are what God gives in return. The covenant is reciprocal even when it is given freely. And the last clause answers a complaint that will open chapter 4: The last clause, "and thou shalt know the LORD," answers the book's diagnosis that there is no "knowledge of God in the land" (4:1), and the Hebrew daʻat can carry the knowledge of intimacy (Genesis 4:1). The doctrinal connector agrees that knowledge is the goal: Hosea's betrothal vows name the covenant's terms, and its outcome is knowledge: "thou shalt know the LORD" (2:20). Jesus defined eternal life the same way, as knowing God (John 17:3).

The word behind "lovingkindness" is hesed, which will run through the whole book. President Russell M. Nelson's article "The Everlasting Covenant" (Liahona, October 2022) is the fullest modern prophetic commentary on that word. He defines it as a covenant bond in which both parties are bound to be loyal, and he makes Hosea's claim in modern words: "God will not abandon His relationship with those who have forged such a bond with Him," and "should they stray, He will help them find their way back to Him."

The practical disciple treats the five gifts as a checklist for a marriage: righteousness, judgment, lovingkindness, mercies, faithfulness. Pick the one you have been weakest in this year and do one thing about it before Sunday.

The chapter ends by undoing every name of chapter 1: "I will sow her unto me in the earth; and I will have mercy upon her that had not obtained mercy; and I will say to them which were not my people, Thou art my people; and they shall say, Thou art my God" (2:23).

Bought back (3:1–5)

"Then said the LORD unto me, Go yet, love a woman beloved of her friend, yet an adulteress, according to the love of the LORD toward the children of Israel, who look to other gods, and love flagons of wine. So I bought her to me for fifteen pieces of silver, and for an homer of barley, and an half homer of barley" (3:1–2). The chapter is in the first person and does not name the woman, so whether she is Gomer cannot be settled. The price is oddly specific, which is one reason many scholars think some real event lies behind the symbol.

Redemption, buying back, is literal here before it becomes doctrinal. The practical disciple notices the verb. Hosea 3:1 does not say "tolerate" her. It says love her. Most of us, the disciple says, have someone we would quietly trade away if we could: a relapsing sibling, a child who left the Church and is sharp about it, a ward member we stopped visiting. Whom have I mentally traded away? What would it cost me, in time, in pride, in convenience, to go buy them back?

The chapter ends with a long wait, "many days without a king, and without a prince, and without a sacrifice," and then a return: "Afterward shall the children of Israel return, and seek the LORD their God, and David their king; and shall fear the LORD and his goodness in the latter days" (3:4–5). That verse is one of the lines scholars credit to the Judean editors, and it points ahead to the "return" of chapter 14.

Think deeply.

  1. The Talmud's story says Hosea's marriage was God teaching the prophet to love the people he wanted to trade away. Who is the person, group, or family member you would most like to "exchange for another"? What would pleading for them, rather than about them, sound like this week?
  2. The panel offers at least four readings of 1:2 (real marriage, vision, hindsight, allegory). Which do you lean toward, and why? Does your answer change what the book teaches you, or only how comfortable you feel reading it?
  3. Hosea condemns the bloodshed that an earlier prophet's circle set in motion and the book of Kings praised. How do you hold continuing revelation when it reassesses something earlier leaders approved? Where have you seen that happen in your own life or tradition?
  4. Israel credited Baal with the grain God gave. What, concretely, do you credit for what your household has: an employer, a market, your own hustle, a government program? What would giving the credit back look like in your prayers and your giving?
  5. The thorns in 2:6 block the road away from God, never the road home. Can you see any thorns in your own past that, looking back, kept you from overtaking something you were chasing?
  6. Ishi or Baali: in your closest relationship, where do you use leverage (silence, money, withdrawal, telling others) instead of speaking to the heart? Which of the five betrothal gifts in 2:19–20 is weakest in you right now?

25–30 minSession 2: A Controversy with the Land

Reading assignment: Hosea 4, 5, 6

Eighth-century Israel: from boom to collapse

Chapters 4–6 shift from family to courtroom and from symbol to street. It helps to know the history. Hosea seems to begin in the last good years of Jeroboam II, who died about 750 BC, and to live into the chaos that followed. Second Kings 15 records four kings assassinated within a generation. Assyrian royal inscriptions confirm the outline. Tiglath-pileser III lists "Menahem of Samaria" among his tributaries and boasts that he put Hoshea on Israel's throne after Pekah was killed.

You will meet that tribute diplomacy in 5:13: "then went Ephraim to the Assyrian, and sent to king Jareb: yet could he not heal you." No Assyrian king named Jareb is known, and many scholars divide the Hebrew consonants differently to get malki rab, "the great king," a standard Assyrian royal title. And the alarms of 5:8, "Blow ye the cornet in Gibeah, and the trumpet in Ramah," sound on the border between Israel and Judah. Hosea 5:8–6:6, with its alarm trumpets at Gibeah and Ramah on the Benjaminite border, has long been read against the Syro-Ephraimite war of about 734–732 BCE, when Israel and Damascus attacked Judah (2 Kings 16:5–9; Isaiah 7).

"The LORD hath a controversy" (4:1–3)

"Hear the word of the LORD, ye children of Israel: for the LORD hath a controversy with the inhabitants of the land, because there is no truth, nor mercy, nor knowledge of God in the land" (4:1). The word "controversy" is a legal word; God is bringing a lawsuit. The words of Hosea 4:1 are the book's thesis: "there is no truth, nor mercy, nor knowledge of God in the land."

Two Hebrew words in that verse carry the whole book. The first is hesed, here translated "mercy." The LDS scholar tracks it through the betrothal ("lovingkindness," 2:19), the complaint about Israel's "goodness" (6:4), and "I desired mercy" (6:6). The KJV renders one Hebrew word three different ways in these verses, which shows how hard it is to translate. The academic scholar adds that the KJV's "mercy," "goodness," and "lovingkindness" all miss its sense of obligation. President Nelson defined hesed as the special love God extends to those who have made covenants with him, and, the LDS scholar observes, Nelson did not cite Hosea, but no book of the Old Testament illustrates his definition better. In Hosea, hesed is covenant loyalty that keeps going after the other party has broken faith.

The second word is "knowledge." For the academic scholar, Daʻat ʼelohim, "knowledge of God" (4:1, 6; 6:6), is not doctrinal information but acknowledgment, the way a vassal "knows" an overlord by keeping faith with him. The doctrinal connector draws the same conclusion from 4:6, "My people are destroyed for lack of knowledge": In Hosea the opposite of covenant is not mainly disobedience but ignorance of a person: "My people are destroyed for lack of knowledge" (4:6).

Verse 2 shows what fills the empty space: "By swearing, and lying, and killing, and stealing, and committing adultery, they break out, and blood toucheth blood." The practical disciple reads 4:1–2 as cause and effect. Honesty and mercy are not add-ons to worship; they are what worship was supposed to produce. And verse 3 says the land itself mourns: beasts, birds, and even fish suffer for what people do.

"Like people, like priest" (4:4–19)

Hosea turns on the priests, who were supposed to teach knowledge and instead "eat up the sin of my people" (4:8). "And there shall be, like people, like priest" (4:9). Then comes the worship he condemns: "My people ask counsel at their stocks, and their staff declareth unto them" (4:12), sacrifice "upon the tops of the mountains... under oaks and poplars and elms, because the shadow thereof is good" (4:13). The shrine names are real places. He calls the nation "Ephraim," he knows its shrines (Gilgal, Mizpah, Tabor, and Bethel, which he mocks as Beth-aven, "house of iniquity," 4:15; 10:5), and he watches its kings fall.

Wrestle: what did Israelites actually believe?

The book paints a clean choice between the LORD and Baal. Archaeology paints something messier. Archaeology shows how mixed northern religion actually was. At Kuntillet ʻAjrud, a way station in the Sinai from the early eighth century, Hosea's own century, storage jars carry blessings "by YHWH of Samaria and his asherah" and "YHWH of Teman and his asherah." What is an asherah? The final report, edited by Zeʼev Meshel (2012), argues that "asherah" here means a cultic object such as a sacred tree or pole; other scholars read it as the goddess Asherah, YHWH's consort. The two scholars agree on the main point. Either way, the jar records the kind of popular Yahwism Hosea attacked: the LORD worshipped "of Samaria," at local sanctuaries, alongside a feminine cult symbol. Either way, people in Hosea's world combined devotion to YHWH with practices he condemned, and they invoked the name of his own capital to do it.

The LDS scholar adds a longer history: Mark S. Smith's The Early History of God (2nd ed., Eerdmans, 2002) traces how Baal, once an accepted presence in Israelite religion, became Yahweh's rival during the monarchy. Readers react to that differently. That unsettles some readers, since monotheism then looks like a cause the prophets fought for rather than a settled national belief. Others find it clarifying, because it shows why "thou shalt know no god but me" (13:4) needed saying.

There is a Latter-day Saint wrinkle. Daniel C. Peterson's "Nephi and His Asherah" (Journal of Book of Mormon Studies, 2000) reads Nephi's tree-and-virgin vision (1 Nephi 11) against Asherah's links to sacred trees and motherhood, and the article is often brought up in discussions of a Mother in Heaven. That work is interesting, but a reader of Hosea should see its limits. Hosea treats the asherah cult as unfaithfulness, not a lost truth: "Ephraim is joined to idols: let him alone" (4:17). He presents it as infidelity: "My people ask counsel at their stocks" (4:12), "Ephraim is joined to idols" (4:17). Whatever Latter-day Saints believe about a divine Mother rests on modern revelation and teaching, not on rehabilitating the cult that Hosea, the prophet closest to it in time and place, condemned.

Wrestle: when did Hosea stop prophesying? (5:1–15)

Chapter 5 indicts priests, people, and "house of the king" alike, and then sounds the alarm at Gibeah and Ramah. Here a small point touches Latter-day Saint study aids. The Bible Dictionary says Hosea "probably died before the accession of Pekah, 736 B.C., for he makes no allusion to the Syro-Ephraimitic war." But since Albrecht Alt's 1919 essay, many have read 5:8–6:6 (alarms at Gibeah and Ramah, Ephraim and Judah fighting over the border) as reflecting exactly that war of 734–732 BC; Davies calls Alt's reconstruction "plausible" while noting dissent. The Bible Dictionary is a study aid, and here it takes one side of an open question. The academic scholar, as you saw above, reads 5:8–6:6 against that war too.

Notice where chapter 5 ends. God becomes a moth, rottenness, and a lion (5:12–14), and then withdraws: "I will go and return to my place, till they acknowledge their offence, and seek my face: in their affliction they will seek me early" (5:15). The people's response begins the next chapter.

"Come, and let us return" (6:1–3)

"Come, and let us return unto the LORD: for he hath torn, and he will heal us; he hath smitten, and he will bind us up. After two days will he revive us: in the third day he will raise us up, and we shall live in his sight" (6:1–2). It sounds like repentance. But read on to verse 4, and God sighs: "O Ephraim, what shall I do unto thee?" The words came easily; the loyalty did not last. Most of us know the resolve after a powerful sacrament meeting that is gone by Tuesday.

Wrestle: a third-day resurrection? (6:2)

Christians have long heard Easter in "in the third day he will raise us up." Three essays push back on reading it as a prediction. The LDS scholar: "In the third day he will raise us up" (6:2) is in context the people's too-easy confidence, which God immediately answers with "your goodness is as a morning cloud" (6:4). The honest questioner: the verse is the people's own confession, and God's reply in 6:4 ("your goodness is as a morning cloud") treats it as shallow. The academic scholar adds that an older theory, which explained the verse as borrowed from a "dying and rising" fertility god, has collapsed; most commentators now take "two days... third day" as an idiom for "soon," and the "raising" as recovery from the wounds of 5:13. The reading of the verse as a reference to resurrection is a later Christian one.

The rabbi complicates that last claim. Christian readers hear the resurrection, and that hearing is not foreign to Judaism. The Aramaic Targum renders the verse "on the day of the resurrection of the dead He will raise us up." That is the resurrection of the nation and of all the dead, at the end. Rashi reads it historically: the "two days" are the two Temples destroyed, and on the third day God will set us up with the third Temple. None of these is one individual's third day. So the rabbi and the academic scholar disagree on how "late" a resurrection reading is, but agree that the verse is about a people, not one person.

"Your goodness is as a morning cloud" (6:4–6)

"O Ephraim, what shall I do unto thee? O Judah, what shall I do unto thee? for your goodness is as a morning cloud, and as the early dew it goeth away" (6:4). "Goodness" is hesed again. Hosea's charge is that Israel's side of that loyalty evaporates. Hosea also sets two kinds of moisture against each other. Here, human loyalty is dew that burns off by mid-morning. In chapter 14 you will see the other kind.

Then the book's most quoted line: "For I desired mercy, and not sacrifice; and the knowledge of God more than burnt offerings" (6:6). [The famous line "I desired mercy [hesed], and not sacrifice" (6:6) is not a rejection of ritual in principle.](personas/academic-scholar.html#not-rejection) Its second half, "more than burnt offerings," shows that the idiom means "rather than."

Jesus quoted the verse twice. Jesus used Hosea 6:6 twice, both times against people whose observance had become an excuse for unkindness: when Pharisees objected to his eating with publicans and sinners ("go ye and learn what that meaneth," Matthew 9:13), and when they condemned his hungry disciples for plucking grain on the Sabbath (Matthew 12:7). Apply that to ministering. Am I keeping the forms, the reported contact, while avoiding the person who actually needs mercy?

For Jews the verse did something even larger. For Jews it is the founding text of life after the Temple. When Rabbi Joshua wept over the ruined Temple, Rabban Yohanan ben Zakkai told him they had another atonement just as effective, acts of lovingkindness, "as it is stated, For I desire lovingkindness and not sacrifice." The lesson summarizes 6:6 as "Devotion to God must be felt inwardly, not just expressed outwardly." A Jewish reader would push back a little. In Hosea, hesed is not a feeling. Rabban Yohanan did not replace the altar with a mood. He replaced it with deeds. Hold that against the lesson's phrasing. The two are not far apart, but the rabbi's version asks what you did, not only what you felt.

Wrestle: "like men," "like Adam," or "at Adam"? (6:7–11)

"But they like men have transgressed the covenant: there have they dealt treacherously against me" (6:7). Hosea 6:7 shows how text criticism and Restoration theology can meet. The Hebrew keʼadam can mean "like men," "like Adam," or, with a one-letter change, "at Adam," a town on the Jordan (Joshua 3:16). The next verse names Gilead, a nearby region. [In 6:7, "they like men [keʼadam] have transgressed the covenant" can mean "like Adam," but the next words, "there have they dealt treacherously," point to a place.](personas/academic-scholar.html#point-to-place)

A Latter-day Saint will be drawn to "like Adam." Restoration scripture develops an Adamic covenant more fully than the Bible does (Moses 5–6). The doctrinal connector sees a striking pairing: If "like Adam" is right, chapter 6 holds both ends of the plan of salvation within a few verses: the Fall in 6:7, and in 6:2 the "third day" hope Christians have long heard as pointing to the Resurrection. The LDS scholar adds the brake: Scholarly honesty requires saying that the geographic reading has strong support, and that this verse cannot carry much doctrinal weight alone. Note the "if" in the connector's sentence; the two Latter-day Saint essays are closer than they look.

Think deeply.

  1. Hosea says the land has "no truth, nor mercy, nor knowledge of God." If a prophet brought a lawsuit against your neighborhood, workplace, or online community, which of the three would be missing first? Which is weakest in you?
  2. The academic scholar says knowing God in Hosea is less like knowing facts and more like a vassal keeping faith with his lord. How would your prayers change this week if "knowing God" meant loyalty rather than information?
  3. The people of Kuntillet ʻAjrud probably thought of themselves as loyal worshippers of the LORD. What practices in your life might a prophet see as divided loyalty while you see them as harmless additions?
  4. Compare 6:1–3 with 6:4. When has your own repentance been fluent and confident and gone by Tuesday? What made the difference the times it lasted?
  5. The lesson says devotion must be "felt inwardly"; the rabbi says hesed is a deed, not a feeling. Which emphasis do you need more right now? Name one act of hesed that would cost you something this week.
  6. "Like men," "like Adam," or "at Adam": how much doctrinal weight are you comfortable resting on a verse whose translation is uncertain? Where else have you built on a single verse?

25–30 minSession 3: Cords of a Man, Bands of Love

Reading assignment: Hosea 10, 11, 12, 13

An empty vine and fallow ground (10:1–15)

The reading skips chapters 7–9 and resumes with Israel as a vine that grew rich and spent its riches on altars: "according to the multitude of his fruit he hath increased the altars" (10:1). "Their heart is divided" (10:2). The kings are going: Hosea's lines "her king is cut off as the foam upon the water" (10:7) and "I gave thee a king in mine anger, and took him away in my wrath" (13:11) fit this run of coups. The golden calf of Bethel will be "carried unto Assyria for a present to king Jareb" (10:6).

In the middle of the chapter is a farmer's invitation: "Sow to yourselves in righteousness, reap in mercy; break up your fallow ground: for it is time to seek the LORD, till he come and rain righteousness upon you" (10:12). The lesson builds a children's activity on this verse. The practical disciple reads it for tired adults. Fallow ground is not ruined ground; it has been left alone until it crusted over. Breaking it up is the work of a season, not a miracle.

"Out of Egypt have I called my son" (11:1–7)

"When Israel was a child, then I loved him, and called my son out of Egypt. As they called them, so they went from them: they sacrificed unto Baalim, and burned incense to graven images. I taught Ephraim also to go, taking them by their arms; but they knew not that I healed them. I drew them with cords of a man, with bands of love" (11:1–4). God is no longer a husband here but a parent. That is a parent teaching a toddler to walk, who falls constantly while the parent keeps reaching.

Then 11:5: "the Assyrian shall be his king, because they refused to return." The verb is shuv again. It can mean repentance (6:1; 14:1), refusal (11:5, "they refused to return"), or a return to Egypt as captivity. The whole book turns on that one verb.

Wrestle: Matthew's "out of Egypt" (11:1)

Matthew 2:15 applies "Out of Egypt have I called my son" to the infant Jesus. But Hosea's next verse is about idolatry. The verse's own context, "they sacrificed unto Baalim" (11:2), shows Matthew was reading typologically rather than predictively. The honest questioner gives both sides. Critics call this proof-texting. Defenders call it typology, Israel's story relived by Israel's Messiah, which is a real method but not the straightforward prediction a casual reader of Matthew assumes. As the honest questioner notes, Matthew 2:15 applies it to the child Jesus, and the Latter-day Saint chapter heading reads it "in similitude of our Lord." That is the language of type, not prediction. None of this shows the apostles were wrong. It does show that "Hosea predicted this" and "a New Testament writer found Christ here" are different claims.

"How shall I give thee up?" (11:8–12)

"How shall I give thee up, Ephraim? how shall I deliver thee, Israel? how shall I make thee as Admah? how shall I set thee as Zeboim? mine heart is turned within me, my repentings are kindled together. I will not execute the fierceness of mine anger, I will not return to destroy Ephraim: for I am God, and not man; the Holy One in the midst of thee: and I will not enter into the city" (11:8–9).

Most of the essays come to rest here. Admah and Zeboim were destroyed with Sodom (Deuteronomy 29:23). God is weighing whether to do to Ephraim what was done to the cities of the plain, and cannot. The resolution, "for I am God, and not man" (11:9), sets divine holiness against human vengefulness. The honest questioner reads the same line as an answer to chapter 2's angry husband: And in 11:9 God refuses to act like an injured man: "I will not execute the fierceness of mine anger... for I am God, and not man."

A God who feels. The doctrinal connector sets these verses beside Enoch's vision of a weeping God (Moses 7:28–33) and the Lord of the vineyard who wept, "What could I have done more for my vineyard?" (Jacob 5:41). In both, judgment is spoken aloud, then deferred by love. The rabbi brings in Abraham Joshua Heschel. Heschel argues that the prophets knew God not as an unmoved mover but through divine pathos: God is moved by and involved in what human beings do. Not every Jewish thinker agreed. Eliezer Berkovits, in "Dr. A. J. Heschel's Theology of Pathos" (Tradition, 1964), objected that this attributes human passions to God, against the Maimonidean philosophical tradition; the argument still runs in Tradition's pages. Latter-day Saint readers, who do not share Maimonides' discomfort with a God who feels, may find Heschel congenial. But notice that Hosea's own verse keeps both sides. God's heart turns over, and the reason given for mercy is that he is not a man.

The Joseph Smith Translation. Here the Joseph Smith Translation is precise. The footnote to 11:8 gives the JST ending as "toward thee, and my mercies are extended to gather thee." God's heart is no longer turned "within" Him but "toward" Ephraim, and the "repentings" become "mercies ... extended to gather." The JST does not cool Hosea's God down; it makes the passion steady, always aimed at gathering. That sits interestingly beside the Heschel and Berkovits debate. The JST keeps the feeling Berkovits worried about but removes the hint of a God who regrets.

Jewish readings of the last clause. Rashi takes "God, and not man" as constancy: "God Who keeps His favorable word, and I do not retract the good." The obscure "I will not enter into the city" the Talmud (Taʻanit 5a) reads as God's vow not to enter the heavenly Jerusalem until he enters the earthly one. God refuses to be at home in heaven while His people are in exile.

The practical disciple's advice is simple: If you suspect God is done with you, read 11:8 slowly: "How shall I give thee up, Ephraim?... mine heart is turned within me."

Jacob, and a merchant with crooked scales (12:1–14)

Chapter 12 turns the nation's ancestor into an indictment. "He took his brother by the heel in the womb, and by his strength he had power with God: Yea, he had power over the angel, and prevailed: he wept, and made supplication unto him" (12:3–4). Chapter 12, by contrast, turns Jacob into an accusation (12:3–4, 12), in a version that differs from Genesis: in 12:4 Jacob weeps and pleads with the angel, a detail Genesis 32 lacks. In the middle sits the call: "Therefore turn thou to thy God: keep mercy and judgment, and wait on thy God continually" (12:6).

Then the businessman: "He is a merchant, the balances of deceit are in his hand: he loveth to oppress. And Ephraim said, Yet I am become rich, I have found me out substance: in all my labours they shall find none iniquity in me that were sin" (12:7–8). That is the voice of someone whose books are technically clean and whose conscience has been outsourced to the books. Is there a place where my scales are slightly weighted: an expense report, a billable hour, a product description, a reference letter?

"There is no saviour beside me" (13:1–13)

Chapter 13 opens with calf-kissing (13:2) and a return to the morning cloud and early dew of 6:4, now applied to the people themselves: "they shall be as the morning cloud, and as the early dew that passeth away" (13:3). Then God's self-identification: "Yet I am the LORD thy God from the land of Egypt, and thou shalt know no god but me: for there is no saviour beside me" (13:4). The Church footnotes this to 2 Nephi 25:20, "there is none other name given under heaven save it be this Jesus Christ." Latter-day Saints read the LORD of Hosea as the premortal Jehovah, so the verse becomes a statement about Christ. The lesson's children's section builds on the same verse.

The next verses diagnose prosperity. Hosea's diagnosis of prosperity is blunt: "they were filled, and their heart was exalted; therefore have they forgotten me" (13:6).

Wrestle: violence in the voice of God (10:14; 13:7–8, 16)

Then the animals. "Therefore I will be unto them as a lion: as a leopard by the way will I observe them: I will meet them as a bear that is bereaved of her whelps, and will rend the caul of their heart" (13:7–8). And the chapter ends: "Samaria shall become desolate; for she hath rebelled against her God: they shall fall by the sword: their infants shall be dashed in pieces, and their women with child shall be ripped up" (13:16). Chapter 10 had already said "the mother was dashed in pieces upon her children" (10:14).

This was real Iron Age warfare; 2 Kings 15:16 credits Israel's own King Menahem with the same atrocity. The honest questioner lays out the answers. Some answer that 13:16 describes what conquest brings rather than what God approves; the verbs are passive. Some say the prophet read catastrophe as judgment because that was how ancient Israel interpreted history. Others find both answers too easy, since in 13:7–8 God himself, speaking in the first person, is the predator. What no one can deny is the book's arrangement: 13:16 is followed immediately by "O Israel, return unto the LORD thy God" (14:1). Hosea sets the horror and the invitation side by side and does not reconcile them.

The practical disciple asks for honest reading. What you can do is refuse to read selectively. Choose with care what you teach children, but as an adult do not let chapter 14's loveliness erase the fact that Hosea spoke to people about to lose everything. Even 13:9 holds both together: "O Israel, thou hast destroyed thyself; but in me is thine help."

The rabbi notes how Jewish practice handled the same chapters. The stripping and the lion and the bear of chapters 2 and 13 are in the scroll, but the liturgy did not choose them. That choice is itself a way of reading the book. Is selecting the tender verses for worship the same thing as reading selectively? The rabbi and the practical disciple might answer differently.

Wrestle: "O death, I will be thy plagues" (13:14)

"I will ransom them from the power of the grave; I will redeem them from death: O death, I will be thy plagues; O grave, I will be thy destruction: repentance shall be hid from mine eyes" (13:14). The lesson's children's section teaches this verse as a statement about Jesus Christ, and Paul built a resurrection hymn on it: "O death, where is thy sting? O grave, where is thy victory?" (1 Corinthians 15:55). Hosea 13:14 is the sharpest case.

The threat reading. Hebrew has no punctuation, and the first lines can be read as questions: "Shall I ransom them from the power of Sheol? Shall I redeem them from death?" The verse is surrounded by the bear, the east wind, and Samaria's fall, and it ends with "repentance shall be hid from mine eyes." Read that way, it summons Death to do its work. Rashi reads it as a threat. In his paraphrase, God is the one who would have ransomed them, and now speaks words of death against them instead. Given the bereaved bear of 13:8 and the east wind of 13:15, his reading is hard to dismiss.

Paul's reading. Paul's use of the verse in 1 Corinthians 15:55 follows a Greek text and turns it in the opposite direction. Davies, the honest questioner reports, thinks Paul's use carries a different sense from the original, though the Septuagint had probably already read the verse positively. So the positive reading is older than Paul.

Holding both. The LDS scholar: Latter-day Saints already accept that inspired reuse can carry a text past its original meaning. Seeing both meanings is more honest than letting Paul's use hide Hosea's. The doctrinal connector sets the verse beside Abinadi's "the grave hath no victory, and the sting of death is swallowed up in Christ" (Mosiah 16:8) and Jacob's "that monster, death and hell" (2 Nephi 9:10). The Restoration reading need not deny the verse's bleak setting; it holds that the God who once called death against a rebellious kingdom has, in Christ, become death's plague. The rabbi offers a similar permission with a condition: Christians are free to read the verse through Easter. They should know that in Hosea's own sequence the promise of chapter 14 has not yet come.

Think deeply.

  1. "Fallow ground is not ruined ground." What part of your spiritual life has crusted over from being left alone rather than from being destroyed? What would one season of breaking it up look like?
  2. God says, "I taught Ephraim also to go, taking them by their arms; but they knew not that I healed them" (11:3). When have you been held up without knowing it? Who taught you to walk, spiritually, and do they know?
  3. In 11:8–9 God argues with himself and chooses mercy "for I am God, and not man." What does it mean to you that God's holiness is the reason for his mercy rather than the limit on it? How does the JST's "toward thee" change the picture?
  4. Hosea 12 describes a merchant whose books are clean and whose scales are crooked. Where in your work is there a gap between what is technically allowed and what is honest?
  5. Hosea puts 13:16 right before 14:1. How do you read the most violent verses in scripture you love? What do you gain, and what do you risk, by reading them rather than skipping them?
  6. Paul read 13:14 as a promise; Rashi read it as a threat. Can both be scripture's meaning? Where else has an inspired later reader carried a verse somewhere its first author may not have meant?

25–30 minSession 4: Take With You Words

Reading assignment: Hosea 14 · Joel 1

The Sabbath of Return (14:1)

Hosea 14 is the haftarah, the prophetic reading, for the Sabbath between the Jewish New Year and Yom Kippur. That Sabbath takes its name, Shabbat Shuvah, from the haftarah's first word, shuvah, "return." So the chapter that ends Hosea has been read by Jews for centuries in the season of repentance.

"O Israel, return unto the LORD thy God; for thou hast fallen by thine iniquity" (14:1). The Hebrew preposition is ʻad, literally "as far as." Return all the way. In the Talmud (Yoma 86a), Rabbi Levi draws the lesson: "Great is repentance, as it reaches the heavenly throne." Another sage notices the second half of the verse. The word for iniquity means deliberate sin, but the verb is "you stumbled." Iniquity, ʻavon, is deliberate sin, yet the prophet calls it stumbling: for one who returns, deliberate sins count as accidents, and, the Gemara adds, when one returns out of love, even as merits.

"Take with you words" (14:2–3)

"Take with you words, and turn to the LORD: say unto him, Take away all iniquity, and receive us graciously: so will we render the calves of our lips" (14:2). The returning person brings sentences instead of a bull. The Talmud adds a contrast: "Take with you words" (14:2): with a human being you have wronged, you cannot know whether words will be enough, but God is appeased by words. The phrase became part of Jewish prayer: In the morning service, a prayer in the sacrificial readings asks God to accept "the bulls of our lips" (14:2; Hebrew 14:3) in place of the daily offering. The doctrinal connector follows the same idea to the risen Christ, who ended burnt offerings and asked instead for "a broken heart and a contrite spirit" (3 Nephi 9:19–20). Hosea 14:2 anticipates it: "take with you words" and offer "the calves of our lips," prayer in place of bulls. The Church footnotes the verse to D&C 59:8, which asks for the same sacrifice.

The words are specific: "Asshur shall not save us; we will not ride upon horses: neither will we say any more to the work of our hands, Ye are our gods: for in thee the fatherless findeth mercy" (14:3). Israel names the exact alliances and idols it is giving up. Compare the easy return of chapter 6. That is the difference between 6:1–3 and 14:1–3: the first is general and confident, the second concrete and humble. Maimonides used this same verse as the test of real repentance. His definition of true repentance (Hilkhot Teshuvah 2:2) uses 14:3 (Hebrew 14:4), "neither will we say any more to the work of our hands, Ye are our gods," as the test: the one who knows hidden things can testify that this person will not go back.

The practical disciple suggests doing literally what the verse says. If you have drifted, write out the words. Name your Asshur, the thing you have been counting on instead of God. Then say it, in prayer, and to any person involved.

"I will love them freely" (14:4)

"I will heal their backsliding, I will love them freely: for mine anger is turned away from him" (14:4). This is the lesson's title verse. "Freely" is nedavah, the word for a freewill offering. The Gemara reads it as saying that repentance counts as more than an obligatory sacrifice; it counts as a voluntary gift. And a rabbi notices the singular "him" at the end of the verse: Because one person returns, the whole world is forgiven.

The doctrinal connector brings a caution from Elder D. Todd Christofferson's "The Love of God" (October 2021) about how far "freely" goes. He warns against stretching God's all-embracing love into a belief that God's "blessings are 'unconditional' and that salvation is 'unconditional.' They are not." He also says that "The love of the Father and the Son is freely given but also includes hopes and expectations." Hosea 14 holds that balance: the love is unearned and arrives before Israel has changed. Notice the order in the chapter itself: the people bring words in verses 2–3, but the healing in verse 4 is described as God's own free choice.

The practical disciple adds Elder Jeffrey R. Holland: Elder Jeffrey R. Holland put the same truth pastorally: "It is never too late so long as the Master of the vineyard says there is time" ("The Laborers in the Vineyard," April 2012).

Dew, lily, olive, Lebanon (14:5–8)

"I will be as the dew unto Israel: he shall grow as the lily, and cast forth his roots as Lebanon. His branches shall spread, and his beauty shall be as the olive tree, and his smell as Lebanon" (14:5–6). Here is the other kind of moisture. Israel's goodness was dew that vanished (6:4); but in chapter 14 God becomes the dew: "I will be as the dew unto Israel: he shall grow as the lily" (14:5). The footnote sends the reader to D&C 128:19, "As the dews of Carmel, so shall the knowledge of God descend upon them!" That answers Hosea 4:1 and 6:6 almost word for word: the missing "knowledge of God" arrives as dew.

The image is also a last blow against Baal, the rain god of chapter 2. Hosea insists that YHWH, not Baal, controls the rain: he "shall come unto us as the rain, as the latter and former rain" (6:3), and "I will be as the dew unto Israel" (14:5). Then Ephraim speaks: "What have I to do any more with idols?... I am like a green fir tree. From me is thy fruit found" (14:8).

The lesson asks what the plant images teach. The practical disciple's answer: Hosea's image of the result is not dramatic. Dew, lily, olive, roots: slow, rooted growth, not one emotional morning.

"Who is wise?" (14:9)

"Who is wise, and he shall understand these things? prudent, and he shall know them? for the ways of the LORD are right, and the just shall walk in them: but the transgressors shall fall therein" (14:9). The book itself admits it can trip a reader. The same roads, the same verses, can lift one reader and trip another.

The rabbi closes the circle back to chapter 1. Maimonides, describing what repentance does, quotes 1:10. Then he turns to Hosea 1:10 (Hebrew 2:1): the language that pushed the sinner away, "Ye are not my people," becomes the language that draws him near, "Ye are the sons of the living God." The verse that closes the Talmud's story of Hosea's marriage is the verse that opens Maimonides' description of return.

Wrestle: when was Joel written? (Joel 1:1)

Now a different prophet. "The word of the LORD that came to Joel the son of Pethuel" (Joel 1:1). That is all. Joel gives no king, no datable event, and nothing about the prophet beyond his father's name. The Bible Dictionary allows anything from "as early as the reign of Joash, before 850 B.C., or even so late as after the Return."

The case for a late date, after the Babylonian exile, rests on clues inside the book: no king, but elders and priests leading the people (1:13–14; 2:16–17); a working temple; Judeans sold "unto the Grecians" (3:6); and open quotations of other prophets. Joel is already reading a body of prophecy. Joel 2:32 even marks a quotation with "as the LORD hath said," citing Obadiah. No single argument is decisive, but most scholars now place Joel after the exile. Not everyone agrees on the details: Hans Walter Wolff dated the book to the first half of the fourth century BC, a date William H. Shea's 1979 review judged "too late," and James L. Crenshaw's Anchor Bible volume (1995) surveys the debate at length. And the six-outlooks essay notes that the evidence can be read both ways: The Bible Gateway Encyclopedia of the Bible entry on Joel lays out the competing arguments: a priestly, temple-centered community with no king in view, and no mention of the northern kingdom or Samaria, push many toward a date after the exile; others find that the same evidence fits earlier periods of Judah's history.

Joel also sits in a larger book. In the Hebrew canon the twelve minor prophets are one scroll, and Joel's "The LORD also shall roar out of Zion" (3:16) repeats the first line of Amos, the next book in Hebrew order. Nogalski's detailed reconstruction is disputed, but the Twelve were read as a unit in antiquity.

The locusts (Joel 1:2–20)

Joel 1 is a lament over a real agricultural catastrophe: "That which the palmerworm hath left hath the locust eaten" (1:4), the field wasted, "the new wine is dried up, the oil languisheth" (1:10). The four names in 1:4, palmerworm, locust, cankerworm, caterpiller, sound like four plagues. The four insect names in 1:4 are probably terms for locusts at different stages or simply different names for them, not four species. John A. Thompson's 1955 article "Joel's Locusts in the Light of Near Eastern Parallels" collected ancient comparisons and supported the view that a real plague lies behind chapter 1.

The damage reaches the temple: "The meat offering and the drink offering is cut off from the house of the LORD" (1:9). There is nothing left to sacrifice. So Joel calls the people to fast: "Sanctify ye a fast, call a solemn assembly, gather the elders and all the inhabitants of the land into the house of the LORD your God, and cry unto the LORD" (1:14). And for the first time he names "the day of the LORD" (1:15), a phrase that will grow through the book.

The practical disciple notices what kind of disaster this is. Joel begins with loss no one caused by a single sin: four waves of insects, each eating what the last left (1:4), then drought and fire (1:19–20). And notice Joel's first instruction: "Tell ye your children of it, and let your children tell their children, and their children another generation" (1:3). Joel's first instruction is not to explain the disaster but to pass it on honestly: "Tell ye your children of it, and let your children tell their children" (1:3). The chapter ends with even the animals praying: "The beasts of the field cry also unto thee" (1:20).

Think deeply.

  1. "Take with you words." If you wrote out the words of your own return this week, what would they say? Be specific: what is your Asshur, your horses, the "work of your hands" you have trusted?
  2. Reish Lakish read "thou hast fallen" as stumbling, and taught that for one who returns out of love even deliberate sins can become merits. Does that seem too generous to you? What would it mean for the sins you are most ashamed of?
  3. "Freely" is the word for a freewill offering. Elder Christofferson warns that God's love is free but not "unconditional." How do you hold those together? Where have you felt loved before you had changed?
  4. Hosea's picture of a restored life is dew and slow roots, not a dramatic morning. What slow growth in you would you have missed if you were only watching for the dramatic?
  5. Joel's first instruction after a disaster is "tell your children." What hard thing in your family's history have you not told the next generation? What would telling it honestly, without explaining it away, look like?
  6. Joel's locusts are a disaster no one caused by a single sin. How do you speak about suffering, your own or a neighbor's, without turning it into a verdict?

25–30 minSession 5: Upon All Flesh

Reading assignment: Joel 2, 3

The army and the day of the LORD (2:1–11)

"Blow ye the trumpet in Zion, and sound an alarm in my holy mountain... for the day of the LORD cometh" (2:1). The locusts return, now as an army. "The appearance of them is as the appearance of horses" (2:4); "they shall climb the wall like men of war" (2:7); "they shall enter in at the windows like a thief" (2:9). Its "army" that runs like horses, climbs walls, and enters windows (2:4–9) accurately describes a locust swarm, but it is also YHWH's "army" (2:11), and 2:20 calls it "the northern army." The lesson asks what similarities you see between our day and the one Joel foresaw. The academic scholar's answer is that Joel himself already moved from one to the other: Many interpreters think the book moves from a real plague to the "day of the LORD" (1:15; 2:1, 11, 31; 3:14), using the plague as a picture of final judgment. The section ends with a question: "the day of the LORD is great and very terrible; and who can abide it?" (2:11).

"Rend your heart, and not your garments" (2:12–17)

The answer to "who can abide it" comes at once. "Therefore also now, saith the LORD, turn ye even to me with all your heart, and with fasting, and with weeping, and with mourning: And rend your heart, and not your garments, and turn unto the LORD your God: for he is gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness, and repenteth him of the evil" (2:12–13).

That last line quotes the words God spoke to Moses about his own character in Exodus 34:6. Jews recite it as the Thirteen Attributes of Mercy, over and over, on Yom Kippur. But Joel changes it. Joel, like Jonah 4:2, adds a clause that is not in Exodus: "and repenteth him of the evil." Exodus says God does not clear the guilty. Joel and Jonah say God turns back from harm. The Jonah link runs deep: [The confession that follows, "gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness [hesed]," draws on Exodus 34:6, the same formula Jonah resents in Jonah 4:2.](personas/lds-scholar.html#jonah-resents) And Joel's next question, "Who knoweth if he will return and repent" (2:14), has a close parallel in Jonah 3:9.

The Joseph Smith Translation handles "repenteth" the same way it handled Hosea 11:8. The JST does the same thing to Joel 2:13–14. It reads "and he will turn away the evil from you" and moves the repentance onto the people: "Therefore repent, and who knoweth but he will return and leave a blessing behind him." Both revisions keep the divine emotion and remove the implication that God changes His mind like a person who has erred, the line Hosea himself draws in 11:9: "for I am God, and not man." Notice the contrast with the rabbi: Jewish tradition treasures the added clause as the heart of Joel's hope, while the JST recasts it. Both readings protect the same thing, a God whose turning toward mercy is reliable.

What does it mean to rend the heart? The lesson explains that tearing clothes was an outward sign of grief, and asks how rending the heart differs. The rabbi answers from the Mishnah. On a fast day for drought, the elder of the town reminded the people that God saw Nineveh's deeds, not its sackcloth (Jonah 3:10), and then quoted Joel 2:13. In the Mishnah, rending the heart means changing what you do. Fasting and prayer without that are not enough. The practical disciple puts it in today's terms: the torn garment might be a public post, a visible fast, an announced resolution. None of those is wrong. The question is whether anything underneath has actually torn.

Then the gathering: "gather the children, and those that suck the breasts: let the bridegroom go forth of his chamber, and the bride out of her closet" (2:16). No one is exempt, not even newlyweds. When your family fasts or prays over a real problem, include the children by name, and include whoever seems least interested.

"I will restore to you the years" (2:18–27)

"Then will the LORD be jealous for his land, and pity his people" (2:18). The promises that follow are about food and rain: "corn, and wine, and oil" (2:19), "the former rain, and the latter rain" (2:23), floors "full of wheat" (2:24). And then: "I will restore to you the years that the locust hath eaten, the cankerworm, and the caterpiller, and the palmerworm, my great army which I sent among you" (2:25).

It does not say the years were not lost. For someone returning after a decade of drift, that is the honest hope: not that the decade did not happen, but that God can make it yield something.

Twice the passage says "my people shall never be ashamed" (2:26–27). The Ashkenazi haftarah of Shabbat Shuvah carries this passage into the week before Yom Kippur, ending at 2:27, "my people shall never be ashamed," just before the outpouring of the spirit. So for some Jewish communities, Hosea 14 and Joel 2 are read together in the same week of the year, just as this week's reading puts them together.

Wrestle: fulfilled at Pentecost, or not yet fulfilled? (2:28–32)

"And it shall come to pass afterward, that I will pour out my spirit upon all flesh; and your sons and your daughters shall prophesy, your old men shall dream dreams, your young men shall see visions: And also upon the servants and upon the handmaids in those days will I pour out my spirit" (2:28–29).

On the day of Pentecost, Peter stood up and said, "this is that which was spoken by the prophet Joel" (Acts 2:16). In September 1823, Moroni quoted Joel 2:28 to the end of the chapter to Joseph Smith "and said that this was not yet fulfilled, but was soon to be" (Joseph Smith—History 1:41). For Latter-day Saints, this is the week's sharpest tension.

The usual reconciliation is layered fulfillment: Pentecost was a real first installment, and the full outpouring, with the sun darkened and the moon turned to blood (2:31), still lies ahead. The LDS scholar holds that view: The two claims do not cancel each other. So does the doctrinal connector: Pentecost was a fulfillment; Moroni announced a larger one. The honest questioner accepts that the reconciliation fits how scripture often handles prophecy, but insists on noticing what it is: But readers construct this reconciliation; neither text states it, and Peter's "this is that" sounds complete on its own terms.

The doctrinal connector adds where the Restoration looked for the larger fulfillment. The Restoration tied that larger fulfillment to temples. At the Kirtland Temple dedication in 1836, Joseph Smith prayed, "Let it be fulfilled upon them, as upon those on the day of Pentecost" (D&C 109:36), and a week later the Lord called it "the beginning of the blessing which shall be poured out upon the heads of my people" (D&C 110:10).

The six-outlooks essay that follows gives you a way to hold these claims apart and together.

Capstone: "I will pour out my spirit upon all flesh," six ways (2:28–32)

A text that is fulfilled, and then fulfilled again, and then declared not yet fulfilled, is a text that forces a reader to ask what kind of reading is going on. Donald W. Parry, a professor of Hebrew Bible at Brigham Young University, offers a useful tool for that question. In a chapter called "Ways to Read Isaiah: Six Outlooks," Parry names six ways a reader can approach a prophetic passage: the near view of historical context, the distant view of prophecy, dual or multiple fulfillment, likening, personal reading, and reading for doctrine. He built the framework for Isaiah, but he himself reaches to Joel 2:28 for one example, by way of a talk by Elder Dallin H. Oaks. That makes Joel 2:28–32 an unusually fitting place to try the whole framework out. He does not rank the six outlooks or name one as the most important. He does suggest starting with the immediate context, so the walk-through begins there.

1. The near view. What did the promise mean for Joel's own people? Look where it begins: "And it shall come to pass afterward." The Hebrew ʼaharei-khen, "after this," marks a seam. In Hebrew Bibles these verses are a separate chapter (Joel 3:1–5). First the land is healed; then the people are filled. The academic scholar stresses how local the audience is: The promise "I will pour out my spirit upon all flesh" (2:28–32; Hebrew 3:1–5) is narrower in context than its later Christian use. "All flesh" is "your sons and your daughters," the community of Judah, set against the nations judged in the next chapter in the "valley of Jehoshaphat," a symbolic name meaning "YHWH judges" (3:2, 12). But within that community the reach is wide. What is startling in the near view is not the geography but the social reach: the Spirit falls on the old and the young, the sons and the daughters, and "also upon the servants and upon the handmaids" (2:29). Moses once wished "that all the LORD's people were prophets" (Numbers 11:29). Joel announces that wish granted.

Jewish commentators argue over the same question of reach. Radak limits it to those in Israel worthy of the holy spirit and notes that Joel does not say all will prophesy, only "your sons and your daughters." Rashi reads "all flesh" as anyone whose heart becomes soft as flesh, citing Ezekiel's "heart of flesh." On Rashi's reading the condition for receiving the spirit is not birth but a softened heart. And a midrash on Moses' seventy elders ends with Joel: A midrash on the seventy elders who received a share of Moses' spirit (Numbers 11) ends with Joel: "In this world, individuals prophesied. But in the World to Come, all of Israel will become prophets" (Bamidbar Rabbah 15:25).

2. The distant view. Verses 30–31 widen the frame from a people to the cosmos: "wonders in the heavens and in the earth, blood, and fire, and pillars of smoke. The sun shall be turned into darkness, and the moon into blood, before the great and the terrible day of the LORD come." Joel used darkened sun and moon for the locusts in 2:10, but here they are attached to a final day, and chapter 3 gathers "all nations" for judgment. The distant view does not erase the near one. It notices that the prophecy's horizon is larger than any single harvest.

3. Dual and multiple fulfillment. Here the passage is almost a textbook case, and here Parry touches Joel directly, through Elder Oaks's statement that many scriptures "have multiple meanings," illustrated by Peter and Moroni. Look at what Peter did with the words. Peter's use in Acts 2 is not a passive quotation. Joel says "afterward"; Peter says "in the last days" (Acts 2:17). The Hebrew text and the Greek Septuagint both read "after these things," so the change is Peter's (or Luke's) interpretive framing; it announces that the age Joel looked toward has begun. Peter stops partway through 2:32 and seems to pick up its last words at the end of his sermon: "the promise is unto you, and to your children, and to all that are afar off, even as many as the Lord our God shall call" (Acts 2:39). And Peter's "all that are afar off" pushes "all flesh" well past Zion's sons and daughters. Then Moroni's 1823 statement. Read alongside Acts 2, that statement is not a contradiction of Peter. Three horizons: Joel's restored community, the church at Pentecost, and the latter-day dispensation. Nothing in the multiple-fulfillment outlook requires choosing among them. The honest questioner's caution from the Wrestle above still applies: this is a way of reading, and a reader should know when he is using it.

Notice a real disagreement here. The academic scholar reads "all flesh" as Judah, set against the nations of chapter 3; the six-outlooks essay notes that Peter and Paul (Romans 10:12–13) both carried it past Israel. Both are accurate about different horizons, which is exactly the point of keeping the outlooks apart.

4. Likening. Parry takes this outlook from Nephi, who would "liken" Isaiah's words to his people (2 Nephi 11:8). Likening is different from the third outlook. Multiple fulfillment claims the prophecy itself had more than one target; likening takes a text with a specific target and holds it against our own lives. The essay compares this with the Dead Sea Scroll commentaries called pesharim, which applied each line of a prophet to the community's own time, and is careful to flag the comparison: Pesher is not the same as Nephi's likening, and Parry does not mention it; the comparison is mine.

The lesson suggests that children put each other's names in place of "your sons" and "your daughters." The adult version is to notice who Joel names. Joel's list has no clergy in it. If the Spirit is promised to every one of those categories, then a ward, a family, or a quorum in which revelation is assumed to come only to the person at the front of the room has not yet taken Joel at his word. The LDS scholar presses the same point: The text gives the prophetic gift to daughters and to slaves. A Latter-day Saint reader who takes Moroni seriously should take that breadth seriously too. The practical disciple makes it personal. Do I take my daughter's spiritual impressions as seriously as my own? Whose dreams in my ward am I not hearing because of who they are?

5. Personal reading. Parry describes reading for personal instruction and guidance, and leans on Elder Oaks's statement that scripture reading "may also lead to current revelation on whatever else the Lord wishes to communicate to the reader at that time." That phrase, "whatever else," is the key. A prompting about a strained relationship while reading Joel 2 is not a misreading; the passage is functioning as a place where revelation happens. But a personal impression received while reading Joel 2 is real and may be precious, but it should not be offered to a class as what Joel meant.

The lesson pairs Joel with President Nelson's warning that "in coming days, it will not be possible to survive spiritually without the guiding, directing, comforting, and constant influence of the Holy Ghost." The doctrinal connector ties the two together: Joel's list (sons, daughters, old men, young men, servants, handmaids, 2:28–29) is the scriptural ground for that expectation: revelation is poured on "all flesh." And the lesson's quotation from Elder David A. Bednar, that the Spirit is "still and small and incremental over time," keeps "pour" from sounding only dramatic. An outpouring can look like a steady trickle across an ordinary week.

6. Doctrine. The essay draws four doctrines from five verses. The first is that the Spirit is God's to give and is given lavishly. The verb is "pour," the very contrast the lesson's children's activity draws between a pour and a trickle. The second is that access to revelation is broad, which Latter-day Saints confess in the seventh article of faith's "prophecy, revelation, visions." The third is that deliverance comes by calling on the name of the Lord: "whosoever shall call on the name of the LORD shall be delivered" (Joel 2:32), a line both Peter and Paul read as an invitation that reaches beyond Israel. The fourth is that the outpouring of the Spirit and "the great and the terrible day of the LORD" belong together; Joel places the gift before the day (2:31), as preparation, not as an afterthought.

Why keep the six apart. The value of Parry's framework is that it separates readings a careful reader already senses at once, so each can be held to its own standard. The near view answers to the Hebrew and to history; the distant and multiple views answer to how Acts and Joseph Smith—History actually use the passage; likening and personal reading answer to the Spirit and the reader's honesty; doctrine answers to the whole canon. Held that way, Peter's "this is that" and Moroni's "not yet" stop competing.

The valley of decision (Joel 3)

Chapter 3 turns to the nations. God will "gather all nations, and will bring them down into the valley of Jehoshaphat" (3:2), a name that means "the LORD judges." Their crimes are specific: they "cast lots for my people; and have given a boy for an harlot, and sold a girl for wine" (3:3), and sold Judah's children "unto the Grecians" (3:6).

Joel has ethical edges that lessons usually skip. The punishment matches the crime: "I will sell your sons and your daughters into the hand of the children of Judah, and they shall sell them to the Sabeans" (3:8). Then a summons: "Beat your plowshares into swords, and your pruninghooks into spears: let the weak say, I am strong" (3:10). "Beat your plowshares into swords" (3:10) deliberately reverses Isaiah 2:4 and Micah 4:3. How should we read that? Some take it as irony, a summons for the nations to arm for a battle they will lose. Others see prophets arguing with one another, or a sharper, more vengeful hope in Joel than in Isaiah.

"Multitudes, multitudes in the valley of decision: for the day of the LORD is near in the valley of decision" (3:14). Then a line that ties Joel to the next book in the Hebrew scroll: One example sits in this week's reading: Joel 3:16, "The LORD also shall roar out of Zion, and utter his voice from Jerusalem," is almost word for word the opening line of Amos (Amos 1:2), the book that follows Joel in the Hebrew order.

The book ends with plenty, "the mountains shall drop down new wine, and the hills shall flow with milk" (3:18), and a fountain flowing out of the house of the LORD. But it also ends with borders. Joel ends on a nationalist note, with Egypt and Edom made desolate while "Judah shall dwell for ever" (3:19–20). The book itself leaves open how that sits with the gift of the spirit. The same book that pours the Spirit on servants and handmaids ends with foreigners kept out of Jerusalem (3:17). Peter and Paul resolved that tension one way; Joel leaves it for the reader.

Where the panel ends

The honest questioner, after every hard verse: A faithful reading does not have to explain the hard verses away. It can admit that the same book contains a God who says "mine heart is turned within me" and a prophet who could not picture covenant love without threats of exposure, and leave the reader to wrestle, as Jacob did in 12:3–4, without expecting the blessing to come easily. The doctrinal connector ends on the road of 14:9: The God who hedged the wrong road with thorns has opened the right one, and He waits on it as dew. The rabbi ends with Rashi's reading of "all flesh" as softened hearts: That is Joel 2:13 again, the heart rent rather than the garment. The six-outlooks essay ends where Joel's promise is still going: Joel 2:28–32 rewards all six, and reading it once through each lens is a good way to see how a single promise about the Spirit could be fulfilled at Pentecost, declared "soon to be" fulfilled in 1823, and still be addressed, this week, to someone's sons and daughters. And the practical disciple, closing on Hosea's last verse: Understanding these chapters is not mainly solving the puzzle of Gomer. It is walking.

Think deeply.

  1. Joel says to rend your heart, not your garments, and the Mishnah says rending the heart means changing what you do. What is your "garment" (the visible sign of devotion), and what would rending the heart underneath it require of you this week?
  2. "I will restore to you the years that the locust hath eaten." Which years in your life, or in the life of someone you love, feel eaten? What would it look like for God to make them yield something without pretending they were not lost?
  3. Peter said "this is that"; Moroni said "not yet fulfilled." Does layered fulfillment satisfy you, or does it feel like something readers built to fit? What do you gain by noticing that neither text states it?
  4. Joel's list of those who receive the Spirit has no clergy in it. In your home, ward, or work, whose spiritual impressions do you discount because of age, sex, or status? What would change if you took them seriously?
  5. Of Parry's six outlooks, which do you use most when you read scripture, and which do you neglect? Try reading one verse from this week through the outlook you use least.
  6. Joel's book pours out the Spirit on all flesh and ends with "no strangers" in Jerusalem. Where do you see the same tension in your own faith: wide promises and tight borders? Which way does your week lean?
