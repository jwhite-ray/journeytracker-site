The Book That Grew Around a Voice

Written by an AI persona in the voice of a non-Latter-day-Saint Hebrew Bible academic — not a real scholar.

Isaiah 1–12 is not a transcript. It is an anthology with an argument, assembled over centuries by editors who left their fingerprints in plain sight, built around a core of oracles that nearly everyone who works on the problem agrees go back to an actual Jerusalemite of the late eighth century BCE. Reading it well means holding both halves of that sentence at once.

Seams you can see without a microscope

Start with the two superscriptions. Isaiah 1:1 announces "the vision of Isaiah the son of Amoz, which he saw concerning Judah and Jerusalem in the days of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah." Then 2:1 announces, again, "the word that Isaiah the son of Amoz saw concerning Judah and Jerusalem." Books do not normally introduce themselves twice. The standard inference is that an older collection began at 2:1 and that chapter 1 was prefixed later — a reading argued influentially by Georg Fohrer, who took chapter 1 as a compressed summary of Isaiah's preaching set at the head of the book ("Jesaja 1 als Zusammenfassung der Verkündigung Jesajas," ZAW 74 [1962]), and developed since by H. G. M. Williamson, whose International Critical Commentary treats chapter 1 as a deliberately composed portal built largely out of material already written down elsewhere in the book.

The popular question about Isaiah — one author or three? — was settled in its broad outlines by Bernhard Duhm's 1892 commentary Das Buch Jesaia, which separated chapters 40–55 and 56–66 from what precedes and gave us Deutero- and Trito-Isaiah. (The Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran copies all sixty-six chapters continuously — a fact about transmission, not authorship.) That is no longer where the interesting argument is. The live question for this week's twelve chapters is smaller and harder: how were chapters 1–12 put together, and by whom?

Three proposals dominate. First, the "Denkschrift" or memoir hypothesis, given its classic form by Karl Budde in Jesajas Erleben (1928), which held that Isaiah 6:1–9:6 was a self-contained composition drawn up by the prophet shortly after the Syro-Ephraimite war and deposited with his disciples. The text half-announces this: "Bind up the testimony, seal the law among my disciples" (8:16). Many scholars now draw the unit's boundary at 8:18 and treat what follows as accretion; some, following Henning Graf Reventlow's critique, doubt the material was ever an autobiographical memoir at all, not least because chapters 6 and 8 speak in the first person while chapter 7 narrates Isaiah in the third. Jacob Stromberg's An Introduction to the Study of Isaiah (2011) and John Barton's Isaiah 1–39 (1995) are the two shortest reliable maps of this terrain.

Second, a Josianic redaction. Hermann Barth's Die Jesaja-Worte in der Josiazeit (1977) argued that a seventh-century editor reworked the Assyria material — the oracle of the child in 9:1–7 and the taunt of the axe in 10:5–19 among it — for an audience watching Assyrian power collapse in Josiah's reign. Whatever one makes of the details, it explains why these chapters keep promising the rod will be broken.

Third, exilic and post-exilic growth. Isaiah 11:1 speaks of "a rod out of the stem of Jesse" — a stump, which presupposes a felled dynasty, not a standing one. And 11:11–16 maps a diaspora across "Assyria, and from Egypt, and from Pathros, and from Cush, and from Elam, and from Shinar, and from Hamath, and from the islands of the sea." That is not an eighth-century horizon.

Chapter 12, finally, is a seam, and a small masterpiece of editorial craft. Two brief psalms ("And in that day thou shalt say…" in v. 1, "And in that day shall ye say…" in v. 4) close the Immanuel material and pivot the book toward the oracles against the nations in chapters 13–23. Verse 2 is not original composition: "the LORD JEHOVAH is my strength and my song; he also is become my salvation" reproduces Exodus 15:2, the Song of the Sea. The editor is telling you that the deliverance just promised is the exodus again. The lesson title for the week, "God is my salvation," is a quotation of a quotation.

Two dates you can check against cuneiform

Chapter 7 is set during the Syro-Ephraimite crisis of about 734 BCE, when Rezin of Damascus and Pekah of Israel moved against Jerusalem to force Ahaz into an anti-Assyrian coalition and replace him with a puppet, "the son of Tabeal" (7:6). Isaiah tells Ahaz to do nothing — "Take heed, and be quiet; fear not" (7:4). According to 2 Kings 16:7–9, Ahaz instead sent silver and gold to Tiglath-pileser III and asked for help. The Assyrian side of that transaction survives: a summary inscription from Calah lists tribute from "Ia-ú-ḫa-zi of Judah" — Jehoahaz, the full theophoric form of the name the Bible shortens to Ahaz — alongside the kings of Ammon, Moab, Edom, and Philistia; the text is edited in Hayim Tadmor's The Inscriptions of Tiglath-pileser III (1994). Isaiah lost that argument, and we have the receipt.

The second date is 701, when Sennacherib campaigned in Judah and, in the words of his own prism inscriptions, shut Hezekiah up in Jerusalem like a caged bird — without taking the city. Chapters 1–12 do not narrate 701, but 1:7–9, with its scorched country and its "daughter of Zion… left as a cottage in a vineyard," reads to many commentators as its aftermath, which would make the book's opening oracle a late one.

The Assyrian inscriptions matter for a second reason. Peter Machinist's "Assyria and Its Image in the First Isaiah" (JAOS 103 [1983]) demonstrated that Isaiah's language about Assyria tracks the phrasing of Neo-Assyrian royal inscriptions closely enough that the prophet — or his tradition — knew the imperial idiom in its official court form. Read 10:8–14 with that in mind. "Are not my princes altogether kings?" is court boast. The catalogue of 10:9 — Calno, Carchemish, Hamath, Arpad, Samaria, Damascus — is a conquest list in annalistic style, and "my hand hath found as a nest the riches of the people" is the annals' own register. Then 10:15 detonates it: "Shall the axe boast itself against him that heweth therewith?" The joke only works if the audience recognizes the voice being imitated. Shawn Zelig Aster's Reflections of Empire in Isaiah 1–39 (2017) presses this further, reading much of First Isaiah as sustained counter-propaganda against Assyrian claims to universal rule.

A song that turns into a subpoena

Isaiah 5:1–7 is the most technically accomplished short poem in the block. It opens as a love song sung on someone else's behalf — "Now will I sing to my wellbeloved a song of my beloved touching his vineyard" — and for two verses it is an agricultural complaint: good hill, cleared stones, choice vines, tower, winepress, and the crop comes in sour. Then in v. 3 the singer turns to the crowd: "judge, I pray you, betwixt me and my vineyard." They have already agreed with him. Gale A. Yee's form-critical study (CBQ 43 [1981]) classified it as a juridical parable in the family of Nathan's ewe-lamb speech in 2 Samuel 12: a story that extracts a verdict before disclosing that the defendant is the audience.

Verse 7 springs the trap, and it cannot be carried into English. God looked for mišpāṭ and got miśpāḥ; for ṣəḏāqāh and got ṣəʿāqāh. Justice, and instead bloodshed; righteousness, and instead a scream. Each pair differs by a single consonant. The KJV's "he looked for judgment, but behold oppression; for righteousness, but behold a cry" is accurate and completely deaf. Translators who reproduce the assonance lose the sense, and the reverse.

Chapter 1 opens with a related legal gesture: "Hear, O heavens, and give ear, O earth" (1:2), summoning the cosmos as witness. Herbert Huffmon (JBL 78 [1959]) connected this to the witness clauses of ancient Near Eastern treaties, arguing for a distinct prophetic lawsuit form, the rîb. The genre label has since been contested — some scholars doubt a fixed "covenant lawsuit" form ever existed as such — but the courtroom posture of 1:2–20, 3:13–15 ("The LORD standeth up to plead, and standeth to judge the people") and 5:1–7 is not in doubt. Marvin Sweeney's Isaiah 1–39 (FOTL, 1996) catalogues the genre assignments unit by unit.

The throne room, and an order to fail

Isaiah 6 is the chapter most readers arrive already knowing. Two things repay attention. First, the divine names: 6:1 and 6:8 use 'ăḏōnāy, "the Lord," while the seraphim in 6:3 and Isaiah in 6:5 say YHWH ṣəḇā'ôṯ, the LORD of hosts. Second, the scene is a recognizable type. When the voice says "Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?" (6:8), the plural is the divine council — the assembly of heavenly beings around the enthroned god, familiar from Ugaritic myth and from 1 Kings 22:19–23, where Micaiah sees YHWH enthroned with the host on either side and a spirit volunteers to become a lying spirit in the mouths of Ahab's prophets. Frank Moore Cross (JNES 12 [1953]) established the type-scene's importance for reading Isaiah; the Micaiah parallel is the closer one here, because in both a prophet is commissioned to produce a failure.

Which brings up the hardest verses in the block. "Make the heart of this people fat, and make their ears heavy, and shut their eyes; lest they see with their eyes… and convert, and be healed" (6:10). These are Hebrew imperatives, and their stated purpose is to prevent repentance. Critics have handled this in three main ways, surveyed at length in the standard commentaries (Wildberger, Blenkinsopp, Childs, Roberts, Williamson). Some read the Hebrew idiom as result-as-purpose: the construction states the outcome of Isaiah's preaching in the grammar of intention, as Hebrew often does. Some read the passage retrospectively — composed or shaped after decades of unsuccessful preaching, casting the actual result backward as the original mandate. Some read it as prophetic irony, a deliberately outrageous commission whose bitterness is the point, which fits a prophet who immediately objects, "Lord, how long?" (6:11).

What is certain is that later readers found it intolerable and edited around it. The Septuagint converts the imperatives into indicative description — the heart of this people has become fat, they have closed their eyes — turning a command into a diagnosis. That Greek form, not the Hebrew, is what Matthew 13:14–15 and Acts 28:26–27 quote. Craig Evans's To See and Not Perceive (1989) traces the whole chain of ancient rewritings.

What the sign to Ahaz says, and what it does not

Isaiah 7:14 is arguably the most consequential translation decision in the Hebrew Bible, and the facts are not much in dispute among people who work on Hebrew. The word is hā-'almāh, with the definite article: the young woman — plausibly someone present, or known to both men. The noun 'almāh occurs seven times in the Hebrew Bible (Genesis 24:43; Exodus 2:8, of Miriam; Psalm 68:25; Proverbs 30:19; Song 1:3; 6:8; Isaiah 7:14), plus two instances of a plural form used as a musical rubric. It denotes a young woman of marriageable age. It does not assert sexual status. The Hebrew word that foregrounds that is bəṯûlāh — and even bəṯûlāh is not airtight, since Genesis 24:16 feels the need to add "neither had any man known her," and Joel 1:8 has a bəṯûlāh lamenting the husband of her youth. Biblical Hebrew classifies women by life stage more than by anatomy. (Hallvard Hagelia's short survey, "'Almah in Isaiah 7:14," lays out the occurrences and the lexical case.)

The Septuagint rendered 'almāh with parthenos. In Hellenistic Greek parthenos usually does mean virgin, but not invariably: LXX Genesis 34:3 calls Dinah parthenos in the verse immediately after her rape. Whether the Alexandrian translator meant to make a claim, or simply reached for the ordinary Greek word for a girl of that age, is unrecoverable. What we do know is that the later Jewish Greek revisers — Aquila, Symmachus, and Theodotion — all replaced it with neanis, "young woman." Matthew 1:22–23 quotes the Septuagint. The KJV's "virgin" descends from that Greek, through the Latin, into English.

None of which settles anything theological one way or the other; it relocates the question. "Virgin" in Isaiah 7:14 is a fact about the history of translation, not a fact about the Hebrew lexicon. And the verses around it are about something else entirely. The sign is a clock: "before the child shall know to refuse the evil, and choose the good, the land that thou abhorrest shall be forsaken of both her kings" (7:16). Rezin and Pekah will be gone within a toddler's lifetime — which, historically, they were, by 732.

Naming a king

Isaiah 9:6 gives the child a string of names: pele' yô'ēṣ, 'ēl gibbôr, 'ăḇî-'aḏ, śar-šālôm. The KJV's five titles are probably four, since "Wonderful, Counsellor" renders a single unit. The verbs are Hebrew perfects — "unto us a child is born" — which is why the passage sounds like a report rather than a forecast.

Albrecht Alt, in "Jesaja 8,23–9,6: Befreiungsnacht und Krönungstag" (1950), proposed that this is an accession text and that the piled-up names imitate the Egyptian convention of bestowing a formal titulary on the pharaoh at his enthronement. The proposal has never commanded universal assent — the Egyptian titulary has five elements, not four, and the passage announces a birth rather than a coronation — but in royal ideology those are not far apart: Psalm 2:7 has the king told at his accession, "Thou art my son; this day have I begotten thee." Most critical readers take 9:1–7 as a dynastic oracle for a Davidic king, Hezekiah the usual candidate, later reread as messianic.

That reading gains force from the comparative material. The Neo-Assyrian prophetic archive from Nineveh, edited by Simo Parpola as Assyrian Prophecies (SAA 9, 1997) and translated with the wider corpus in Martti Nissinen's Prophets and Prophecy in the Ancient Near East, preserves oracles delivered mostly by Ishtar of Arbela to Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal — kings with contested claims — and they open with the same formula Isaiah uses to Ahaz: fear not. Israel's prophets were doing a recognizable job in a recognizable idiom. What distinguishes Isaiah 10 is that he took the idiom of the empire, put it in the empire's mouth, and asked whether an axe can boast against the hand that swings it.
