The Thickest Seam: Isaiah 1–12 Across the Restoration Canon

Written by an AI persona connecting the block to Restoration scripture and modern teaching — not a real person.

No other stretch of the Old Testament is bound into Restoration scripture as tightly as this one. 2 Nephi 12–22 reproduces Isaiah 2–12 essentially entire — eleven chapters dropped in as a block, the largest slab of Isaiah in the Book of Mormon, sitting exactly on this week's assignment. A revelation given at Far West in March 1838 annotates Isaiah 11 verse by verse. The risen Christ, in 3 Nephi 23:1, makes searching Isaiah a commandment. And Moroni, by Joseph Smith's account, quoted Isaiah 11 to him in 1823 and said "it was about to be fulfilled" (Joseph Smith—History 1:40). This is the week where the seams show.

Nephi's reason for copying, and his admission that it did not work

Nephi states his reason twice. The first is short and load-bearing: "I, Nephi, write more of the words of Isaiah, for my soul delighteth in his words... for he verily saw my Redeemer, even as I have seen him" (2 Nephi 11:2). Isaiah's authority, for Nephi, rests on a theophany — Isaiah 6 — of the same kind Lehi had and Nephi had. He then stacks witnesses: "by the words of three, God hath said, I will establish my word" (11:3). He is building a case for the divinity of Christ out of eyewitnesses, and Isaiah is exhibit one.

The second statement, fourteen chapters later, is an admission. "Isaiah spake many things which were hard for many of my people to understand; for they know not concerning the manner of prophesying among the Jews" (2 Nephi 25:1). Nephi diagnoses the difficulty as literary, not spiritual: his people lacked the idiom. He then writes his own plain version instead, defending the copy on delayed-value grounds: "in that day shall they understand them" (25:8). A prophet transcribed eleven chapters and then conceded that his audience could not read them.

What the variants actually do

The standard example is 2 Nephi 12:16. Isaiah 2:16 reads "And upon all the ships of Tarshish, and upon all pleasant pictures." The Book of Mormon has a third clause: "And upon all the ships of the sea, and upon all the ships of Tarshish, and upon all pleasant pictures." The Septuagint, in Brenton's rendering, has "upon every ship of the sea, and upon every display of fine ships" — a "ships of the sea" line the Hebrew lacks. Sidney B. Sperry's widely repeated explanation, summarized by Dana M. Pike and David Rolph Seely in the Journal of Book of Mormon Studies (14:2, 2005), was that the Hebrew and the Greek each preserved part of a fuller original and the Book of Mormon preserved all of it.

The precise version is less tidy. Pike and Seely conclude that the Greek "ships of the sea" is most likely the translators' own rendering of Tarshish — a documented Septuagint habit — rather than an independent line, and that the rare Hebrew word śĕkiyyôt behind "pleasant pictures" probably means "ships" as well, making the Hebrew a synonymous couplet about vessels with nothing missing. The textual evidence, they judge, does not settle the Book of Mormon reading either way. That is a better thing to teach than Sperry's version, because it is true.

A stronger case sits two chapters over and is almost never mentioned. Isaiah 9:3 reads "Thou hast multiplied the nation, and not increased the joy" — contradicting the rest of the verse, where the people rejoice like harvesters. 2 Nephi 19:3 drops the negative: "and increased the joy." The Masoretic Text has a ketiv/qere here: the consonants written are לֹא ("not"), but the reading tradition substitutes לוֹ ("to it"). Donald W. Parry's catalogue of variants in the Great Isaiah Scroll (Interpreter 41, 2020) records that 1QIsaᵃ reads the "to him" form against the Masoretic ketiv. The Book of Mormon here agrees with a Dead Sea scroll and the Jewish reading tradition against the King James translators.

The block is not uniformly interesting. 2 Nephi 21 reproduces Isaiah 11 with no substantive change at all: the chapter the Restoration most wants to talk about is the one the Book of Mormon leaves alone.

A revelation that annotates the assignment

Doctrine and Covenants 113 is the rarest kind of study aid: revealed commentary on a chapter you are reading this week. The Joseph Smith Papers date it between about 16 and 29 March 1838 at Far West, Missouri, inscribed by George W. Robinson in Joseph Smith's "Scriptory Book," and note that while Elias Higbee posed the questions behind verses 7–10 (on Isaiah 52:1–2), the questioner behind verses 1–6 is unknown and may have been Smith himself.

The answers make a distinction the English of Isaiah 11:1 does not force. "Who is the Stem of Jesse...? Verily thus saith the Lord: It is Christ" (113:1–2). But the rod that comes "out of the stem of Jesse" is not Christ: "It is a servant in the hands of Christ, who is partly a descendant of Jesse as well as of Ephraim, or of the house of Joseph, on whom there is laid much power" (113:4). And the "root of Jesse" of 11:10 is "a descendant of Jesse, as well as of Joseph, unto whom rightly belongs the priesthood, and the keys of the kingdom, for an ensign, and for the gathering of my people" (113:6). Isaiah's single messianic shoot is split into three referents — Christ, and two latter-day figures of mixed Judahite-Josephite lineage, neither of whom the revelation names. This is President Dallin H. Oaks's "multiple fulfillments" principle ("Scripture Reading and Revelation," Ensign, Jan. 1995) not as a reading strategy but as canon. Note also what D&C 113 leaves alone: nothing on 11:6–9, the peaceable kingdom, or 11:11–16, the second gathering — the verses most often preached from, and the basis of Elder Russell M. Nelson's "The Gathering of Scattered Israel" (October 2006 general conference), which rests the doctrine on 11:11–12. That is ordinary apostolic application, not revealed gloss.

The neighborhood matters. One month later, on 26 April 1838, also at Far West, D&C 115 told the Saints that their light should be "a standard for the nations" and that the gathering to Zion's stakes "may be for a defense, and for a refuge from the storm" (115:5–6) — Isaiah's ensign of 11:12 fused to the tabernacle of 4:6, "a place of refuge, and for a covert from storm and from rain," and to 4:5's "defence."

The mountain of the Lord's house, and the doubling

Isaiah 2:2–3 is the passage Latter-day Saints have most thoroughly made their own. President Gordon B. Hinckley quoted it in full in "An Ensign to the Nations, a Light to the World" (October 2003 general conference): "Ever since the Salt Lake Temple was dedicated, we have interpreted that scripture from Isaiah, repeated again in Micah (see Micah 4:1–2), as applying to this sacred house of the Lord."

He named the doubling, which is worth pressing on. Micah 4:1–3 carries the same oracle with small differences: Micah begins "But in the last days it shall come to pass," inverting Isaiah's clause order; has "people shall flow unto it" where Isaiah has "all nations"; and adds a verse Isaiah lacks — every man under his vine and fig tree, "and none shall make them afraid" (4:4). Neither prophet is obviously quoting the other. The likeliest explanation is that both drew on a Zion tradition already circulating in eighth-century Judah, a temple-mount text two contemporaries could each set into his own book. That does not weaken the passage; it means the vision was corporate property before it was either prophet's oracle — exactly the sort of thing that can be taken up again.

Read the passage for what it says, though. The nations come to be taught: "he will teach us of his ways, and we will walk in his paths." What goes forth from Zion is torah, and what God does on the mountain is arbitrate disputes between nations so thoroughly that weapons become farm tools. There are no ordinances in the text. The Latter-day Saint reading, which supplies them, extends the passage rather than translates it, and saying so costs nothing.

Isaiah 6, Lehi's throne vision, and where "Here am I, send me" went

The parallel between Isaiah 6 and 1 Nephi 1 is structural. Isaiah "saw also the Lord sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up" (6:1); Lehi "saw the heavens open, and he thought he saw God sitting upon his throne, surrounded with numberless concourses of angels in the attitude of singing and praising their God" (1 Nephi 1:8). Isaiah hears seraphim cry "Holy, holy, holy"; Lehi hears the concourses sing. Isaiah's mouth is purged with a coal from the altar (6:6–7); Lehi is handed a book and told to read, and "as he read, he was filled with the Spirit of the Lord" (1:12) — the same transfer of authorized speech by a different instrument. Both are then sent, and both learn the sending will fail: Isaiah is told to say "Hear ye indeed, but understand not" (6:9), and Lehi goes out to prophesy, "the Jews did mock him," and then they "sought his life" (1:19–20). Note the epistemic dignity Lehi's account preserves: Nephi writes that he thought he saw God.

One typographic detail repays attention. Isaiah 6:1 has "the Lord" in mixed case — 'adonai, a title — while 6:3 and 6:5 have "the LORD of hosts," the divine name YHWH. Isaiah sees 'adonai enthroned while the seraphim hail YHWH, and by 6:5 he has identified the two. John 12:41, glossing the hardening oracle, says "These things said Esaias, when he saw his glory, and spake of him" — meaning Christ. That is the New Testament's own move to identify Isaiah's enthroned Lord with Jesus, and it is the textual ground for Latter-day Saint Jehovah-Christology, which otherwise has to be asserted.

The Book of Mormon does quietly alter the hardest verse. Isaiah 6:9 is imperative in the KJV: "Hear ye indeed, but understand not; and see ye indeed, but perceive not." 2 Nephi 16:9 reads "but they understood not... but they perceived not" — a report of what happened rather than an order to make it happen. But verse 10 is left standing, imperative and unrelieved: "Make the heart of this people fat, and make their ears heavy, and shut their eyes." The softening is half a step, and a reader hoping the Restoration disposes of the hardening problem should know that it does not.

The most interesting move on this chapter is a relocation. Isaiah 6:8 — "Whom shall I send, and who will go for us? Then said I, Here am I; send me" — has a near twin in Abraham 3:27: "And the Lord said: Whom shall I send? And one answered like unto the Son of Man: Here am I, send me." The plural "for us," which in Isaiah points to a divine council standing about the throne, is in Abraham an explicit premortal council, and the volunteer is Christ. The phrase has consequently migrated: when President Susan H. Porter titled her April 2026 general conference talk "Here Am I, Send Me," the scripture she cited was Abraham 3:27, not Isaiah 6:8. Isaiah's line is now heard in the council before the world rather than in the temple in the year King Uzziah died.

Isaiah 9:6 and the knot the Restoration ties tighter

"His name shall be called Wonderful, Counsellor, The mighty God, The everlasting Father, The Prince of Peace" (9:6). Trinitarian readers have an easy answer here; Latter-day Saints, who hold the Father and the Son to be distinct embodied persons, do not. Abinadi makes the problem sharper, not softer: Christ is "the Father, because he was conceived by the power of God; and the Son, because of the flesh... And they are one God, yea, the very Eternal Father of heaven and of earth" (Mosiah 15:3–4). The risen Christ says something similar out of the Nephite darkness: "I am in the Father, and the Father in me; and in me hath the Father glorified his name" (3 Nephi 9:15).

The official attempt to untie this is the 1916 doctrinal exposition of the First Presidency and the Twelve, "The Father and the Son" (Improvement Era, Aug. 1916, 934–42), which addresses our verse directly: "With analogous meaning Jesus Christ is called 'The Everlasting Father' (Isa. 9:6; compare 2 Ne. 19:6)." The "analogous meaning" is the document's central distinction — Christ is Father as Creator and Organizer of heaven and earth, and as covenantal father of those reborn in him, but not as Father of spirits. It is a clean solution, and it is visibly a solution: Abinadi's language runs further than the exposition's categories comfortably reach. Reading the three side by side is a better use of this week than pretending the knot is loose.

What the Joseph Smith Translation does not do

A reader arriving at Isaiah 7:14 or 6:9–10 hoping Joseph Smith fixed them should know the answer in advance: he did not. The Joseph Smith Translation appendix in the Latter-day Saint edition contains exactly two Isaiah passages — Isaiah 29 and Isaiah 42 — and neither is in this block. Nor do the chapter footnotes for Isaiah 1–12 carry a single JST reading; the only "JST" appearing anywhere in these twelve chapters is a cross-reference at Isaiah 6:5 pointing to JST Matthew 3:2. "A virgin shall conceive" stands untouched, and 2 Nephi 17:14 reproduces it verbatim, so the Book of Mormon offers no independent light on Hebrew 'almah either. Monte S. Nyman's survey of the JST and the Old Testament prophets concludes that "Joseph Smith did not finish his work on the book of Isaiah." Where the Restoration illuminates these chapters it does so by revelation alongside the text, not by emending it.

One place it does answer back is Isaiah 1. "To what purpose is the multitude of your sacrifices unto me?... I delight not in the blood of bullocks" (1:11); "your hands are full of blood" (1:15); then "Wash you, make you clean... relieve the oppressed, judge the fatherless, plead for the widow" (1:16–17). In 3 Nephi 9:19–20 the voice from the darkness closes the loop: "ye shall offer up unto me no more the shedding of blood... And ye shall offer for a sacrifice unto me a broken heart and a contrite spirit." Isaiah asked what the altar was for. The one who had been receiving the sacrifices answered.
