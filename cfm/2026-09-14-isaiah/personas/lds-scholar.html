The Least Pressured Chapters: Reading Isaiah 1–12 with the Evidence in Hand

Written by an AI persona in the voice of a Latter-day Saint academic — not a real scholar.

The kings in 1:1 are not a literary frame

Isaiah 1:1 dates the vision "in the days of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, kings of Judah." Two of those four are attested in contemporary extrabiblical sources, and one of the attestations is unusually good.

Ahaz appears in the Assyrian record under the fuller form of his name. A summary inscription of Tiglath-pileser III from Calah (Nimrud) lists tribute from a group of southern Levantine kings including Ya-ú-ḫa-zi — Jehoahaz — "the Judahite," alongside the kings of Ammon, Moab, Edom, and the Philistine cities. That is Ahaz paying for the protection 2 Kings 16:7–9 says he bought, and it sits squarely in the years of the crisis behind Isaiah 7.

Hezekiah is better attested still. In 2009 Eilat Mazar's Ophel excavation, just south of the Temple Mount, recovered a bulla reading "Belonging to Hezekiah (son of) Ahaz, king of Judah," with a two-winged sun flanked by ankh signs; its significance was only recognized in 2015. It matters because it came out of a controlled, stratified dig — the first seal impression of an Israelite or Judahite king ever recovered that way — and because it names Ahaz too, as his father. That is worth stressing, because the much-publicized "Ahaz bulla" itself does not come from an excavation: it surfaced on the antiquities market in 1996 and entered the Moussaieff collection. Most epigraphers accept it, but an unprovenanced object cannot carry weight it has not earned. The Ophel bulla can.

For Sennacherib's 701 campaign there are three copies of the annals — the Taylor Prism (British Museum), the Jerusalem Prism, and the Oriental Institute Prism. They claim forty-six walled cities of Judah taken and 200,150 people deported, and of Hezekiah: "I shut him up in Jerusalem, his royal city, like a bird in a cage." Note what the prisms do not say. They do not claim Jerusalem was captured; an Assyrian scribe with a free hand would have said so. Lachish is another matter: Sennacherib had that siege carved across a twenty-seven-meter relief program in Room XXXVI of his Southwest Palace at Nineveh, excavated by Layard and now in the British Museum — siege ramp, scaling ladders, impaled defenders, files of deportees. That is the "rod of mine anger" of Isaiah 10:5 with its own publicity department.

The Siloam tunnel inscription, found by boys swimming in the channel in 1880 and now in Istanbul, demands the most discipline of all. It records the moment two crews met pick to pick. It does not name Hezekiah, or any king; its late-eighth-century date rests on script and language. It is excellent evidence for monumental Hebrew writing and large-scale waterworks in Isaiah's Jerusalem. It is not a signature.

The same discipline has to apply to the 2018 "Isaiah bulla," also from the Ophel. It reads l-yesha'yah[u] and then nby. The aleph that would make nby into nabi', "prophet," is not there; the reading depends on reconstructing a missing edge. The find is suggestive, not probative, and Latter-day Saints who cite it as proof are lending the Restoration an argument that can be taken away.

Chapter 10 is a parody of Assyrian royal speech

Read the prisms, then read Isaiah 10:8–14. "Are not my princes altogether kings? Is not Calno as Carchemish? is not Hamath as Arpad? is not Samaria as Damascus?" — a roll of cities Assyria actually took in the eighth century, delivered in the cadence of an Assyrian annal. Then: "By the strength of my hand I have done it, and by my wisdom." That is the voice of the royal inscriptions, reproduced accurately enough to be recognizable, and then punctured: "Shall the axe boast itself against him that heweth therewith?" (10:15). The oracle only works if the audience knows what imperial propaganda sounds like. Here the archaeology does more than corroborate — it supplies the literary register the text is mocking.

Where the composition pressure actually falls

Since Bernhard Duhm's Das Buch Jesaia (Göttingen, 1892), which separated chapters 56–66 from 40–55 and isolated the Servant Songs, mainstream scholarship has read Isaiah as a composite book. Chapters 40–55 address an audience already in Babylonian exile and name Cyrus; chapters 56–66 presuppose a return.

Here is the point that gets lost in Latter-day Saint conversation. The Book of Mormon's largest Isaiah block, 2 Nephi 12–24, quotes Isaiah 2–14. That is First Isaiah — the part essentially nobody dates after Lehi. This week's reading is, on the critical question, the least pressured Isaiah material in the Book of Mormon. The pressure falls elsewhere: 1 Nephi 20–21 (Isaiah 48–49), 2 Nephi 7–8 (Isaiah 50–51), Mosiah 14 (Isaiah 53), 3 Nephi 22 (Isaiah 54).

Joshua M. Sears, in "Deutero-Isaiah in the Book of Mormon: Latter-day Saint Approaches" (in They Shall Grow Together: The Bible in the Book of Mormon, ed. Charles Swift and Nicholas J. Frederick, BYU Religious Studies Center / Deseret Book, 2022), sorts Latter-day Saint responses into four families: use the Book of Mormon to dismiss the scholarly dating; argue for Isaiah's unity on biblical grounds; accept the scholarship and account for the brass plates through revised chronologies; or appeal to divine intervention in the text. John W. Welch's "Authorship of the Book of Isaiah in Light of the Book of Mormon" (in Isaiah in the Book of Mormon, ed. Donald W. Parry and John W. Welch, FARMS, 1998, 423–37) argues the second and third lines together. Kent P. Jackson, in A Reason for Faith (ed. Laura H. Hales, BYU Religious Studies Center / Deseret Book, 2016, 69–78), takes the first: the Book of Mormon's quotation of that material puts it before 600 BCE, which he treats as the most important single piece of evidence for Isaiah's authorship of the later chapters — while conceding that the Book of Mormon vouches only for chapters 48–51, 53, and parts of 40 and 55, so it cannot settle the authorship of the whole book. Joseph M. Spencer's The Vision of All (Greg Kofford Books, 2016) asks instead what Nephi was doing with Isaiah; Grant Hardy's Annotated Book of Mormon (Oxford, 2023) simply glosses Deutero-Isaiah as a term readers need. Positions differ, among people equally committed and equally informed.

One more move cuts against the comfortable version. "First Isaiah is eighth-century, therefore untroubled" is too simple; critical scholars find later editorial layers inside chapters 1–39 as well. Isaiah 2:2–4 appears nearly verbatim as Micah 4:1–3, which means at minimum that one prophet is quoting the other or both are drawing on a shared liturgical piece. Isaiah 11:11–16 speaks of gathering a remnant "the second time" from Assyria, Egypt, Pathros, Cush, Elam, and Shinar — a diaspora horizon wider than 734 BCE. None of that is fatal to anything, but a scholar should say it rather than let "First Isaiah" do work it cannot do.

1QIsaᵃ: stability, and where it isn't

The Great Isaiah Scroll is the only complete biblical scroll from the eleven Qumran caves, paleographically dated roughly 125–100 BCE, with radiocarbon ranges bracketing it in the second to first century BCE — about a thousand years older than the Masoretic codices. Donald W. Parry of BYU co-edited the standard critical edition with Elisha Qimron (The Great Isaiah Scroll [1QIsaᵃ]: A New Edition, Brill, 1999) and has since catalogued the variants of all twenty-one Isaiah scrolls verse by verse (Exploring the Isaiah Scrolls and Their Textual Variants, Brill, 2019), sorting them into accidental errors, intentional changes, synonymous readings, and scribal conventions.

The headline is stability: the scroll is substantially the book we have. But "substantially" is not "identically," and the differences are real. In our chapters, 1QIsaᵃ at Isaiah 1:15 adds a fourth colon — "your hands are full of blood, your fingers with iniquity" — absent from the Masoretic Text and from 4QIsaᶠ, and it completes the parallelism so neatly that it may well be original and lost by scribal accident. At 2:3 the scroll suffers a haplography triggered by repeated prepositions; at 6:2 the copyist's eye skips repeated words; at 9:8 a dalet and a resh get confused. There are also places where the scroll writes the Tetragrammaton where the Masoretic Text has 'adonai, and the reverse.

That is what a real transmission history looks like: overwhelming continuity punctuated by the ordinary failures of hand and eye, not doctrinal rewriting. For Latter-day Saints who read the eighth Article of Faith's "as far as it is translated correctly" as licensing sweeping suspicion of the biblical text, 1QIsaᵃ is a corrective in the conservative direction.

What kind of variants are the Book of Mormon's?

John A. Tvedtnes's "Isaiah Variants in the Book of Mormon" (in Isaiah and the Prophets, ed. Monte S. Nyman and Charles D. Tate Jr., BYU Religious Studies Center, 1984, 165–78) counted 478 Isaiah verses in the Book of Mormon and 234 variants from the KJV across seventeen categories, judging 59 to favor the Book of Mormon reading, 126 neutral, and 49 to favor the KJV — and he stated his own limits plainly, including that errors could stand on the brass plates themselves. David P. Wright's critique in American Apocrypha (2002) called much of that evidence ambiguous or strained; Tvedtnes answered in the FARMS Review 16/2 (2004). Royal Skousen's Analysis of Textual Variants, part 2, treats this block line by line and is where to go before repeating any claim about a particular verse.

The most-cited single case is 2 Nephi 12:16, which has three cola where the Masoretic Isaiah 2:16 has two: "upon all the ships of the sea, and upon all the ships of Tarshish, and upon all pleasant pictures." The Septuagint has "ships of the sea"; the Hebrew has "ships of Tarshish"; the Book of Mormon has both. Dana M. Pike and David Rolph Seely revisited this for the Journal of Book of Mormon Studies 14/2 (2005) and found that the Göttingen apparatus records no Greek witness with three lines at all. Their conclusion is notable for its restraint: on the available Hebrew and Greek evidence, they judge that where one lands here is finally a matter of faith commitment rather than of textual analysis alone. Two BYU professors declining to overclaim on the field's favorite proof text is the model.

The most quantitatively striking finding is Stan Spencer's, in "Missing Words: King James Bible Italics, the Translation of the Book of Mormon, and Joseph Smith as an Unlearned Reader," Interpreter 38 (2020): 45–106. In 2 Nephi 16–17 — Isaiah 6–7, this week's chapters — roughly 41 percent of the words the KJV sets in italics (the translators' supplied words, absent from the Hebrew) are missing or altered, against about 1 percent of the non-italicized words. Across the Book of Mormon's Isaiah generally the figure averages near 37 percent. Whatever mechanism one prefers, the correlation is not plausibly chance, and it ties the Book of Mormon Isaiah text to the printed page of the King James Bible, italics and all. That is a datum every position has to accommodate.

Four words worth the space

7:14. The Hebrew is hā-'almāh — the young woman, definite, someone Ahaz could presumably identify. 'Almāh occurs nine times in the Hebrew Bible and denotes a girl of marriageable age; betûlāh, the word that does carry the sense of virginity, occurs about fifty times and is not used here. Hallvard Hagelia's summary of the consensus is blunt: the term does not refer explicitly to virginity. The Septuagint rendered it parthenos, and Matthew 1:23 quotes the Greek. Note also that the sign is time-bound: 7:16 gives it a deadline — before the child can tell good from evil, the two kings Ahaz fears will be gone. President Oaks's "multiple fulfillments" framing, which the lesson quotes, is doing genuine work here rather than papering over a problem, but it only works if the eighth-century fulfillment is allowed to be real first.

6:1 and 6:3. The KJV's typography preserves a distinction most readers slide past. In 6:1 Isaiah sees "the Lord" — 'adonai, mixed case. In 6:3 and 6:5 the seraphim and the prophet name YHWH ṣəḇā'ôṯ, printed LORD in small capitals. Two different words, one scene. The vision's addressee is titled before he is named.

9:6. The Hebrew has four throne-names, not five; the KJV's comma splits pele' yô'ēṣ, "wonder of a counselor." The remaining three are 'ēl gibbôr, 'ăḇî-'aḏ, śar šālôm. The set has long been compared to the five-part Egyptian royal titulary proclaimed at a pharaoh's accession, a comparison usually traced to Albrecht Alt's work on 8:23–9:6. The syntax problem is whether these are descriptions of the child or theophoric sentence-names describing the God who gives him — Hebrew names routinely make a statement about God rather than about their bearer. One datum inside this week's reading settles more than most commentators grant: at 10:21, "the remnant shall return, even the remnant of Jacob, unto the mighty God," 'ēl gibbôr unambiguously denotes God. Within Isaiah 1–12 the phrase has a fixed referent.

11:1. Nēṣer, "shoot," sprouting from the gezaʿ, the cut stump, of Jesse. The image presumes the Davidic tree already felled. Matthew 2:23's "he shall be called a Nazarene" is often explained as a pun on nēṣer; the wordplay works only in Hebrew, and it remains contested. Latter-day Saints have their own reception history here: Moroni quoted Isaiah 11 to Joseph Smith and said it was about to be fulfilled (Joseph Smith—History 1:40), and Doctrine and Covenants 113:1–6 offers explicit readings of the stem, rod, and root of Jesse. Those are Restoration interpretive claims, not philological ones, and they should be labeled as such.

The last word is a name

Chapter 12 closes the unit by quoting the Song of the Sea. "The LORD JEHOVAH is my strength and my song; he also is become my salvation" (12:2) reproduces Exodus 15:2 almost exactly — and 11:16 has just said the returning remnant will have a highway "like as it was to Israel in the day that he came up out of the land of Egypt." The exodus is the template.

And the noun in "God is my salvation" is yəshûʿāṯî, from the root y-sh-ʿ. It is the root in the prophet's own name, Yəshaʿyāhû: YHWH is salvation. Isaiah's sons carry sermons for names — Shear-jashub, "a remnant shall return" (7:3; cf. 10:21), Maher-shalal-hash-baz, "swift to the spoil" (8:1, 3). So does he. The lesson title for this week is, in Hebrew, the prophet's own name turned into a confession.
