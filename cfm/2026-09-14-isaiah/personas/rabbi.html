Chanted, Not Mined: Isaiah 1–12 in the Jewish Year

Written by an AI persona giving a Jewish reading in the voice of a rabbi — not a real rabbi.

What most often goes unsaid when Christians and Jews read Isaiah together is not a disagreement about a word. It is a difference in how the book arrives.

For a Jew who prays, Isaiah does not arrive as a book to be opened and searched. It arrives on a calendar, in a melody, chanted aloud to a congregation that already knows what is coming. Long before a Jewish child learns that Isaiah 6 is a "call narrative," that child has stood on tiptoe in a synagogue and said Isaiah 6:3 out loud. The verse is in the mouth before it is on the page.

The calendar reads Isaiah before you do

Isaiah 1:1–27 is the haftarah — the prophetic reading paired with the Torah portion — for Parashat Devarim, which always falls on the Shabbat immediately before Tisha b'Av, the fast for the destruction of both Temples. That Shabbat is named for the reading's first word: Shabbat Chazon, the Sabbath of the Vision. It is the last of the three haftarot of rebuke read during the Three Weeks of mourning; the first two are from Jeremiah (Chabad, "Devarim ('Chazon') Haftarah Companion").

And in many Ashkenazi congregations much of it is chanted not in ordinary haftarah cantillation but in the trope of Eichah — Lamentations — the mode otherwise heard in full only once a year, on the night of Tisha b'Av itself; customs differ as to which verses get the mournful melody. The effect is unmistakable: a funeral melody laid over a text not yet describing a funeral. Isaiah's eighth-century warning is sung in the key of the catastrophe it failed to prevent.

Then, beginning the Shabbat after the fast, come the shiva d'nechemta, the seven haftarot of consolation — all seven from Isaiah 40–66, carrying the Jewish people out of mourning and up to Rosh Hashanah (OU, "Tisha B'Av and the Haftarot of Consolation").

So: Isaiah is consolation literature. That is its shape in Jewish hands — not a quarry of proof-texts but the voice that says the destruction was deserved, and then says it is over. A reader working through Isaiah 1–12 verse by verse, asking of each line what it predicts, is doing something Jews essentially never do with this book.

Why the haftarah stops at verse 27

Isaiah 1 has thirty-one verses; the haftarah ends at twenty-seven, "Zion shall be redeemed with judgment, and her converts with righteousness." Verses 28–31 — "the destruction of the transgressors and of the sinners shall be together" — are simply left off. That is not an accident of length. It reflects a durable instinct that a public reading should not end on rebuke — the same instinct that made the Masoretes direct that the penultimate verse be repeated at the close of Isaiah, Malachi, Lamentations, and Ecclesiastes (Jewish Encyclopedia, "Malachi, Book of"). On the Sabbath before the saddest day of the year, in a reading chanted in the melody of Lamentations, the last word the congregation hears is redemption. Rashi keeps even that concrete rather than eschatological: Zion is redeemed through the justice practiced within her, and "her penitents" through the people who make themselves righteous (Rashi on Isaiah 1:27).

"I am full of the burnt offerings of rams"

Isaiah 1:11–17 is often read as a prophetic assault on the sacrificial cult. The rabbinic tradition reads it much more narrowly, and I think more accurately. Rashi's gloss on "I delight not" is decisive: since you transgress My Torah, and "the sacrifice of the wicked is an abomination" (Proverbs 21:27). The offerings are not the problem. The offerers are (Rashi on Isaiah 1:11). Ibn Ezra adds that "I am full" is anthropomorphism; God needs nothing (Ibn Ezra on Isaiah 1:11).

The proof is what comes next. God does not say stop sacrificing. God says "wash you, make you clean... seek judgment, relieve the oppressed, judge the fatherless, plead for the widow" (1:16–17) — and only then, in verse 18, "Come now, and let us reason together." Rashi catches the sequence: the invitation to argue with God comes after "cease to do evil; learn to do well." First you change; then you come and say, we have done what was ours to do, now You do what is Yours (Rashi on Isaiah 1:18).

Only after the altar was gone, and not by choice, does the tradition speak of a replacement for it — Rabban Yochanan ben Zakkai consoling Rabbi Joshua in the ruins: "We have another means of atonement which is as effective — acts of lovingkindness" (Avot d'Rabbi Natan 4). And even that is substitution under duress, not abolition: Rambam states flatly that when the Temple is rebuilt "we will offer sacrifices" again (Hilchot Melachim 11:1). Isaiah 1 is a critique of hypocrisy, not a repeal.

Kadosh, kadosh, kadosh: you have been saying this since childhood

Isaiah 6 is the haftarah for Parashat Yitro, the portion containing Sinai — God's self-disclosure to a nation paired with God's self-disclosure to one man. Here the rites differ, and the difference matters: Ashkenazim read Isaiah 6:1–7:6 plus 9:5–6, while Sephardim read Isaiah 6:1–13 and stop (My Jewish Learning, "Haftarah for Yitro"). So in the Ashkenazi rite the throne-names of chapter 9 arrive as the end of the Yitro haftarah, tethered to Isaiah's vision — not floating free as a Christmas reading.

But the liturgical weight of Isaiah 6 is not in the haftarah. It is in verse 3. Kadosh, kadosh, kadosh, YHWH tzeva'ot, melo khol ha'aretz kevodo. This is the Kedushah, and it is everywhere: in the Yotzer Or blessing before the Shema each morning — Rashi, on 6:3, points at that blessing by name (Rashi on Isaiah 6:3) — in the reader's repetition of the Amidah at Shacharit and Minchah, and again in Uva le-Tzion, where it is said with its Aramaic Targum: "Holy in the highest heavens, the house of His Shekhinah; holy upon the earth, the work of His might; holy forever and to all eternity" (Targum Jonathan on Isaiah 6:3). Three holinesses for three domains.

And it is said with the body. Jewish law directs that the feet be held together during Kedushah, in imitation of the angels of Ezekiel 1:7, "their feet were a straight foot" (Shulchan Arukh, Orach Chayim 95:1; 125:2), and the Rema adds the Ashkenazi custom of raising the eyes and lifting oneself from the ground, once at each kadosh. Children learn this by copying the adults beside them years before they can read the verse.

Two further things. The tradition does not read verse 1 as a plain sighting of God: "I saw the Lord" collides with Exodus 33:20, and the Talmud answers that every prophet but Moses saw through a clouded glass — while in the same passage Isaiah dies at the cedar-saw precisely at his mouth, punished for verse 5, "I dwell in the midst of a people of unclean lips" (Bavli Yevamot 49b). His confession of humility is heard as slander of Israel, and it costs him. And on the hardening of 6:9–10, Rashi declines to make God the author of the blindness: the verbs denote continuous action — the heart keeps getting fatter — and the cause is the people's own refusal, "for they fear lest His words please them, and they will understand with their heart, and return" (Rashi on Isaiah 6:10).

7:14, read in Hebrew

Here I will be plain, and I will not pretend we agree. The Hebrew is ha-almah harah ve-yoledet ben — "the young woman is pregnant and is bearing a son." ʻAlmah denotes a young woman of marriageable age; it says nothing about virginity. The word for that is betulah — and even betulah wants a clarifying phrase: Genesis 24:16 calls Rebekah betulah and adds "and no man had known her," while at 24:43 the same Rebekah is ha-almah. Proverbs 30:19 lists among inscrutable things "the way of a man with an almah," where virginity is precisely not the point. To be honest in both directions, Joel 1:8 has a betulah mourning "the husband of her youth," so neither word is technical. The claim is not that ʻalmah means "non-virgin," but that it says nothing either way — and that the Septuagint's parthenos, a Jewish rendering made before the Common Era, narrowed what the Hebrew left open.

Context governs. Ahaz is besieged by Rezin and Pekah; the oracle is a sign for him, in his crisis; and verse 16 sets the clock: "before the child shall know to refuse the evil, and choose the good, the land that thou abhorrest shall be forsaken of both her kings." A sign whose fulfillment is seven centuries away is not a sign to a frightened king.

The medievals then divide — exegesis working, not a party line. Rashi takes the almah to be the prophet's own wife, who bears Maher-shalal-hash-baz in 8:3, and rejects Hezekiah on chronology — "when you count his years, you find that Hezekiah was born nine years before his father's reign" (Rashi on Isaiah 7:14). Radak states the lexical point outright — ha-almah einah betulah ela almah — and prefers the wife of Ahaz, since 8:8 addresses "the breadth of thy land, O Immanuel," which suggests a royal son (Radak on Isaiah 7:14). Ibn Ezra, who will follow a plain sense off a cliff if that is where it goes, rules out Hezekiah by arithmetic and makes Immanuel a third son of Isaiah, alongside Shear-jashub and Maher-shalal-hash-baz: "Behold, I and the children whom the Lord hath given me are for signs and for wonders" (8:18). He adds, with mild astonishment rather than heat, that he is surprised anyone reads the verse of Jesus, "since the sign was given to Ahaz" (Ibn Ezra on Isaiah 7:14).

The Jewish reading, then, is not a reaction to Christianity; it is what the Hebrew and the chapter's own emergency produce. But I will not pretend the polemic is absent either: on 9:6 the printed Rashi turns and addresses Christian interpreters directly, asking what "from now and forever" could mean if the referent did not arrive for five hundred years (Rashi on Isaiah 9:6). Both are true. The reading stands on its own; the argument also happened.

The eight names (9:5–6 in the Hebrew)

Note the numbering: what Christian Bibles print as 9:6–7 is 9:5–6 in the Hebrew — a small thing, and a source of endless confusion.

On the throne-names — pele yo'etz el gibbor avi-ad sar shalom — the Jewish grammatical reading takes God as the subject of "and he called his name." Rashi: "The Holy One, blessed be He, Who gives wondrous counsel, is a mighty God and an everlasting Father, called Hezekiah's name 'the prince of peace,' since peace and truth will be in his days" (Rashi on Isaiah 9:5). The great titles describe the namer, not the child.

The rabbis were not squeamish about the child. The Talmud counts eight names in 9:5 — Pele, Yoetz, El, Gibbor, Avi, Ad, Sar, Shalom — as Hezekiah's, matched against Sennacherib's eight, so that the eight-named king may exact retribution from the eight-named king (Bavli Sanhedrin 94a).

On the same folio comes the passage that shows what Jewish messianism actually is. Bar Kappara asked why the mem of l'marbeh (9:6) is written closed, in final form, in the middle of a word. Because, he answered, the Holy One sought to make Hezekiah the Messiah and Sennacherib the Gog and Magog of the end — and the attribute of justice objected: David sang many songs before You and was not made Messiah; shall Hezekiah, for whom You did all these miracles, and who did not sing, be made Messiah? So the mem was sealed. An opening for redemption closed.

Redemption, in that story, was genuinely available in the eighth century BCE and was lost — not by decree, but because a righteous king failed to say thank you. That is a theology of history with real contingency in it, and a very different thing from a fixed schedule of fulfilled prophecy.

Rambam's sober Messiah (Isaiah 11)

Readers are often startled by what Rambam does with the wolf and the lamb. "Do not presume that in the Messianic age any facet of the world's nature will change... the world will continue according to its pattern." Isaiah 11:6 is "a metaphor and a parable": Israel will dwell securely among nations likened to wolves and leopards — Jeremiah 5:6 uses the same animals the same way — who will return to the truth and no longer plunder (Hilchot Melachim 12:1).

He goes further. "There will be no difference between the current age and the Messianic era except the emancipation from our subjugation to the gentile kingdoms" (12:2). The Messiah need not work miracles or raise the dead; the proof is that Rabbi Akiva backed Bar Kokhba as Messiah and no one asked him for a sign (11:3). The point is not dominion or feasting but freedom to study without interference (12:4), until "the world will be filled with the knowledge of God as the waters cover the ocean bed" — Isaiah 11:9, the one verse Rambam leaves entirely literal (12:5). And he warns against dwelling on the order of these events at all — "study of them will neither bring fear or love of God" — or calculating the end (12:2).

Two verses that ended up in everyone's hands

Isaiah 12 is six verses, and two of them are among the most recited lines in Jewish practice. Hinei El yeshuati, evtach ve-lo efchad — "Behold, God is my salvation; I will trust and not be afraid" (12:2) — together with 12:3 opens the Havdalah ceremony that closes every Shabbat, over the wine cup, in the dark. Every Saturday night, Isaiah 12 is the first thing said.

And 12:3, u-she'avtem mayim be-sason mi-ma'ayanei ha-yeshuah, "with joy shall ye draw water out of the wells of salvation," is the verse behind the Simchat Beit HaShoevah, the Sukkot water-drawing, when priests drew from the Siloam pool at dawn to trumpet blasts while the pious juggled torches on the fifteen steps. "One who did not see the Celebration of the Place of the Drawing of the Water," says the Mishnah, "never saw celebration in his days" (Sukkah 5:1, 5:4). The Jerusalem Talmud asks why it bore that name and answers in the name of Rabbi Joshua ben Levi: "because from there one was drawing the holy spirit" — citing our verse (Yerushalmi Sukkah 5:1). Water and prophecy from the same well. Rashi, following the Targum, reads the drawing as Torah: a new teaching, and the secrets forgotten in exile revealed (Rashi on Isaiah 12:3).

One last note, with no argument attached. The lesson title for this week, "God is my salvation," is 12:2 — El yeshuati. The prophet's own name, Yesha'yahu, means exactly that: YHWH is salvation.

Read it that way. Not mined — chanted.
