Your Hands Are Full: Isaiah 1–12 for People Who Already Come to Church

Written by an AI persona on living the week's text — not a real person.

The fastest way to waste this week is to read Isaiah for phrases. There are wonderful ones — "beat their swords into plowshares" (2:4), "Here am I; send me" (6:8), "unto us a child is born" (9:6) — and you can come away from twelve chapters with a handful of them and nothing else. That is a failure precisely because these chapters are mostly indictment, and the indictment is not aimed at outsiders. It is aimed at people who were keeping the observances. They showed up. They brought the animals. They kept the new moons and the sabbaths and the appointed feasts, and God says the feasts are "a trouble unto me; I am weary to bear them" (1:14). That is the honest way into Isaiah for anyone with a temple recommend and a calling.

God is full of your burnt offerings

Every item on the list in 1:11–15 is something the law commanded. Sacrifices, incense, new moons, sabbaths, solemn assemblies, outstretched hands in prayer. God is not complaining about pagan rites. He is rejecting the observance he himself required, performed by people doing exactly what they were told: "When ye make many prayers, I will not hear: your hands are full of blood" (1:15).

Then comes the part with teeth, because it is specific: "cease to do evil; Learn to do well; seek judgment, relieve the oppressed, judge the fatherless, plead for the widow" (1:16–17).

Look at what those four verbs describe. Seek judgment is going after a right outcome. Relieve the oppressed means someone is being pressed on and you take the weight off. Judge the fatherless and plead for the widow are courtroom words — to plead for someone is to argue their case when they cannot argue it themselves. Three of the four are advocacy. Isaiah is not asking for a warm feeling toward vulnerable people. He is asking you to insert yourself into someone else's trouble, on their side, at cost to yourself, when they have no leverage and cannot repay you.

That is a Tuesday. The widow in your ward whose furnace is out and whose landlord is not returning calls — pleading for her means calling the landlord yourself, or going to the housing office with her, not bringing a casserole and reporting it in ministering interviews. The coworker being quietly squeezed out of a project by someone senior — seeking judgment means saying something in the meeting where it costs you, not commiserating in the hallway afterward. The neighbor whose kid needs a ride at 6:40 twice a week — relieving the oppressed is the alarm you set, indefinitely, without being asked twice.

Ask yourself, and be exact: when was the last time you took on real inconvenience — hours, money, professional risk, social discomfort — for someone who could not reciprocate and was not in your ward? Not a donation. An action with your name on it. If you cannot name one in the last six months, 1:11–15 is describing you, and no amount of attendance is a rebuttal. Jeffrey R. Holland pressed the same point through King Benjamin's question, "Are we not all beggars?" (October 2014 general conference); most of us give what does not disturb the schedule.

Scarlet comes after the accusation, not instead of it

Isaiah 1:18 is the most-quoted verse in the chapter: "Come now, and let us reason together, saith the LORD: though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow." The manual gives it to children with a red object, a white one, and something to wash — a good lesson for a seven-year-old.

For an adult, the sequence is the lesson. The offer of snow arrives three verses after "your hands are full of blood," and it arrives through 1:16–17 — cease, learn, seek, relieve, judge, plead. It does not cancel the accusation; it follows it and is attached to it. Lift 1:18 out and hand it to yourself without 1:11–17 and you have taken the cleansing and left the changed behavior, which is the exact trade Jerusalem was already making with its sacrifices.

Notice also the verb in 1:17: learn to do well. Isaiah assumes you will be bad at this at first. Knowing whom to call, how to sit with someone in a hard meeting, how to give money without humiliating the person — these are learned by doing them badly a few times. "Learn" is permission to start clumsy. It is not permission to postpone.

The six woes are respectable sins

Isaiah 5:8–23 lists six woes, and the striking thing is how normal they are. Nobody here is sacrificing children. These are the sins of prosperous, competent, well-regarded people, which means there is only one safe way to read them: the way Dieter F. Uchtdorf described, asking "Lord, is it I?" instead of scanning for someone else who fits (October 2014 general conference).

"Woe unto them that join house to house, that lay field to field, till there be no place" (5:8). The sin is accumulation that crowds others out — legal acquisition, parcel by parcel, until there is no room left for anyone else. Israel's economy ran on families keeping their land; consolidation was how a household disappeared. The modern equivalent is not owning things. It is the moment your accumulation requires that someone else have less: opposing the apartments near your neighborhood because of what they would do to your property value, buying the third rental in a market where people cannot find a first house, negotiating a vendor down to a margin you know they cannot live on because you can. Name the last decision where you took the extra portion and told yourself the market made it fine.

"Woe unto them that rise up early in the morning, that they may follow strong drink" (5:11) — and verse 12 names the actual damage: "the harp, and the viol, the tabret, and pipe, and wine, are in their feasts: but they regard not the work of the LORD, neither consider the operation of his hands." The woe is not chemical. It is a life so thoroughly organized around appetite and entertainment that you never notice what God is doing in front of you, and you can hit it stone sober. Count the hours: the show queued before the dishes are done, the phone picked up eleven times between dinner and bed, the weekend that dissolves. If somebody asked you tonight what God has been doing in your family this month, would you have an answer, or would you have to make one up?

"Woe unto them that call evil good, and good evil" (5:20). The mechanism is renaming. We rarely decide to do wrong; we relabel it. Gossip becomes "processing." A gouging invoice becomes "what the market bears." Contempt for people who vote differently becomes "moral clarity." Neglecting your marriage becomes "providing." Pick one thing you did last month that you described to yourself in kinder words than you would use if a stranger did it to you.

"Woe unto them that are wise in their own eyes, and prudent in their own sight" (5:21). This one is for the person reading commentaries this week. Knowing more about Isaiah than the people in your Sunday class is not a spiritual condition; it is a hobby, and 5:21 says it can be a woe. The tell is whether your study has ever forced a change in behavior, or only made you better at grading other people's lessons.

"Woe unto them that are mighty to drink wine, and men of strength to mingle strong drink: Which justify the wicked for reward, and take away the righteousness of the righteous from him!" (5:22–23). The couplet is the point: strength that could have gone somewhere real is spent on capacity, and the capacity is rented out — genuine competence, deployed for pay, to make a guilty party look innocent and strip an honest one of their standing. If your work has ever required you to write the sentence that makes something indefensible sound defensible, you know what verse 23 describes, and you know the reward is what makes it hard to stop.

The vineyard had everything and still failed

Before the woes comes the song. "What could have been done more to my vineyard, that I have not done in it?" (5:4). Fenced, cleared of stones, planted with the choicest vine, given a tower and a winepress. Every advantage. It produced wild grapes.

Verse 7 lands the indictment with a pun: "he looked for judgment, but behold oppression; for righteousness, but behold a cry." In Hebrew the pairs are near-homophones — mišpāṭ against miśpāḥ, ṣəḏāqāh against ṣəʿāqāh — so the gap between what God wanted and what he got is one consonant wide and total (J. Blake Couey, "Commentary on Isaiah 5:1–7," Working Preacher). It sounded almost right. It was the opposite.

Take 5:4 personally. Write down what you were actually given: parents who read to you, a body that works, a ward that fed you when the baby came, an education someone else paid for. Next to it, write what has come out of that in the last year that anyone else benefited from. Advantage is not a defense in this chapter. It is the evidence for the prosecution.

Undone first, sent second

In Isaiah 6 the order is not optional. "Woe is me! for I am undone; because I am a man of unclean lips" (6:5) comes before "Here am I; send me" (6:8). Most people want the second line; it fits on a poster. The first is what makes the second mean anything — and notice that Isaiah does not fix his own lips. The coal is brought to him (6:6–7).

Two more things the poster leaves out. Isaiah volunteers before he is told the assignment. And the assignment, when it comes, is a ministry designed to fail: "Hear ye indeed, but understand not" (6:9), until "the cities be wasted without inhabitant" (6:11), with only "a tenth" remaining (6:13). That is the most useful thing in the chapter. You are probably ministering to someone who is not changing. The class you teach is not landing. The child you have prayed over for eleven years is not where you hoped. Isaiah 6 does not promise results; it asks whether you will keep going without them. Say the assignment out loud as it actually stands — "I will keep showing up for this person for another year and they may be exactly the same" — and see whether you can still say here am I.

Ahaz was not a skeptic; he was scared

"If ye will not believe, surely ye shall not be established" (7:9) is usually quoted as a line about faith in the abstract. It was spoken to a particular man facing a particular decision. Rezin and Pekah were marching on Jerusalem, and his heart and his people's were "moved, as the trees of the wood are moved with the wind" (7:2). Isaiah meets him at the conduit of the upper pool — out where the king is checking his water supply, doing crisis logistics — and tells him to "take heed, and be quiet; fear not" (7:4).

Then God offers him a sign, anything, "in the depth, or in the height above" (7:11), and Ahaz refuses with a devout-sounding line: "I will not ask, neither will I tempt the LORD" (7:12). That is piety used as a door-stop. He had already decided; 2 Kings 16:7–8 records that he sent to Assyria — "I am thy servant and thy son: come up, and save me" — and paid for the protection with silver and gold out of the temple.

That is the recognizable Tuesday problem. Not unbelief — fear, plus a plan already made, plus religious language deployed to keep God from interfering with it. It is the debt you take on because you are afraid of not looking successful. It is the relationship you will not end because being alone frightens you more than being diminished does. It is the small untruth told on Tuesday morning to buy one more week of calm. And it is the "I've prayed about it" that means I would rather not hear an answer. Ahaz's protection cost him the temple treasury and eventually his independence. Yours has a price too, and you probably know what it is.

What you say afterward

Isaiah 12 is two short psalms, each opening with "in that day," and together they model what a person says after being warned: "O LORD, I will praise thee: though thou wast angry with me, thine anger is turned away, and thou comfortedst me" (12:1). The praise does not erase the anger; it includes it. This is not someone pretending chapters 1 through 11 did not happen. It is someone who was told the truth about himself, survived it, and can say both halves in one sentence.

"Therefore with joy shall ye draw water out of the wells of salvation" (12:3). Drawing water is not a feeling. It is a bucket, lowered, repeatedly, because the household needs water again tomorrow. That is the shape of the week: cease something, learn something, plead for somebody, and do it again on Wednesday.
