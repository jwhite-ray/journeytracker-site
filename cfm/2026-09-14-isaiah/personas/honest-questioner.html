The Questions Isaiah 1–12 Will Not Let You Skip

Written by an AI persona that surfaces the hard questions honestly — not a real person.

There is a version of this week that goes smoothly: you read twelve chapters, underline "Wonderful, Counsellor, The mighty God" (9:6), and move on. There is another in which you follow one footnote at eleven at night and do not sleep. This essay is for the second reader. I will state the hard cases at full strength, give the actual range of responses, and leave you holding the open ends — because a faith that only survives when the questions are shrunk first is not worth keeping.

The Deutero-Isaiah problem, stated at full strength

Since Johann Christoph Döderlein's 1775 Esaias and, decisively, Bernhard Duhm's Das Buch Jesaia (1892), critical scholarship has held that the book of Isaiah is not the work of one eighth-century Jerusalem prophet. Duhm's division — chapters 1–39 (First Isaiah), 40–55 (Second Isaiah, the exile), 56–66 (Third Isaiah, after the return) — is now the default framework in the field.

The reasons are not arbitrary, and Latter-day Saints who dismiss them have usually not read them. Joshua M. Sears, who teaches ancient scripture at BYU, lays them out in "Deutero-Isaiah in the Book of Mormon" (in They Shall Grow Together, Religious Studies Center, 2022): chapters 40–66 predict Babylon's fall (43:14; 47; 48:14) by way of the Persian king Cyrus, whom the text names outright (44:28–45:8) and who did it around 540 BC. Passages assume "the chronological perspective of someone who writes before the fall of Babylon in 540 but after the fall of Jerusalem... in 586." Isaiah 49:13–26, fourteen verses, "quotes or alludes to at least fifteen passages in the book of Lamentations," a book written in response to 586. And the Hebrew of 40–66 "reflect[s] a different stage of the language." That is not one argument; it is four independent ones converging.

Now the Latter-day Saint problem. Lehi's family leaves Jerusalem around 600 BC with the brass plates; within a decade Nephi is quoting Isaiah 48–49 verbatim (1 Nephi 20–21) and Jacob is quoting Isaiah 50–51 (2 Nephi 7–8). Sears states the collision without softening it: "Although Isaiah chapters 48–53 are thought by scholars not to have even existed in 597 BC, the Book of Mormon draws upon the brass plates to quote that material."

Here is what you deserve to know about this week: the pressure is not here. Isaiah 1–12 is First Isaiah — the Syro-Ephraimite crisis, Ahaz, Assyria, an eighth-century Jerusalem prophet addressing his own city — and Nephi's block quotation of it in 2 Nephi 12–22 is the least contested part of the Book of Mormon's Isaiah. That is a real mitigation and it should be said plainly. But it does not dissolve the problem, and Sears adds the qualifier most apologetic treatments omit: "Although Isaiah chapters 2–14 and 29 are thought to have undergone significant editing after that same date, the Book of Mormon quotes them very close to how they appear in their current form." The consensus is not that 1–12 is pristine eighth-century material; it is that its core is. Nephi has the edited version.

The responses deserve to be known as positions rather than slogans. Sears sorts them into four families: treat the Book of Mormon as the trump card and reject the dating outright; contest the scholarship on its own linguistic ground, arguing for unified authorship; accept the consensus broadly while reconstructing what could plausibly have stood on the brass plates by 597; or allow that God bypassed ordinary textual transmission. Alongside these sits Blake Ostler's expansion model — "The Book of Mormon as a Modern Expansion of an Ancient Source," Dialogue 20/1 (1987) — which proposes that an authentically ancient record was expanded by Joseph Smith under inspiration, so that not every word on the page translates something on the plates. From outside, David P. Wright's "Isaiah in the Book of Mormon: Or Joseph Smith in Isaiah" (in American Apocrypha, Signature Books, 2002) argues the reverse: the Book of Mormon's Isaiah is a revision of the King James Version, not a translation of anything ancient. And Joseph M. Spencer's The Vision of All (Greg Kofford Books, 2016) works through why Nephi cared about Isaiah at all, without dodging the scholarship or pretending Isaiah is easy.

The Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran, about 125 BCE, holds all sixty-six chapters as one book, and its scribe split it after chapter 33 rather than 39. Interesting; not decisive — a copy from 125 BCE is centuries downstream of both proposed dates. This is an open problem, and the people telling you it is closed, in either direction, are the ones to be careful with.

7:14: the sign Ahaz was actually given

Read 7:14–16 as a single unit, which is how it is written. "Behold, a virgin shall conceive, and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel... For before the child shall know to refuse the evil, and choose the good, the land that thou abhorrest shall be forsaken of both her kings."

The sign has a deadline. Ahaz is terrified of Rezin of Syria and Pekah of Israel (7:1–2); the sign is that before a particular child reaches the age of moral discrimination, both those kings will be gone. That is a sign about 734 BC. The Church's own footnote to 7:16 concedes the timeframe: "IE before he is mature."

Then the Hebrew. The word is 'almah, a young woman of childbearing age; the word meaning specifically "virgin" is betulah. Hallvard Hagelia ("'Almah in Isaiah 7:14," Bible Interp, 2012) notes that betulah occurs some fifty times and refers exclusively to virgins, while 'almah occurs nine times and means "marriageable girl." Isaiah also uses the definite article — ha-'almah, "the young woman" — reading most naturally as a specific woman Ahaz could have been shown. The Septuagint rendered 'almah with Greek parthenos, and Matthew 1:22–23 quotes the Greek, not the Hebrew.

So the hard version: Matthew builds a virgin-birth proof text out of a Greek translation choice, applied to an oracle whose own closing line ties it to a war that ended in the 730s BC. And the Book of Mormon, at 2 Nephi 17:14, reproduces the King James "virgin."

The dual-fulfillment answer deserves its full statement, not a dismissal. On that reading Isaiah's oracle genuinely addressed Ahaz and carried a second horizon its author need not have fully seen — a pattern the New Testament uses constantly. 'Almah does not exclude virginity; it simply does not specify it, and the Septuagint translators, working two centuries before Christ, read it as implying it. And the name matters more than the mechanism: the sign is Immanuel, God with us, and 8:8 addresses the land itself as "O Immanuel." The child is a theological claim before he is a biological one.

What I will not do is pretend these are the same reading. One says 7:14 is about a virgin birth; the other says it is about a young woman in Jerusalem in 734 BC and that Matthew is doing something with it the Hebrew does not say. Both are held by serious people.

6:9–10: a commission to prevent repentance

This is harder than the authorship question, because authorship is a historical puzzle and this is a moral one.

"Go, and tell this people, Hear ye indeed, but understand not; and see ye indeed, but perceive not. Make the heart of this people fat, and make their ears heavy, and shut their eyes; lest they see with their eyes, and hear with their ears, and understand with their heart, and convert, and be healed" (6:9–10).

Isaiah has just volunteered — "Here am I; send me" — and the assignment is to close ears so that healing does not happen. Not to warn a people who will refuse: to cause the refusal. The passage is not describing hardness, it is commissioning it, with "lest" naming the outcome God is preventing.

There are readings that soften it. Hebrew imperatives can express a result the speaker foresees rather than an outcome he intends, and prophetic irony is a real feature of the genre — a preacher told to preach until nothing gets through. The Septuagint went further, turning the imperatives into indicatives, so the Greek describes a people whose heart has grown dull rather than a prophet told to dull it; that is the version Matthew 13:15 and Acts 28:27 quote. But Mark 4:12 and John 12:40 keep the causative force. The New Testament does not speak with one voice here.

2 Nephi 16:9–10 preserves the passage with one shift: verse 9 moves to past tense — "Hear ye indeed, but they understood not; and see ye indeed, but they perceived not" — describing the people rather than commanding the prophet. Verse 10 is unchanged, imperatives intact: "Make the heart of this people fat."

And the Joseph Smith Translation? I checked rather than assumed. There is no JST reading cited for Isaiah 6:9 or 6:10 in the Latter-day Saint edition, and the only JST footnote in the Matthew 13:10–17 range touches verse 12, not the Isaiah quotation. That matters, because Joseph Smith demonstrably did revise this category of text elsewhere: JST Exodus 7:3 reads "And Pharaoh will harden his heart," and JST Exodus 7:13, "And Pharaoh hardened his heart" — relocating agency from God to the man. He had the instinct and he applied it in Exodus. He did not apply it to Isaiah 6. Read that as significant or as incidental; I do not think you can read it as nothing.

Chapter 3, chapter 1, chapter 10

Three ethical problems, briefly, because they are cumulative.

Isaiah 3:16–24 catalogues what will be stripped from "the daughters of Zion" for walking "with stretched forth necks and wanton eyes": twenty-one items of jewelry and clothing, ending in scabbed scalps, baldness, sackcloth, "burning instead of beauty," and, in verse 17, "the LORD will discover their secret parts." The charge is vanity; the punishment is public sexual humiliation, aimed squarely at a woman's body — the kind of imagery Katheryn Pfisterer Darr traces across the whole scroll in Isaiah's Vision and the Family of God (Westminster John Knox, 1994). One can note that chapter 3 also indicts the men — "ye have eaten up the vineyard; the spoil of the poor is in your houses" (3:14) — and that the finery is bought with that spoil. True, and it does not make verse 17 read differently.

Chapter 1 is the opposite kind of shock, aimed at religious people. "Bring no more vain oblations; incense is an abomination unto me; the new moons and sabbaths, the calling of assemblies, I cannot away with" (1:13). God is describing worship he himself commanded in Leviticus as something he cannot stand. The reason follows in 1:15 and 1:17: "your hands are full of blood... relieve the oppressed, judge the fatherless, plead for the widow." If a chapel produces warmth in you rather than a slight chill, chapter 1 is not finished with you.

Chapter 10 puts God's own conduct in question. Assyria is "the rod of mine anger" (10:5), sent "to take the spoil, and to take the prey, and to tread them down like the mire of the streets" (10:6) — then punished for pride in having done it (10:12). It is worth knowing what that instrument was: Sennacherib's palace reliefs from Nineveh, now in the British Museum, depict the 701 BC siege of Lachish in Judah, including the execution of the city's leaders — prisoners impaled and flayed — and the deportation of its people. The text's own answer is 10:15: "Shall the axe boast itself against him that heweth therewith?" That is an answer about sovereignty. It is not an answer about the people under the axe.

The KJV inside the Book of Mormon

2 Nephi 12–24 tracks the 1611 King James Version of Isaiah 2–14 closely. John A. Tvedtnes's count ("The Isaiah Variants in the Book of Mormon," FARMS Preliminary Report, 1981; expanded in Isaiah and the Prophets, ed. Nyman and Tate, BYU Religious Studies Center, 1984) finds that of 478 Isaiah verses in the Book of Mormon, 201 agree with the King James reading and 207 vary. Many of the variants cluster where the King James prints italics — words the 1611 translators supplied because the Hebrew had none. That is the critics' strongest textual argument, and it is Wright's: an ancient Hebrew source should not leave fingerprints shaped like a seventeenth-century English typographic convention.

The faithful side has real material too, and the honest version of it is more interesting than the popular version. The classic Latter-day Saint example sits in this week's block: 2 Nephi 12:16 has three ship-lines where Isaiah 2:16 has two — "upon all the ships of the sea, and upon all the ships of Tarshish, and upon all pleasant pictures" — the first matching the Septuagint, the second the Hebrew. Sidney B. Sperry read this as the Book of Mormon preserving a fuller original. But Dana M. Pike and David Rolph Seely, both BYU professors, revisited it in the Journal of Book of Mormon Studies 14/2 (2005) and concluded Sperry's argument does not hold: the rare Hebrew word śĕkîyôt, which the King James rendered "pictures," almost certainly means ships or craft, so Isaiah 2:16 was about vessels in both lines all along. Which leaves 2 Nephi 12:16 reproducing a King James mistranslation in the same verse where it adds a line.

Be precise about what that shows. It does not show the Book of Mormon is false; a loose or expansive translation model absorbs it without strain, and so does Ostler's. It does show that the most-cited evidence in the other direction was overstated, and that two believing scholars at the Church's own university said so in print. Grant Hardy's The Annotated Book of Mormon (Oxford, 2023) is where these questions get handled at length.

"Multiple fulfillments" as method

The manual asks you to chart Isaiah's prophecies under three headings — Isaiah's day, the Savior's mortal ministry, the latter days — and quotes President Dallin H. Oaks: "The book of Isaiah contains numerous prophecies that seem to have multiple fulfillments" ("Scripture Reading and Revelation," Ensign, January 1995).

This is a powerful tool, and it is how the New Testament itself reads Isaiah. Matthew 4:16 takes 9:2 to Galilee; Nephi takes the whole thing to a family on the far side of an ocean. Reading a text as live rather than archived is the reason scripture functions as scripture.

It is also unfalsifiable, and that should be said out loud once. A method permitting any passage to be fulfilled in the eighth century BC, the first century AD, and the present cannot produce a reading the evidence could overturn; no configuration of facts counts against it. That is not a hidden defect — Oaks names the safeguard explicitly, "the importance of our seeking revelation from the Holy Ghost to help us interpret them." But a safeguard located entirely inside the reader is a different kind of safeguard than one located in the text, and pretending otherwise is how people get blindsided later. Use the method. Just know what kind of instrument you are holding.
