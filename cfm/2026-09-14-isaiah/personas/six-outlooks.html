The Young Woman Is Already Pregnant: Isaiah 7:14 Through Six Outlooks

Written by an AI persona applying Donald W. Parry's six-outlooks framework. Professor Parry did not write, review, or endorse this essay, and no words are attributed to him that are not cited to a published source.

Donald W. Parry, professor of Hebrew Bible at BYU and a member of the international team of Dead Sea Scrolls translators, lays out six ways of reading Isaiah in a chapter called "Ways to Read Isaiah: Six Outlooks," in Search Diligently the Words of Isaiah: the near or historical view; the distant or prophetic view; dual- and multiple-fulfillment; likening Isaiah's words to ourselves; reading for personal reasons; and reading to understand doctrine. Isaiah, he writes, "presents us with views and perspectives that can impact us immediately (here and now) or into the far future."

I want to run all six on one verse and report honestly what each yields. I chose Isaiah 7:14 because it is the rare passage where both the near referent and the distant referent are stated in a text rather than supplied by an interpreter: the near timetable is in verse 16, the distant claim in Matthew 1:22–23. Nothing has to be smuggled in. There is also a fair-test element — Parry himself uses 7:14 as his worked example of dual fulfillment.

What the Hebrew actually says

Four features of the verse do not survive into the KJV, and all four matter.

The giver of the sign changes. In 7:11 Ahaz is told, "Ask thee a sign of the LORD thy God" — full capitals, the divine name YHWH. In 7:14 it is "the Lord himself," mixed case, Hebrew 'adonai. Ahaz refused to ask of YHWH; the sign comes anyway.

The addressee changes. Through verse 12 Isaiah speaks to Ahaz in the singular. In 7:13 he turns: "Hear ye now, O house of David" — plural. The sign is given to a dynasty, not to one man.

The tense is not future. The Hebrew reads hinneh hā-'almāh hārāh wəyōleḏeṯ bēn. Hārāh is a feminine singular adjective, "pregnant"; wəyōleḏeṯ a Qal participle, "and bearing." Closer to "behold, the young woman is pregnant and is bearing a son." And it is the young woman — the noun carries the definite article. Somebody in that conversation knew who she was.

The name is two words: 'immanū 'ēl, "with-us God" — a sentence, not a label. Isaiah uses those same two words twice more, in 8:8 and 8:10, and the KJV renders them as a proper name the first time and a clause the second, concealing the point he is making.

1. The near view

The setting is the Syro-Ephraimite crisis, roughly 734–732 BCE. Rezin of Damascus and Pekah of Israel march on Jerusalem to depose Ahaz and install "the son of Tabeal" (7:6), and the court reacts as you would expect: "his heart was moved, and the heart of his people, as the trees of the wood are moved with the wind" (7:2). Isaiah catches the king inspecting the water supply — "at the end of the conduit of the upper pool in the highway of the fuller's field" (7:3) — because Ahaz expects a siege.

The message is a counsel of nerve: "Take heed, and be quiet; fear not, neither be fainthearted for the two tails of these smoking firebrands" (7:4) — the two kings are spent matches. Verse 9 closes with a pun the KJV flattens: 'im lō' ta'amīnū kī lō' tē'āmēnū, two stems of the root 'mn — if you will not hold firm, you will not be held firm.

Ahaz declines the sign with a piece of piety — "I will not ask, neither will I tempt the LORD" (7:12) — that is almost certainly cover, because he had already chosen another solution: "Ahaz sent messengers to Tiglath-pileser king of Assyria, saying, I am thy servant and thy son... And Ahaz took the silver and gold that was found in the house of the LORD... and sent it for a present" (2 Kings 16:7–8). It worked, in the short and disastrous way such things work; Damascus fell in 732 and Rezin was killed (2 Kings 16:9).

So the sign's near function is a clock, not a miracle: "before the child shall know to refuse the evil, and choose the good, the land that thou abhorrest shall be forsaken of both her kings" (7:16). Two or three years. The deadline was met — and Isaiah's next breath is that the rescue is the next catastrophe: "the LORD shall bring upon thee... even the king of Assyria" (7:17), the hired razor of 7:20. Who the child was, the text never says; a child of Isaiah, a royal child, and an unnamed woman present have all been proposed, and the passage is indifferent. This outlook is rich, and it is the one Parry insists on starting with: "readers should begin by examining the book in its immediate context—a near view."

2. The distant view

Matthew makes the claim outright: "Now all this was done, that it might be fulfilled which was spoken of the Lord by the prophet, saying, Behold, a virgin shall be with child, and shall bring forth a son, and they shall call his name Emmanuel, which being interpreted is, God with us" (Matthew 1:22–23).

Candor is required. Nothing inside Isaiah 7 signals that the verse looks eight centuries downfield; the surrounding material is relentlessly local — Rezin, Pekah, Damascus, Samaria, the Assyrian razor, the briers and thorns of 7:23–25. The distant view here is not derived from Isaiah. It is asserted by an evangelist, and it runs through the Greek: Matthew's wording follows the Septuagint, which rendered 'almāh as parthenos, "virgin," where the second-century Jewish revisers Aquila, Symmachus, and Theodotion all used neanis, "young woman." Hallvard Hagelia is blunt: "the point at issue in Isa 7:14 is not the question of virgin birth but a divine message of God being with his people."

There is a distant arc internal to Isaiah, though, and it is easy to miss: the name keeps traveling — 8:8, 8:10, and then the swell into "For unto us a child is born, unto us a son is given... The Prince of Peace" (9:6).

3. Dual or multiple fulfillment

This is Parry's own example. He holds that "the contemporary fulfillment pertains to the birth of the son of Isaiah and his wife (see 8:3–7)" and the future fulfillment to Mary and Jesus, citing President Dallin H. Oaks — whom this week's lesson quotes as well — that Isaiah's prophecies "seem to have multiple fulfillments."

The honest difficulty: the child of 8:3 is named Maher-shalal-hash-baz, "quick to the plunder," not Immanuel, and his mother is "the prophetess," not "the young woman." Equating the two is an inference, not something the text states. But it is a reasonable one, because what repeats is the clock. Set 7:16 beside 8:4: "before the child shall have knowledge to cry, My father, and my mother, the riches of Damascus and the spoil of Samaria shall be taken away before the king of Assyria." Same mechanism, same deadline, same war. And 8:18 says the design is deliberate: "I and the children whom the LORD hath given me are for signs and for wonders in Israel." Shear-jashub means "a remnant shall return." These children are walking oracles.

What makes 7:14 the best demonstration verse is that the reuse is visible inside a single chapter, before Christianity exists: 'immanū 'ēl appears at 8:8 as a cry over catastrophe and at 8:10 as the ground of deliverance. Matthew does at distance what Isaiah 8 does at close range.

4. Likening

Nephi states the practice: "I did liken all scriptures unto us, that it might be for our profit and learning" (1 Nephi 19:23). Parry calls it "a productive, profitable, and sacred exercise."

The comparison to pesher is instructive mainly by contrast. The Qumran commentaries — 1QpHab is the showpiece — quote a lemma and then declare what its interpretation concerns, mapping the prophet's words onto the sect's own history and enemies; Habakkuk's seventh-century invaders become the commentator's contemporaries, and the original setting drops out. Likening, as Parry frames it, is additive, which is why he puts the near view first. That constraint is the part of the method most often ignored, and 7:14 is where ignoring it costs most: the likening people reach for — a verse about a miraculous birth — is one the near context will not support.

Liken it properly and it is sharper anyway. A frightened man is offered help on absurdly generous terms — ask a sign "either in the depth, or in the height above" (7:11), any size you like — and declines in devout language because he has already wired the money to Assyria. The question is not about virgin birth. It is: what arrangement have I already made that makes the offered help inconvenient to accept?

5. Reading for personal reasons

Thin here, and pretending otherwise would misrepresent the framework. Parry's own list of passages for personal reading — for mourning, fear, tribulation, forgiveness — draws almost entirely from Isaiah 40–66 (25:8, 40:11, 40:31, 41:13, 43:2, 58:9, 64:8, 66:13). Exactly one entry comes from this week's chapters, and it is 12:2, the closing hymn. That is not an oversight: Isaiah 7 is a confrontation scene ending in briers and thorns, not built to comfort.

What ports over is the name, and only the name — qualified by 8:8, where 'immanū 'ēl is spoken over water that "shall reach even to the neck." If the phrase worked only in fair weather, Isaiah would not have put it there. That is a small thing to carry out of the passage, and the framework is better served by saying so than by manufacturing more.

6. Reading to understand doctrine

Parry gives this outlook the shortest section in his chapter and the largest claim: Isaiah "presents scores and hundreds of significant truths and doctrines." He grounds it in vocabulary — kfr, "to make atonement," five times in the Hebrew; ga'al, "Redeemer, to redeem," twenty-five; yasha', "Savior, salvation, to save," twenty; padah, "to ransom," four. (Isaiah's own name is built from yasha'.) Four doctrines are recoverable from 7:14, and from its grammar rather than by association.

The nature of a sign. Hebrew 'ōth — a mark, a token, evidence. God proposes one unprompted, at any scale Ahaz cares to name; Ahaz refuses; it is given regardless: "Therefore the Lord himself shall give you a sign." Revelation is not contingent on the recipient's appetite for it, and judgment in Isaiah frequently consists of getting exactly what you were warned about, on schedule, with the warning still on file.

Covenant outlasting the officeholder. The shift from singular to plural in 7:13 — "Hear ye now, O house of David" — is the doctrinal payload of the address. The sign is not extended to Ahaz's faith, which has just failed, but to the dynasty and the covenant behind it. God's commitments are not indexed to the performance of whoever holds the office.

Presence as distinct from rescue. Every Hebrew term Parry lists names something God does — atones, redeems, saves, ransoms. 'Immanū 'ēl names something God is: located here. And Isaiah immediately stress-tests it. In 8:8 the Assyrian flood "shall pass through Judah; he shall overflow and go over, he shall reach even to the neck; and the stretching out of his wings shall fill the breadth of thy land, O Immanuel." Two verses later the identical Hebrew closes a promise: "Take counsel together, and it shall come to nought... for God is with us" (8:10). The KJV makes one a name and the other a sentence, hiding the fact that Isaiah says the same thing in both places. The doctrine is precise and unsentimental: divine presence is not immunity from the flood; it is presence in it. That is the most serious thing Isaiah 7:14 teaches, and no outlook but the doctrinal one turns it up.

The incarnation, and what it does not rest on. Matthew glosses the name rather than the birth: "which being interpreted is, God with us" (1:23). His claim is not that a prediction cleared its due date but that the sentence finally means what it says — the presence has a face. Read that way the near fulfillment is not falsified by the distant one; a child named God-with-us in 732 BCE was a promise with a placeholder in it.

Which is where the 'almāh anxiety can be set down. In Latter-day Saint scripture the virgin birth is asserted directly and does not lean on Isaiah's noun: "he shall be born of Mary... she being a virgin, a precious and chosen vessel, who shall be overshadowed and conceive by the power of the Holy Ghost" (Alma 7:10). And the Book of Mormon's Isaiah text does not adjudicate the lexical question either way, since it reproduces the King James wording (2 Nephi 17:14); it inherits the translation. The philology and the doctrine are not wired to each other: establishing that 'almāh means a young woman of marriageable age takes nothing away, and finding parthenos in the Septuagint adds nothing Alma 7:10 had not already said. Much of the worry about this verse is worry about a wall that is not holding anything up.

What the exercise showed

The outlooks are not equal. On Isaiah 7:14 the near view, dual fulfillment, and doctrine are rich and reinforce each other. The distant view is real but borrowed — it comes from Matthew and through the Greek. Likening works once you refuse the obvious application and take the harder one. Personal reading yields almost nothing, which tells you something true about where in Isaiah that outlook lives.

The first five outlooks ask where a passage points. The sixth asks what it says is true. A verse can run out of referents in both directions and still not be finished.
