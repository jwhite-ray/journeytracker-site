The Child, and the Names

NATHAN: Chapter 9, which for most listeners is a Christmas card. "For unto us a child is born, unto us a son is given... and his name shall be called Wonderful, Counsellor, The mighty God, The everlasting Father, The Prince of Peace."

THE LDS SCHOLAR: Four names.

ELI: Five. I can count.

THE LDS SCHOLAR: (amused) Four. The King James comma between "Wonderful" and "Counsellor" splits one Hebrew unit — "a wonder of a counselor."

NATHAN: Here's a bigger one. The Hebrew verbs are perfects. "Unto us a child is born." It reads like a report, not a forecast.

THE LDS SCHOLAR: Which is why most critical readers take it as a dynastic oracle — for a Davidic king's birth or accession, Hezekiah the usual candidate, later reread messianically. Albrecht Alt proposed the piled-up names imitate the titulary proclaimed at a pharaoh's enthronement.

ELI: And the Jewish reading?

THE LDS SCHOLAR: It takes God as the subject of "he called his name," so the great titles describe the namer, not the child. Rashi: the Holy One, who gives wondrous counsel, who is a mighty God and an everlasting Father, called Hezekiah's name "prince of peace."

NATHAN: Which isn't a dodge. Hebrew names routinely say something about God, not their bearer.

ELI: Anything in this week's chapters that pushes one way?

THE LDS SCHOLAR: Chapter 10, verse 21: "the remnant shall return... unto the mighty God." Same phrase, 'el gibbor, and there it unambiguously means God. Inside these twelve chapters it has a fixed referent.

NATHAN: And the rabbis weren't squeamish about the child. There's a Talmudic passage here that told me more about Jewish messianism than anything else I read this week. The Axe That Doesn't Know Whose Hand

The Stump, and the Song That Names the Week

NATHAN: Chapter 11. "And there shall come forth a rod out of the stem of Jesse, and a Branch shall grow out of his roots."

THE LDS SCHOLAR: A shoot out of a cut stump — which presumes the Davidic tree already felled. An image of hope that begins by conceding the disaster.

ELI: And the peaceable kingdom. Wolf and lamb, a child leading them.

NATHAN: On which Rambam startles people — he reads the wolf and the lamb as a metaphor for Israel dwelling securely among nations that stop plundering, with nothing in the world's nature changed.

THE LDS SCHOLAR: And chapter 11 is the one the Restoration most wants to talk about. Moroni quoted it to Joseph Smith in 1823 and said it was about to be fulfilled. And Doctrine and Covenants 113 is revealed commentary on it. It splits Isaiah's single shoot into three referents: the Stem of Jesse is Christ; the rod out of the stem is not — it's "a servant in the hands of Christ, who is partly a descendant of Jesse as well as of Ephraim"; and the root of Jesse is a third figure, unnamed.

NATHAN: So "multiple fulfillments" isn't just a reading strategy here.

THE LDS SCHOLAR: Here it's canon. And notice what the revelation doesn't touch — nothing on the peaceable kingdom, nothing on the second gathering. The verses we preach from most are the ones it leaves alone.

ELI: Then chapter 12, where the title comes from.

THE LDS SCHOLAR: Two short psalms, each opening "in that day." And verse 2 isn't original composition — "the LORD JEHOVAH is my strength and my song; he also is become my salvation" reproduces Exodus 15:2, the Song of the Sea. And 11:16 has just promised the remnant a highway "like as it was to Israel in the day that he came up out of the land of Egypt."

NATHAN: The exodus is the template.

THE LDS SCHOLAR: And the editor who put chapter 12 here is telling you so. The lesson title is a quotation of a quotation.

ELI: Say the name thing. Best fact of the week.

THE LDS SCHOLAR: (smiling) The noun in "God is my salvation" is yeshu'ati, from the root y-sh-'. That's the root in the prophet's own name. Yesha'yahu — YHWH is salvation. Isaiah's sons carry sermons for names: Shear-jashub, "a remnant shall return"; Maher-shalal-hash-baz, "swift to the spoil." So does he. The lesson title is, in Hebrew, the prophet's own name turned into a confession.

ELI: And our rabbi adds the thing that made me put the essay down and stare at the wall. Those verses open Havdalah — every Saturday night, over the wine cup, as Shabbat ends.

NATHAN: Meanwhile chapter 1 is the haftarah for the Sabbath before Tisha b'Av — Shabbat Chazon, the Sabbath of the Vision — and in many Ashkenazi congregations it's chanted in the trope of Lamentations.

ELI: A funeral melody over a text that isn't describing a funeral yet.

NATHAN: Isaiah's warning sung in the key of the catastrophe it failed to prevent. And they stop at verse 27, "Zion shall be redeemed with judgment," leaving the last four verses off, because a public reading shouldn't end on rebuke.

ELI: So on the saddest Sabbath of the year, in a funeral melody, the last word the congregation hears is redemption.

NATHAN: That's Isaiah in Jewish hands. Consolation literature, not a quarry of proof texts.

Now the Part Where We Don't Flinch

ELI: Hard stuff. Four of them.

NATHAN: Seven fourteen.

THE LDS SCHOLAR: The facts aren't much in dispute among people who work on Hebrew. The word is ha-'almah, with the definite article — the young woman, which reads most naturally as somebody Ahaz could have been shown. 'Almah occurs about nine times in the Hebrew Bible and denotes a woman of marriageable age. The word that foregrounds virginity is betulah, and it is not used here.

NATHAN: To be fair in both directions, betulah isn't airtight either — Genesis 24 calls Rebekah betulah and then adds "neither had any man known her." Biblical Hebrew classifies women by life stage more than by anatomy. So the claim isn't that 'almah means non-virgin. It's that the word says nothing either way.

ELI: Then where does "virgin" come from?

THE LDS SCHOLAR: The Septuagint. The Greek translators rendered it parthenos, and Matthew 1:23 quotes the Greek, not the Hebrew. The King James "virgin" descends from that Greek through the Latin.

NATHAN: And parthenos usually means virgin in Greek — but not invariably; the Septuagint calls Dinah parthenos in the verse right after her rape. And the later Jewish Greek revisers all replaced it with neanis, "young woman."

ELI: So what do I tell my class?

THE LDS SCHOLAR: That "virgin" in Isaiah 7:14 is a fact about the history of translation, not about the Hebrew lexicon — and that this relocates the question rather than settling it. Look at the next verse: "before the child shall know to refuse the evil, and choose the good, the land that thou abhorrest shall be forsaken of both her kings." The sign has a deadline — two or three years. It was met: Damascus fell in 732.

ELI: A sign whose fulfillment is seven centuries out isn't a sign to a frightened king.

THE LDS SCHOLAR: That's the Jewish objection essentially verbatim, and it has to be taken seriously. Ibn Ezra says — mildly, not heatedly — that he's surprised anyone reads the verse of Jesus, since the sign was given to Ahaz. And the medievals don't agree on who she was: Rashi says Isaiah's wife, Radak the wife of Ahaz, Ibn Ezra a third son of Isaiah.

ELI: So is dual fulfillment just cover?

THE LDS SCHOLAR: No — but it only works if the eighth-century fulfillment is allowed to be real first. And there's an argument for it internal to Isaiah. The name 'immanu 'el — "with-us God" — appears twice more. In 8:8 it's a cry over catastrophe: the Assyrian flood "shall reach even to the neck... O Immanuel." Two verses later the identical Hebrew closes a promise: "for God is with us."

ELI: Same two words, opposite weather.

THE LDS SCHOLAR: And the King James makes one a proper name and the other a sentence, hiding that Isaiah says the same thing twice. So the doctrine you get is unsentimental: divine presence isn't immunity from the flood. It's presence in it.

ELI: So the anxiety about the noun is misplaced.

THE LDS SCHOLAR: Latter-day Saint scripture asserts the virgin birth directly and doesn't lean on it — Alma 7:10 has Christ born of Mary, "she being a virgin, a precious and chosen vessel." Much of the worry here is worry about a wall that isn't holding anything up.

ELI: Second one. Six nine and ten.

NATHAN: Harder, and here's why: authorship is a historical puzzle. This is a moral one. "Go, and tell this people, Hear ye indeed, but understand not... Make the heart of this people fat, and make their ears heavy, and shut their eyes; lest they see with their eyes, and hear with their ears, and understand with their heart, and convert, and be healed."

ELI: He just volunteered — and the assignment is to close ears so healing doesn't happen.

NATHAN: Not to warn a people who will refuse — to cause the refusal. Those are imperatives, and "lest" names the outcome being prevented.

THE LDS SCHOLAR: Three readings, all in the standard commentaries. Hebrew often states a result in the grammar of intention, so this may describe the outcome of Isaiah's preaching rather than its purpose. Or it was shaped retrospectively, casting decades of failure backward as the mandate. Or it's prophetic irony — an outrageous commission whose bitterness is the point, which fits a prophet who objects, "Lord, how long?"

NATHAN: What's certain is that later readers found it intolerable and edited around it. The Septuagint turns the imperatives into indicatives: this people's heart has become fat, they have closed their eyes. A command becomes a diagnosis — and that Greek form is what Matthew 13 and Acts 28 quote. But Mark 4:12 and John 12:40 keep the causative force. The New Testament doesn't speak with one voice here.

THE LDS SCHOLAR: And the Restoration doesn't dispose of it. Second Nephi 16:9 shifts to past tense — "but they understood not... but they perceived not," describing the people rather than commanding the prophet. Verse 10 is untouched. Imperatives intact.

ELI: Half a step.

THE LDS SCHOLAR: Half a step. And there's no Joseph Smith Translation reading for either verse — our questioner checked rather than assuming.

NATHAN: Which is interesting, because he had the instinct elsewhere. The JST relocates the hardening of Pharaoh's heart in Exodus 7 from God to Pharaoh. He made that move there. He didn't make it in Isaiah 6.

THE LDS SCHOLAR: And Rashi declines to make God the author of the blindness at all. He reads the verbs as continuous action — the heart keeps getting fatter — and locates the cause in the people's own refusal.

ELI: Third. The one everybody's nervous about. Two Isaiahs.

THE LDS SCHOLAR: Here's where I get to say the thing Nathan made me hold. This week is the least pressured Isaiah material in the Book of Mormon. Not unpressured — least. Since Döderlein in 1775 and decisively Duhm in 1892, scholarship divides the book at 39 and 55. The arguments aren't arbitrary. Chapters 40 to 66 address people already in Babylonian exile, name Cyrus the Persian outright, allude heavily to Lamentations — written after 586 — and reflect a later stage of the Hebrew language. Four independent arguments converging.

ELI: And Lehi leaves around 600.

THE LDS SCHOLAR: Within a decade Nephi is quoting Isaiah 48 and 49 verbatim and Jacob is quoting 50 and 51. That's the collision. But this week's block — Second Nephi 12 through 22 reproducing Isaiah 2 through 12 — is First Isaiah. The part essentially nobody dates after Lehi. The pressure is elsewhere on the calendar.

NATHAN: Now add the qualifier apologetic treatments leave off.

THE LDS SCHOLAR: Our questioner is right to insist on it. The consensus isn't that chapters 1 through 12 are pristine eighth-century material — it's that the core is. Scholars find later editorial layers inside First Isaiah too. 2:2–4 appears nearly verbatim as Micah 4:1–3, and 11:11–16 describes a diaspora far wider than a 734 horizon.

ELI: So Nephi has the edited version.

THE LDS SCHOLAR: On the consensus view, yes. None of which is fatal to anything. But "First Isaiah, therefore fine" is letting a label do work it can't do.

NATHAN: And the responses are positions, not slogans.

THE LDS SCHOLAR: Joshua Sears at BYU sorts our approaches into four families: reject the dating outright; contest the scholarship on linguistic grounds; accept the consensus and reconstruct what could have stood on the brass plates; or allow that God bypassed ordinary transmission. Blake Ostler's expansion model runs alongside. David Wright argues from outside that this Isaiah is a revision of the King James.

ELI: Is there one fact every position has to deal with?

THE LDS SCHOLAR: Stan Spencer's study in Interpreter, 2020. In Second Nephi 16 and 17 — Isaiah 6 and 7, this week's chapters — roughly 41 percent of the words the King James prints in italics, supplied by the 1611 translators because the Hebrew had none, are missing or altered in the Book of Mormon. Against about 1 percent of the non-italicized words.

NATHAN: That's not chance.

THE LDS SCHOLAR: Not plausibly. It ties the Book of Mormon's Isaiah to the printed page of the King James Bible, italics and all. Critics read that one way and believers another, but every position has to accommodate it.

ELI: Fourth, and then we land. Chapter 3.

NATHAN: (careful) Verses 16 through 24. The charge is vanity — the daughters of Zion walk with stretched-forth necks and wanton eyes. Then twenty-one items of jewelry and clothing are stripped, ending in baldness, sackcloth, "burning instead of beauty." And verse 17: "the LORD will discover their secret parts." Which is public sexual humiliation, aimed at women's bodies, as punishment for vanity.

ELI: The mitigation?

NATHAN: Real, and I don't think sufficient. Two verses earlier the same chapter indicts the men, hard: "ye have eaten up the vineyard; the spoil of the poor is in your houses." The finery is bought with that spoil — the oracle is about an economy, not jewelry.

ELI: And it still doesn't make verse 17 read differently.

NATHAN: It does not. Our questioner's judgment is right: note the context, then don't pretend it dissolves the verse.

ELI: One thing on method and we're done. The manual asks you to chart Isaiah's prophecies under three headings — Isaiah's day, the Savior's ministry, the latter days — and quotes President Oaks: the book "contains numerous prophecies that seem to have multiple fulfillments."

NATHAN: Which is how the New Testament reads Isaiah, and how Nephi reads him.

ELI: And reading a text as live rather than archived is why scripture functions as scripture. But our questioner names the thing out loud once: a method that permits any passage to be fulfilled in the eighth century BC, the first century AD, and this afternoon can't produce a reading that evidence could overturn.

THE LDS SCHOLAR: And President Oaks names the safeguard in the same paragraph — seeking revelation from the Holy Ghost to interpret them.
