"God Is My Salvation" · the Church's lesson page

The Prophet Named for the Last Line

≈30 min · listen or read · the week's introduction — not one of the five sessions

The first Isaiah week, and the hosts spend the opening arguing about who chapter 1 is actually yelling at — because the answer is people who were keeping the observances and showing up. Our guest is the panel's Latter-day Saint scholar, since an opening Isaiah week turns on how to read the book at all: the eighth century with Assyria bearing down, a frightened king at the water supply, the manuscripts, and the fact that 2 Nephi 12–22 quotes these exact chapters. Context, then content, then a closing stretch where they take the hard questions straight — ʻalmah and “virgin” in 7:14, the commission in 6:9–10 to keep a people from being healed, and the authorship problem — without tying any of them off.

The dateline in Isaiah 1:1 is not stage dressing. Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah ruled a small hill-country state wedged between Egypt and the most efficient war machine the ancient Near East had yet produced, and two of the four turn up outside the Bible. A summary inscription of Tiglath-pileser III lists tribute from Ya-ú-ḫa-zi the Judahite — Ahaz, under the fuller form of his name. Hezekiah is better attested still: a clay bulla naming him and his father came out of Eilat Mazar's Ophel excavation, and it came out of a controlled, stratified dig, which is what separates it from the unprovenanced objects that circulate on the antiquities market. These twelve chapters were spoken into a specific emergency by a man whose neighbors left receipts.

The book that resulted is hard, and it is not a transcript. It is an anthology with an argument, assembled by editors who left their seams visible — a book that introduces itself twice, quotes Exodus at its close, and carries a diaspora horizon in chapter 11 that no eighth-century Judahite could have surveyed. It is also, for Jewish readers, not a book to be searched but one chanted on a calendar; working through it verse by verse asking what each line predicts is something Jews essentially never do with this book. And it is a quarry too easy to strip-mine. The fastest way to waste the week is to read Isaiah for phrases, because the famous ones sit inside an indictment aimed squarely at people who were keeping the observances.

For Latter-day Saints this is also the thickest seam in the canon. 2 Nephi 12–22 reproduces Isaiah 2–12 essentially entire — the largest slab of Isaiah in the Book of Mormon, sitting exactly on this week's assignment — and Doctrine and Covenants 113 annotates Isaiah 11 verse by verse. The textual questions here are not abstractions imported by skeptics; they are questions about a text the Restoration itself copied out.

The five sessions follow the block's own architecture. Session 1 (chapters 1–4) takes the opening lawsuit and the Zion oracle that answers it. Session 2 (5–6) pairs the vineyard that failed with the throne room where the prophet who must announce the failure is commissioned — chapter 5 ends with an army roaring in from far countries, and chapter 6 answers how long. Session 3 (7–8) is the Immanuel material at close range, closing with a capstone that runs six reading methods across one verse. Session 4 (9–10) sets the child with four throne-names against the empire that boasts in the voice of its own annals. Session 5 (11–12) is short to read and long to think about: the shoot from a cut stump, the psalm the week is named for, and a reckoning with what kind of book this is.

The questions at the end of each session are not review. Several have more than one defensible answer, and a few are questions the text itself declines to close.

25–30 minSession 1: The Lawsuit and the Mountain

Reading assignment: Isaiah 1, 2, 3, 4

A book that introduces itself twice

Read 1:1 and 2:1 back to back. "The vision of Isaiah the son of Amoz, which he saw concerning Judah and Jerusalem in the days of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah" — then, twenty-two verses later, "The word that Isaiah the son of Amoz saw concerning Judah and Jerusalem." Books do not normally introduce themselves twice, and the standard inference is that an older collection began at 2:1 with chapter 1 prefixed later as a composed portal.

And the portal is a courtroom. "Hear, O heavens, and give ear, O earth" (1:2) summons the cosmos as witness, the way ancient Near Eastern treaties name their witnesses. The posture holds across the block: 1:2–20 is an arraignment, 3:13–15 has "the LORD standeth up to plead," and 5:1–7 springs the trap.

The worship he himself commanded

The shock of 1:11–15 is what is being rejected. Sacrifices, incense, new moons, sabbaths, solemn assemblies, hands spread in prayer — every item is something the law required. This is not a complaint about pagan rites. It is God describing the observance he prescribed as something he cannot stand: "the new moons and sabbaths, the calling of assemblies, I cannot away with" (1:13).

Two traditions read the rejection differently, and both are worth carrying. The rabbinic line, following Rashi, narrows it: the offerings are unacceptable because of who brings them, since "the sacrifice of the wicked is an abomination." On that reading Isaiah 1 is a critique of hypocrisy, not a repeal, and Maimonides can still say flatly that sacrifice resumes when the Temple is rebuilt. The Restoration takes the other fork: in 3 Nephi 9:19–20 the voice from the darkness says "ye shall offer up unto me no more the shedding of blood... a broken heart and a contrite spirit." Isaiah asked what the altar was for; the one who had been receiving the sacrifices answered.

What God asks instead is concrete: "cease to do evil; learn to do well; seek judgment, relieve the oppressed, judge the fatherless, plead for the widow" (1:16–17). Three of those four verbs are courtroom words — to plead for someone is to argue their case when they cannot argue it themselves — so the demand is not warm feeling toward vulnerable people. It is inserting yourself into someone else's trouble, on their side, at cost to yourself, when they have no leverage and cannot repay you.

Then 1:18: "though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow." Notice where it sits. It arrives three verses after "your hands are full of blood," and it arrives through the imperatives of 1:16–17. It does not cancel the accusation; it follows it and is attached to it.

Hear the chapter where Jews hear it. Isaiah 1:1–27 is the haftarah for the Sabbath before Tisha b'Av, the fast for the destruction of both Temples, and in many Ashkenazi congregations much of it is chanted not in ordinary trope but in the melody of Lamentations, heard in full only once a year. Isaiah's eighth-century warning is sung in the key of the catastrophe it failed to prevent. The reading then stops at verse 27 — "Zion shall be redeemed with judgment" — leaving off 28–31, because a public reading should not end on rebuke. The congregation's last word before the saddest day of its year is redemption.

The mountain, and what the text actually promises

Isaiah 2:2–4 is the passage Latter-day Saints have made most thoroughly their own, and President Gordon B. Hinckley applied it to the Salt Lake Temple in general conference while naming the complication himself: the same oracle appears, with small differences, at Micah 4:1–3. Neither prophet is obviously quoting the other. The likeliest explanation is that both drew on a Zion tradition already circulating in eighth-century Judah, a temple-mount text two contemporaries could each set into his own book. That does not weaken it; the vision was corporate property before it was anyone's oracle, which is exactly the sort of thing that can be taken up again.

But read what it says. The nations come to be taught: "he will teach us of his ways, and we will walk in his paths." What goes out of Zion is torah, and what God does on the mountain is arbitrate between nations so thoroughly that weapons become farm tools. There are no ordinances in the text; the Latter-day Saint reading, which supplies them, extends the passage rather than translates it, and saying so costs nothing.

Chapter 3, and the part nobody quotes

Isaiah 3 strips Jerusalem of its leadership and indicts the men in economic terms: "ye have eaten up the vineyard; the spoil of the poor is in your houses" (3:14). Then 3:16–24 turns to "the daughters of Zion," catalogues twenty-one items of jewelry and clothing, and promises baldness, sackcloth, "burning instead of beauty," and, in verse 17, that "the LORD will discover their secret parts." The charge is vanity; the punishment is public sexual humiliation aimed at a woman's body. One can note that the finery was bought with that spoil, and that the chapter's target is a whole ruling class. True, and it does not make verse 17 read differently.

Chapter 4 is the six-verse turn: the branch of the Lord, the survivors called holy, and a canopy over Zion that is "a place of refuge, and for a covert from storm and from rain" (4:6) — an image Doctrine and Covenants 115:5–6 would put to work on the stakes of Zion, "a defense, and... a refuge from the storm."

Think deeply.

  1. God rejects sacrifices he commanded. Rashi says the offerings were fine and the offerers were not; 3 Nephi 9 says the offerings themselves are finished. Which reading does Isaiah 1 actually support — and does your answer change what you think worship is for?
  2. Isaiah 2:2–4 promises that nations will come to be taught and that God will arbitrate their disputes; the Latter-day Saint reading adds temple ordinances the text does not mention. Is that extension a legitimate way to read scripture or a substitution — and how would you tell the difference from inside?
  3. If a chapel produces warmth in you rather than a slight chill, chapter 1 is not finished with you. Name the last time you took on real cost — hours, money, professional risk — for someone outside your ward who could not repay you. If you cannot, what exactly is your attendance a rebuttal to?
  4. Isaiah 3:17 promises the public humiliation of women's bodies as divine judgment. Assume it is scripture and not a mistake. What does it require you to believe about God, and what does it require you to refuse?

25–30 minSession 2: The Vineyard That Failed and the Voice That Volunteered

Reading assignment: Isaiah 5, 6

A love song that turns into a subpoena

Isaiah 5:1–7 is the most accomplished short poem in the block. It opens as a song sung on a friend's behalf, and for two verses it is agricultural complaint: good hill, cleared stones, choicest vine, tower, winepress — and the crop comes in sour. Then verse 3 turns to the crowd: "judge, I pray you, betwixt me and my vineyard." They have already agreed with him. Form critics classify it as a juridical parable in the family of Nathan's ewe-lamb speech, a story that extracts a verdict before disclosing that the defendant is the audience.

Verse 7 springs the trap and cannot be carried into English. God looked for mišpāṭ and got miśpāḥ; for ṣəḏāqāh and got ṣəʿāqāh. Justice, and instead bloodshed; righteousness, and instead a scream — each pair differing by a single consonant. The KJV's "he looked for judgment, but behold oppression; for righteousness, but behold a cry" is accurate and completely deaf. It sounded almost right.

And the parable's question is 5:4: "What could have been done more to my vineyard, that I have not done in it?" Take that personally. Write down what you were given — parents who read to you, a body that works, an education someone else paid for — and next to it what came out of it in the last year that anyone else benefited from. Advantage is not a defense in this chapter. It is the evidence for the prosecution.

Then 5:8–23 lists six woes, and the striking thing is how ordinary they are: joining house to house until no room is left for anyone else; a life so organized around appetite that "they regard not the work of the LORD" (5:12); calling evil good, which is mostly relabeling. And 5:21, "woe unto them that are wise in their own eyes," is aimed with some precision at the person reading a study guide about Isaiah. Knowing more about Isaiah than the people in your Sunday class is not a spiritual condition; it is a hobby, and 5:21 says it can be a woe.

The chapter ends by whistling for an army: "he will lift up an ensign to the nations from far... behold, they shall come with speed swiftly" (5:26). Read straight into chapter 6 from there. The vineyard is stripped in 5:5–6; the prophet will be told in 6:11 that this goes on "until the cities be wasted without inhabitant."

The throne room

Two things in chapter 6 repay slow attention. First, typography. In 6:1 Isaiah sees "the Lord" in mixed case — Hebrew 'adonai, a title. In 6:3 and 6:5 the seraphim and the prophet say "the LORD of hosts," small capitals, the divine name YHWH. The vision's addressee is titled before he is named, and by 6:5 Isaiah has identified the two.

Second, the scene is a recognizable type. "Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?" (6:8) — the plural is the divine council, familiar from Ugaritic myth and from 1 Kings 22:19–23, where Micaiah sees the LORD enthroned with the host on either side and a spirit volunteers to become a lying spirit in the mouths of Ahab's prophets. The Micaiah parallel is the closer one, because in both a prophet is commissioned to produce a failure.

Latter-day Saints have their own doubles — Lehi "thought he saw God sitting upon his throne" (1 Nephi 1:8), his mouth authorized by a book rather than a coal, and he too is sent to people who will not hear. And 6:8 itself has migrated: Abraham 3:27 relocates "Here am I, send me" to the premortal council, which is why President Susan H. Porter's April 2026 talk of that title cited Abraham, not Isaiah. Isaiah's line is now heard in the council before the world rather than in the temple in the year King Uzziah died.

The commission to fail

Now the hardest verses in the block. "Make the heart of this people fat, and make their ears heavy, and shut their eyes; lest they see with their eyes, and hear with their ears, and understand with their heart, and convert, and be healed" (6:10). These are imperatives, and "lest" names the outcome God is preventing. Isaiah has just volunteered, and the assignment is to close ears so that healing does not happen. The passage is not describing hardness, it is commissioning it.

There are three serious ways to soften it, all held by competent people: Hebrew can state a foreseen result in the grammar of intention; the oracle may have been shaped after decades of failed preaching, casting the outcome backward as the mandate; or it is prophetic irony, whose bitterness is the point — which fits a prophet who objects, "Lord, how long?" What is not in doubt is that later readers found it intolerable and edited around it. The Septuagint turned the imperatives into indicatives, describing a people whose heart has grown fat, and that Greek form is what Matthew 13 and Acts 28 quote — while Mark 4:12 and John 12:40 keep the causative force. The New Testament does not speak with one voice.

The Jewish tradition takes the same exit by a different door. Rashi declines to make God the author of the blindness, reading the verbs as continuous — the heart keeps getting fatter — with the cause in the people's own refusal. The Restoration goes half a step: 2 Nephi 16:9 shifts verse 9 to past tense, "but they understood not," describing rather than commanding, and leaves verse 10 standing with its imperatives intact. And there is no Joseph Smith Translation of Isaiah 6:9–10, which matters precisely because he demonstrably had the instinct elsewhere: JST Exodus 7:13 reads "And Pharaoh hardened his heart." He had the instinct and he applied it in Exodus. He did not apply it to Isaiah 6.

Set against all of that is what chapter 6 has become in Jewish practice. Verse 3 is the Kedushah, said in the morning blessing before the Shema and in the repetition of the Amidah, with the feet held together in imitation of the angels and, in the Ashkenazi custom, a small lift onto the toes at each kadosh — learned by copying the adults alongside, long before reading the chapter. The verse is in the mouth before it is on the page.

And then the practical edge the poster version of 6:8 never carries: Isaiah volunteers before he is told the assignment, and the assignment is a ministry designed not to work, running until the cities are wasted and a tenth remains. Isaiah 6 does not promise results; it asks whether you will keep going without them.

Think deeply.

  1. The vineyard song works by getting the audience to convict itself. Can that move be made honestly more than once, or does it only work on people who do not see it coming?
  2. Take 6:9–10 at full strength: God commissions a prophet to prevent repentance. Of the three softenings — Hebrew idiom, retrospective composition, prophetic irony — which do you actually find persuasive, and what would it cost you to conclude that none of them work?
  3. Isaiah says "here am I" before he knows the job, and the job is failure. Say your own current assignment out loud in its worst honest form — this person may be exactly the same in a year. Can you still say it?
  4. Five of the six woes in chapter 5 describe behavior that was legal, profitable, and socially admired. What in your own week would survive a legal audit and fail Isaiah's?

25–30 minSession 3: The Sign Ahaz Did Not Want

Reading assignment: Isaiah 7, 8

A frightened king at the water supply

The setting is the Syro-Ephraimite crisis, roughly 734–732 BCE. Rezin of Damascus and Pekah of Israel march on Jerusalem to depose Ahaz and install a puppet, "the son of Tabeal" (7:6), and the court reacts as you would expect: the king's heart "was moved... as the trees of the wood are moved with the wind" (7:2). Isaiah intercepts him "at the end of the conduit of the upper pool" (7:3) — out checking the water supply, doing siege logistics — and tells him to do nothing: "Take heed, and be quiet; fear not, neither be fainthearted for the two tails of these smoking firebrands." Two spent matches.

Verse 9 closes with a pun the KJV flattens: 'im lō' ta'amīnū kī lō' tē'āmēnū, two stems of one root — if you will not hold firm, you will not be held firm. Then God offers a sign of any size Ahaz likes, "in the depth, or in the height above" (7:11), and Ahaz refuses with a devout-sounding line: "I will not ask, neither will I tempt the LORD." That is piety used as a door-stop. He had already made his arrangement: 2 Kings 16:7–8 records that he sent to Tiglath-pileser — "I am thy servant and thy son: come up, and save me" — and paid with gold out of the temple. The Assyrian side of that transaction survives in the Calah tribute list. Isaiah lost that argument, and we have the receipt.

That makes him a more recognizable figure than the Sunday School villain. Not unbelief — fear, plus a plan already made, plus religious language deployed to keep God from interfering with it.

What 7:14 says in Hebrew

Four features of the verse do not survive into English. The giver changes: in 7:11 the sign is asked of "the LORD thy God," YHWH; in 7:14 it is "the Lord himself," 'adonai. The addressee changes: at 7:13 Isaiah turns from the singular to "Hear ye now, O house of David." The sign goes to a dynasty, not a man — not extended to Ahaz's faith, which has just failed, but to the covenant behind the office. The tense is not future: hinneh hā-'almāh hārāh wəyōleḏeṯ bēn, "behold, the young woman is pregnant and is bearing a son." And the noun carries the definite article. Somebody in that conversation knew who she was.

Then the word itself. 'Almāh denotes a young woman of marriageable age; the word that foregrounds virginity is bəṯûlāh, and it is not used here. Even bəṯûlāh is not airtight: Genesis 24:16 adds "neither had any man known her," and Joel 1:8 has one mourning the husband of her youth. Biblical Hebrew classifies women by life stage more than by anatomy. The Septuagint rendered 'almāh with parthenos; the later Jewish Greek revisers — Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion — all replaced it with neanis, "young woman." Matthew 1:23 quotes the Septuagint, and the KJV's "virgin" descends from that Greek through the Latin into English. That relocates the question rather than settling it: "virgin" here is a fact about the history of translation, not about the Hebrew lexicon.

And the sign has a deadline built in. "Before the child shall know to refuse the evil, and choose the good, the land that thou abhorrest shall be forsaken of both her kings" (7:16) — two or three years, and the deadline was met: Damascus fell in 732 and Rezin was killed. A sign whose fulfillment is seven centuries away is not a sign to a frightened king.

The medieval Jewish commentators worked this out long before the modern debate and did not agree with one another. The medievals then divide — exegesis working, not a party line. Rashi takes the almah to be Isaiah's own wife, who bears Maher-shalal-hash-baz in 8:3; Radak prefers the wife of Ahaz, since 8:8 addresses "thy land, O Immanuel"; Ibn Ezra makes Immanuel a third son of Isaiah and adds that he is frankly surprised anyone reads the verse of Jesus, since the sign was given to Ahaz.

The children are the argument

Chapter 8 is where Isaiah's method becomes visible. He writes a name on a great scroll before the child exists — Maher-shalal-hash-baz, "swift to the spoil" — and 8:4 repeats the clock of 7:16 exactly: "before the child shall have knowledge to cry, My father, and my mother, the riches of Damascus and the spoil of Samaria shall be taken away." Same mechanism, same deadline, same war. And 8:18 says the design is deliberate: "I and the children whom the LORD hath given me are for signs and for wonders in Israel." Shear-jashub means "a remnant shall return." These children are walking oracles, and so is their father: Yesha'yahu, YHWH is salvation.

Chapter 8 also holds the block's clearest hint about how it was assembled: "Bind up the testimony, seal the law among my disciples" (8:16). Whatever one makes of the old proposal that 6:1–9:6 was a memoir deposited with Isaiah's disciples, this material was collected as a unit, and 8:16 half-tells you so.

Then watch what Isaiah does with the name. At 8:8 the Assyrian flood "shall reach even to the neck; and the stretching out of his wings shall fill the breadth of thy land, O Immanuel." Two verses later the identical Hebrew closes a promise: "Take counsel together, and it shall come to nought... for God is with us." The KJV makes one a proper name and the other a sentence, hiding the fact that Isaiah says the same thing in both places. The doctrine that falls out is precise and unsentimental: divine presence is not immunity from the flood; it is presence in it.

Go deeper: six ways to read one verse

Donald W. Parry lays out six outlooks for reading Isaiah: the near or historical view, the distant or prophetic view, dual and multiple fulfillment, likening, reading for personal reasons, and reading to understand doctrine. Run all six across 7:14 and they do not come out equal, which is the useful finding.

The near view is rich, and Parry insists you start there: a clock, not a miracle, in a specific war. The distant view is real but borrowed — nothing inside Isaiah 7 signals that the verse looks eight centuries downfield. It is asserted by an evangelist, and it runs through the Greek. Dual fulfillment is Parry's own worked example, and its best evidence is internal: Isaiah redeploys 'immanū 'ēl at 8:8 and 8:10, so Matthew does at distance what Isaiah 8 does at close range. Likening usually goes wrong here, because the obvious application — a miraculous birth — is the one the near context will not support. Liken it properly and it is sharper: a frightened man is offered help on absurdly generous terms and declines in devout language because he has already wired the money to Assyria. What arrangement have I already made that makes the offered help inconvenient to accept? Personal reading yields almost nothing here, and saying so is more honest than manufacturing something. Doctrine pays out most: a sign given to someone who refused to ask, covenant outlasting the officeholder, presence distinguished from rescue.

In Latter-day Saint scripture the virgin birth is asserted directly and does not lean on Isaiah's noun at all — Alma 7:10 calls Mary "a virgin, a precious and chosen vessel." Establishing that 'almāh means a young woman of marriageable age takes nothing from that, and finding parthenos in the Septuagint adds nothing Alma had not already said. Much of the worry about this verse is worry about a wall that is not holding anything up.

Think deeply.

  1. Isaiah tells a king facing two invading armies to take no action. Was that good counsel? Ahaz's alternative worked in the short term and cost him his independence — but if you were his security adviser with the information he had, what would you have said?
  2. If 7:14 was a sign with a two-year deadline about a woman Ahaz could identify, on what grounds does Matthew get to apply it to Mary — and is that a discovery of something latent in the text or an act of creative appropriation the church later canonized? Can it be both?
  3. Isaiah says God with us twice in chapter 8: once over floodwater at the neck, once as the ground of deliverance. Which of the two have you actually experienced, and which one does your prayer language assume?
  4. Ahaz refused a sign by quoting scripture at God. What is the piety you deploy when you have already decided?

25–30 minSession 4: Four Names and an Axe

Reading assignment: Isaiah 9, 10

A birth announcement in the perfect tense

"Unto us a child is born, unto us a son is given" (9:6). The Hebrew verbs are perfects — a report, not a forecast, which is why the passage has the cadence of an announcement from the palace. Albrecht Alt proposed that 8:23–9:6 is an accession text and that the piled-up names imitate the Egyptian practice of bestowing a titulary on a pharaoh at his enthronement. In royal ideology birth and accession are not far apart — Psalm 2:7 has the king told at his crowning, "Thou art my son; this day have I begotten thee." Most critical readers take 9:1–7 as a dynastic oracle for a Davidic king, Hezekiah the usual candidate, later reread as messianic.

The KJV's five titles are probably four: the comma in "Wonderful, Counsellor" splits one Hebrew unit, pele' yô'ēṣ, "wonder of a counselor." The real question is syntactic — descriptions of the child, or sentence-names describing the God who gives him? Hebrew names routinely make a statement about God rather than their bearer, and one datum in this week's reading settles more than commentators grant: at 10:21, "the remnant shall return... unto the mighty God," 'ēl gibbôr unambiguously denotes God. Within Isaiah 1–12 the phrase has a fixed referent.

The Jewish reading takes that syntax and runs. (Note the numbering: what Christian Bibles print as 9:6–7 is 9:5–6 in the Hebrew.) Rashi makes God the subject of "and he called his name": the Holy One, who gives wondrous counsel, who is a mighty God and an everlasting Father, called Hezekiah's name Prince of Peace. The great titles describe the namer, not the child. The Talmud is bolder still, counting eight names in the verse and assigning them all to Hezekiah, matched against Sennacherib's eight, so the eight-named king may exact retribution from the eight-named king.

On the same folio sits the strangest passage in the Jewish reception of this chapter. Bar Kappara asked why the mem of l'marbeh in 9:6 is written closed, in its final form, in the middle of a word. Because, he answered, God sought to make Hezekiah the Messiah — and the attribute of justice objected: David sang many songs and was not made Messiah; shall Hezekiah, for whom all these miracles were done and who did not sing? So the mem was sealed. Redemption, in that story, was genuinely available in the eighth century BCE and was lost — not by decree, but because a righteous king failed to say thank you. That is a theology of history with real contingency in it, very different from a fixed schedule of fulfilled prophecy.

The knot the Restoration ties tighter

"The mighty God, The everlasting Father" is easy for a Trinitarian and hard for a Latter-day Saint, who holds the Father and the Son to be distinct embodied persons. Abinadi makes it harder, not easier: Christ is "the Father, because he was conceived by the power of God; and the Son, because of the flesh... And they are one God" (Mosiah 15:3–4). The official attempt to untie it is the 1916 exposition "The Father and the Son," which distinguishes Christ as Father in an "analogous" sense — Creator, and covenantal father of those reborn in him — from the Father of spirits. It is a clean solution, and it is visibly a solution: Abinadi's language runs further than the exposition's categories comfortably reach.

One variant here is more interesting than the famous one in chapter 2 and almost never gets mentioned. Isaiah 9:3 reads "Thou hast multiplied the nation, and not increased the joy," contradicting the rest of the verse, where the people rejoice like harvesters. 2 Nephi 19:3 drops the negative — and the Masoretic Text has a ketiv/qere at exactly that word, the written consonants meaning "not" and the reading tradition substituting "to it," with the Great Isaiah Scroll siding with the latter. The Book of Mormon here agrees with a Dead Sea scroll and the Jewish reading tradition against the King James translators.

The axe that boasts

Chapters 9:8–10:4 run four strophes, each ending in the same refrain — "For all this his anger is not turned away, but his hand is stretched out still" — a hammering structure that begins back at 5:25. Hold that refrain for chapter 12, where the first thing the redeemed say is "thine anger is turned away."

Then 10:5: "O Assyrian, the rod of mine anger." Read 10:8–14 beside the Assyrian royal inscriptions. "Is not Calno as Carchemish? is not Hamath as Arpad? is not Samaria as Damascus?" is a conquest list in annalistic style, naming cities Assyria actually took; "by the strength of my hand I have done it, and by my wisdom" is the annals' own register, reproduced accurately enough to be recognizable. Then 10:15 detonates it: "Shall the axe boast itself against him that heweth therewith?" The joke only works if the audience recognizes the voice being imitated.

The archaeology also supplies the horror. Sennacherib had the 701 siege of Lachish carved across a twenty-seven-meter relief program at Nineveh — siege ramp, impaled defenders, files of deportees — and his annals claim forty-six walled cities of Judah taken and Hezekiah shut up "like a bird in a cage," while conspicuously not claiming Jerusalem fell.

Which is where the chapter's theology becomes a problem rather than a comfort. Assyria is sent "to take the spoil, and to take the prey, and to tread them down like the mire of the streets" (10:6), then punished for the pride with which it did so (10:12). The text's own answer is the axe question. That is an answer about sovereignty. It is not an answer about the people under the axe.

Chapter 10 closes on the remnant — "the remnant shall return, even the remnant of Jacob" (10:21), Shear-jashub's name unpacked three chapters after he walked onstage as a silent child — and on the felling of the Assyrian forest, "and Lebanon shall fall by a mighty one" (10:34). That cut forest is what chapter 11 opens out of.

Think deeply.

  1. If 9:6 was first an accession oracle for a Judahite king, does calling it messianic add something the text can bear, or replace a meaning the text had? What would it take to say "both" without the word doing no work?
  2. Rashi reads the great titles as describing the God who names the child, not the child. The Hebrew permits it. What is your actual reason for preferring the other reading — the grammar, the New Testament, or the hymn?
  3. The Talmud says redemption was available in Hezekiah's lifetime and was forfeited because a rescued king did not sing. Could redemption really have arrived early and been lost? What does your answer commit you to about how God's timetable works?
  4. God uses an empire to punish Judah and then punishes the empire for what it did. The text answers with "shall the axe boast?" Is that a satisfying answer, an evasion, or a refusal to answer the question you were actually asking?

20–25 minSession 5: A Shoot from a Stump, and What You Say Afterward

Reading assignment: Isaiah 11, 12

The stump implies a felling

"And there shall come forth a rod out of the stem of Jesse, and a Branch shall grow out of his roots" (11:1). The Hebrew is nēṣer, a shoot, sprouting from the gezaʿ, the cut stump — a hopeful image built out of a catastrophic one, and the catastrophe it presumes had not happened in Isaiah's lifetime. The wordplay may run further: Matthew 2:23's "he shall be called a Nazarene" is often explained as a pun on nēṣer, though it works only in Hebrew and is contested.

What follows is the block's most familiar vision: judgment "not after the sight of his eyes," the wolf with the lamb, and "the earth shall be full of the knowledge of the LORD, as the waters cover the sea" (11:9). Maimonides handles that startlingly. "Do not presume that in the Messianic age any facet of the world's nature will change" — 11:6 is metaphor, Israel dwelling securely among nations likened to wolves who return to the truth and no longer plunder. He goes further: no difference between this age and the messianic era except freedom from subjugation, and the Messiah need not work miracles or raise the dead — the proof being that Rabbi Akiva backed Bar Kokhba and nobody asked him for a sign. The one verse he leaves entirely literal is 11:9.

Revealed commentary on this week's chapter

Doctrine and Covenants 113, given at Far West in March 1838, is the rarest kind of study aid: revealed commentary on a chapter of this week's reading. It makes a distinction the English of 11:1 does not force. The Stem of Jesse is Christ; the rod that comes out of the stem is not, but "a servant in the hands of Christ, who is partly a descendant of Jesse as well as of Ephraim," and the root of Jesse in 11:10 is another descendant, "unto whom rightly belongs the priesthood, and the keys of the kingdom." Isaiah's single messianic shoot is split into three referents, neither latter-day figure named. That is the multiple-fulfillment principle not as a reading strategy but as canon. Notice, too, what the revelation leaves alone: nothing on the peaceable kingdom, and nothing on 11:11–16, the second gathering — the verses preached most, and the basis of President Nelson's 2006 address on the gathering of Israel. That is ordinary apostolic application, not revealed gloss.

Those closing verses are also where the chapter's own dating pressure lives. Isaiah 11:11–16 promises a remnant recovered "the second time" from Assyria, Egypt, Pathros, Cush, Elam, Shinar, Hamath, and the islands of the sea. That is not an eighth-century horizon. They also give you the week's controlling image: the remnant travels a highway "like as it was to Israel in the day that he came up out of the land of Egypt" (11:16). The exodus is the template.

The question hanging over the whole book

State the authorship problem plainly here, because chapter 11 is where it stops being someone else's issue. Since Döderlein in 1775 and decisively since Duhm in 1892, critical scholarship has held that Isaiah is not the work of one eighth-century prophet: chapters 40–55 address an audience already in Babylonian exile and name Cyrus outright; 56–66 presuppose a return. The arguments are not arbitrary. Those chapters assume the standpoint of someone writing after 586 and before 540; fourteen verses of Isaiah 49 allude to at least fifteen passages in Lamentations, a book written in response to 586; and the Hebrew reflects a different stage of the language. That is not one argument; it is four independent ones converging.

The Latter-day Saint collision is that Lehi leaves Jerusalem around 600 BC and Nephi is soon quoting Isaiah 48–49 verbatim. But the honest news about this week is that the pressure is not here. Isaiah 1–12 is First Isaiah — the part essentially nobody dates after Lehi, and 2 Nephi 12–22 is the least contested stretch of the Book of Mormon's Isaiah. That is a real mitigation and should be said plainly. It is not a dissolution, though, because the same scholarship that leaves the core of these chapters in the eighth century holds that they were significantly edited afterward. The consensus is not that 1–12 is pristine eighth-century material; it is that its core is. Nephi has the edited version.

Stan Spencer's count in 2 Nephi 16–17, which is Isaiah 6–7, found that roughly 41 percent of the words the KJV sets in italics — the translators' supplied words, absent from the Hebrew — are missing or altered, against about 1 percent of the non-italicized words. Whatever mechanism one prefers, the correlation is not plausibly chance, and it ties the Book of Mormon Isaiah text to the printed page of the King James Bible, italics and all. The responses on offer are positions rather than slogans: reject the dating on the Book of Mormon's authority; contest the scholarship on linguistic ground; accept it and reconstruct what could have stood on the brass plates; or allow that God bypassed ordinary transmission. Serious, informed, believing people hold each.

What you say afterward

Chapter 12, finally, is a seam, and a small masterpiece of editorial craft. Two brief psalms, each opening "in that day," close the Immanuel material and pivot the book toward the oracles against the nations in chapters 13–23. And verse 2 is not original composition: "The LORD JEHOVAH is my strength and my song; he also is become my salvation" reproduces Exodus 15:2, the Song of the Sea. Set beside the highway of 11:16, the editor's point is unmissable — the deliverance just promised is the exodus again.

What the psalm models is worth more than its brevity suggests. "O LORD, I will praise thee: though thou wast angry with me, thine anger is turned away, and thou comfortedst me" (12:1). That is the refrain of chapters 5 through 10 finally released, and it does not pretend the anger did not happen. The praise includes it — a man who was told the truth about himself, survived it, and can say both halves in one sentence.

These six verses have an afterlife most Christian readers never encounter. Verses 2 and 3 open the Havdalah ceremony that closes every Sabbath, over a cup of wine in the dark: every Saturday night, Isaiah 12 is the first thing said. And "with joy shall ye draw water out of the wells of salvation" stands behind the Sukkot water-drawing at the Siloam pool.

And the practical shape of it is unglamorous. Drawing water is not a feeling. It is a bucket, lowered, repeatedly, because the household needs water again tomorrow. Cease something, learn something, plead for somebody, and do it again on Wednesday.

One last caution about the method the manual hands you. President Oaks's "multiple fulfillments" is a powerful tool and it is how the New Testament itself reads Isaiah. It is also unfalsifiable, and that should be said out loud once: a method permitting any passage to be fulfilled in the eighth century BC, the first century AD, and the present cannot produce a reading the evidence could overturn. Oaks names the safeguard explicitly — seeking revelation from the Holy Ghost. But a safeguard located entirely inside the reader is a different kind of safeguard than one located in the text, and pretending otherwise is how people get blindsided later. Use the method; know what kind of instrument you are holding.

And then the last word, which is a name. The noun in "God is my salvation" is yəshûʿāṯî, from the root y-sh-ʿ — the same root in the prophet's own name, Yəshaʿyāhû, YHWH is salvation. His sons carried sermons for names; so did he. The lesson title for this week is, in Hebrew, the prophet's own name turned into a confession.

Think deeply.

  1. D&C 113 splits Isaiah's one shoot into three referents and leaves the peaceable kingdom unglossed. When revelation reads scripture for you, does that close the passage or open it — and are you permitted to read 11:1 any other way afterward?
  2. Maimonides insists the messianic age changes nothing in nature and that the wolf and the lamb are metaphor. Is that a diminished hope or a more serious one? What in your own expectation of the Second Coming would survive his reading?
  3. The Book of Mormon's Isaiah tracks the King James text down to its italics. Hold the four standard responses in mind and answer for yourself: which do you actually believe, and what would have to be true for you to be wrong?
  4. "Though thou wast angry with me, thine anger is turned away." Can you say both halves of that sentence about something in your own life, or do you keep only one? Which half do you drop, and why that one?
