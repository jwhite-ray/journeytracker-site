Seek Ye Me, and Ye Shall Live: False Balances, a Gloating Brother, and a Prophet Who Resented Mercy

≈30 min · listen or read · the week's introduction — not one of the five sessions

Nathan and Eli open the week with Amos's merchants "making the ephah small, and the shekel great," then walk the oracles that close on Israel, "Seek ye me, and ye shall live," the day of the LORD as darkness, Amaziah at Bethel, the famine of hearing, Obadiah's gloating brother, and Jonah's flight, fish, and sulk. The panel's practical disciple joins them for the hard ground: the fish and history, a God who "repents," vengeance on Edom, and mercy for Nineveh.

This week's reading gathers three short books that sit side by side in the Bible. Amos was "among the herdmen of Tekoa," in Judah, but he preached in the northern kingdom of Israel "in the days of Uzziah king of Judah, and in the days of Jeroboam the son of Joash king of Israel, two years before the earthquake" (Amos 1:1). His nine chapters move from fire on the nations to a basket of summer fruit, a famine of hearing, and a fallen booth raised up again. Obadiah is a single chapter of twenty-one verses against Edom, the nation descended from Jacob's brother Esau, and it ends with "saviours" on mount Zion. Jonah is a story about a prophet who ran the other way, and it ends with a question from God that Jonah never answers. The canon ties the three together: the Jonah of the story is the Jonah son of Amittai who, in 2 Kings 14:25, foretold the expansion of Jeroboam II's kingdom, the very kingdom Amos condemned. Read critically, though, the three books cover roughly three centuries of Israelite history.

The panel reads these chapters from different places. The practical disciple says the books speak to ordinary weeks of buying, selling, and resenting, and puts the third book in one line: Jonah is about what happens when God is kinder than you wanted Him to be. The rabbi begins on the afternoon of Yom Kippur, when the synagogue chants the whole book of Jonah, and draws a rule from it: That habit is the key to all three books: Jewish tradition prayed them, argued with them, and aimed them at itself. The honest questioner lists a man inside a fish, a curse on a neighboring nation, and a God who "repents," and promises to give each problem at full strength. The academic scholar sets the books in the archaeology of a boom economy and the debates over how the books grew. The LDS scholar sets the scholarship beside the Restoration meanings of these books and insists that the first need not threaten the second. The doctrinal connector follows the verses into the Book of Mormon, the Joseph Smith Translation, and modern temple teaching, and promises to say plainly where a Restoration reading goes beyond what the verse meant in its own setting. And the six-outlooks essay takes Amos 8:11–12, two verses that have been read as a threat against eighth-century Samaria, a rabbinic warning that the Torah would be forgotten, a medieval description of exile, and a Latter-day Saint description of the Great Apostasy.

The lesson's title comes from Amos 5: "Seek ye me, and ye shall live" (5:4) and "Seek the LORD, and ye shall live" (5:6). The lesson finds the same hope in all three books: of all the "secrets" God reveals (Amos 3:7), "this one is among the most precious: that because of Jesus Christ, it's not too late to change." The books do not make that hope cheap. Amos is mostly doom, Obadiah is mostly vengeance, and the mercy in Jonah makes its own prophet "very angry" (Jonah 4:1). The question under all five sessions is what it means to seek the LORD rather than only his word, his shrines, or his judgments on other people.

Session 1 (Amos 1–3) meets a herdsman in a boom economy, hears fire fall on six nations and then on Judah and Israel, asks how the book was built, and wrestles with "he revealeth his secret unto his servants the prophets" and what it promises. Session 2 (Amos 4–6) hears the cows of Bashan, the five refrains of "yet have ye not returned unto me," the Talmud's one-commandment summary of the law, the God who hates feast days, and the woe on those "at ease in Zion." Session 3 (Amos 7–8) watches Amos argue with God and win, wrestles with a God who "repented," stands with the herdsman at the king's chapel, follows cheating merchants to the famine of hearing, and ends with a capstone that reads 8:11–12 six ways. Session 4 (Amos 9 and Obadiah 1) hears that Israel is no more God's than the Ethiopians, wrestles with the book's happy ending, then turns to Edom, the sin of standing on the other side, and the "saviours" of mount Zion. Session 5 (Jonah 1–4) sails with a fleeing prophet, wrestles with the fish and the genre, hears a five-word sermon that turns a city around, and sits with Jonah under the gourd as God asks his last question.

The questions at the end of each session are not a quiz. Most have more than one defensible answer, and several ask about your own week rather than the text.

25–30 minSession 1: The Lion Hath Roared

Reading assignment: Amos 1, 2, 3

A herdsman in a boom economy (1:1)

The early eighth century was a good time to be rich in the northern kingdom. With Aram reduced, Jeroboam II could push Israel's borders outward, after Assyrian pressure had weakened Damascus; 2 Kings 14:25 credits that success to a prophecy of "Jonah, the son of Amittai," the same Jonah whose book closes this week's reading. It was a prosperous, confident kingdom. Within a few decades of Amos's ministry, Assyria took Samaria and carried Israel away (2 Kings 17:5–6). His first hearers lived in the good years.

Archaeology shows the money. The Samaria ostraca are potsherds recording deliveries of "old wine" and "washed oil" to the capital from surrounding villages and estates. A 2020 handwriting study concluded they were most likely written by just two scribes in Samaria: a small, centralized palace bureaucracy. The excavations at Samaria also turned up hundreds of fragments of carved ivory, mostly furniture inlays in Phoenician and North Syrian styles. Their exact date and place of manufacture are debated, but they show the kind of luxury Amos has in mind with "beds of ivory" (6:4) and "the houses of ivory shall perish" (3:15).

The man who came north to denounce it all was "among the herdmen of Tekoa" (1:1). Hold the word "herdmen" until Session 3, where the panel argues about what kind of herdsman Amos was. For now, notice the date: "two years before the earthquake." Zechariah 14:5 still remembered "the earthquake in the days of Uzziah" centuries later. Steven A. Austin and colleagues, writing in International Geology Review in 2000, gathered destruction evidence from widely separated sites in Israel and Jordan and placed a major earthquake around 750 BC, supporting an eighth-century setting for the book's core. The academic scholar adds a caution and a suggestion. Austin is a young-earth creationist, and others treat the precise date and magnitude more cautiously, but a major mid-eighth-century quake is reasonably well supported. It may also explain why Amos's words were kept: a prophet who said the land would "tremble" (8:8) looked vindicated.

"For three transgressions... and for four" (1:2–2:5)

The book opens with a roar: "The LORD will roar from Zion, and utter his voice from Jerusalem" (1:2). Then come seven short oracles in the same pattern: "For three transgressions of Damascus, and for four, I will not turn away the punishment thereof." Read them in order and watch where they land. Damascus, because "they have threshed Gilead with threshing instruments of iron" (1:3). Gaza, for selling a whole population of captives "to deliver them up to Edom" (1:6). Tyre, for the same trade, when it "remembered not the brotherly covenant" (1:9). Edom, "because he did pursue his brother with the sword, and did cast off all pity" (1:11). Ammon, because "they have ripped up the women with child of Gilead, that they might enlarge their border" (1:13). Moab, because "he burned the bones of the king of Edom into lime" (2:1). These are war crimes by foreigners, and an Israelite audience could cheer each sentence. Then Judah, Amos's own homeland: "they have despised the law of the LORD" (2:4). And then the eighth oracle falls on the listeners themselves (2:6).

Keep Edom's charge in mind. Obadiah will build a whole book on it in Session 4.

Wrestle: a book built in layers

Did Amos write every word of these oracles? Few scholars think Amos wrote the book as we have it; the question is how much later material it contains. Hans Walter Wolff's Hermeneia commentary (1977) proposed six stages of growth; Jörg Jeremias also traces a book in which later Judean tradents reread a northern prophet's words in light of Samaria's fall in 722 BCE and what followed. The academic scholar lists the passages most often called additions. The Judah oracle is one, whose generic Deuteronomistic language ("despised the law of the LORD") lacks the concrete war crimes charged elsewhere. The Tyre and Edom oracles are another, shorter and formulaic, with charges against Edom that fit the sixth century better than the eighth. You will meet the other two candidates later: three hymn fragments that end "The LORD... is his name" (4:13; 5:8–9; 9:5–6), and the happy ending of 9:11–15.

On the other side, Shalom M. Paul's Hermeneia volume (1991) is the main defense of near-unity: he argues that the book is substantially Amos's own, with at most a few minor additions. Francis I. Andersen and David Noel Freedman's massive Anchor Bible commentary (1989) also leans toward reading the book as a coherent whole. The LDS scholar, discussing a verse some critics assign to a later hand, gives a Latter-day Saint way to hold the debate: That makes it no less scripture, but changes what kind of evidence it is. The Church's own Bible Dictionary sets the same posture. Its introduction says that many entries "draw on the work of Bible scholars and are subject to reevaluation," and that it is not "an official statement of Church doctrine."

"They sold the righteous for silver" (2:6–16)

"For three transgressions of Israel, and for four, I will not turn away the punishment thereof; because they sold the righteous for silver, and the poor for a pair of shoes" (2:6). Israel's crimes are not war crimes. They happen in courts, at markets, and at the altar. The charges in the text are specific. The complaints are fraud, debt slavery, and courts ("the gate," 5:10, 12, 15) captured by the rich.

Look at 2:8: "they lay themselves down upon clothes laid to pledge by every altar." The Law said a poor man's cloak taken as collateral had to be returned by sundown, because "it is his raiment for his skin: wherein shall he sleep?" (Exodus 22:26–27). The wealthy in Amos's Israel kept the cloaks and stretched out on them at worship. The question for us is not whether we hold anyone's cloak, but whether we press every advantage the rules allow against people who have little leverage: the late fee charged to someone we know is struggling, the deposit withheld on a technicality, the contractor paid late because we can.

How did the poor lose their land? How the system worked is disputed. One model, associated with Bernard Lang, describes "rent capitalism": an urban elite absorbing peasant land through loans and foreclosure, turning smallholders into tenants and debt-slaves. Critics argue that the model imports categories from much later economies; Walter Houston's Contending for Justice is a careful treatment of how prophetic critique relates to the actual economy. The archaeology confirms wealth concentrated in the capital, not the mechanics of dispossession.

Jewish commentators read the verse closely. On "they sold the righteous for silver, and the poor for a pair of shoes" (2:6), Rashi gives the plain sense. Judges take bribes and squeeze a poor farmer off the plot that lies between their fields so they can join them up (Rashi on Amos 2:6). Radak asks why exile came for this "fourth" sin when Israel had committed graver ones. His answer is that, as with the generation of the Flood, God drew the line at chamas, violence and robbery, all the more because it came from the judges. For Radak, Amos's social critique is not ethics set beside the covenant. Corruption in the courts is the sin that fills the measure. Midrash also hears an older crime in the verse. Joseph's brothers sold him for twenty pieces of silver and each took two pieces to buy shoes (Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer 38). On this reading Samaria is not inventing a new decadence. Israel is still selling its brother.

Then God reviews what he did for them: destroyed the Amorite, brought them from Egypt, "raised up of your sons for prophets" (2:9–11). Their answer: "ye gave the Nazarites wine to drink; and commanded the prophets, saying, Prophesy not" (2:12). Mark that verse. It sets up the famine of Session 3.

"You only have I known" (3:1–2)

Chapter 3 begins with the shortest summary of Israel's election in the Bible, and it turns at the last word: "You only have I known of all the families of the earth: therefore I will punish you for all your iniquities" (3:2). The academic scholar reads it plainly: the verse turns election into accountability.

The Talmud records sectarian opponents throwing this verse at Rav Safra: who raises his wrath against his beloved? When he cannot answer, Rabbi Abbahu supplies a parable. A man lends money to his friend and to his enemy. From the friend he collects little by little; from the enemy he collects all at once (Babylonian Talmud, Avodah Zarah 4a). Being known does not exempt Israel from the reckoning. It means Israel pays as it goes. You will meet this verse's twin, and its sharpest challenge, in Amos 9:7.

"Surely the Lord GOD will do nothing" (3:3–8)

Now the most quoted verse in the book. Read it in its chain. "Can two walk together, except they be agreed? Will a lion roar in the forest, when he hath no prey?... Can a bird fall in a snare upon the earth, where no gin is for him?... Shall a trumpet be blown in the city, and the people not be afraid? shall there be evil in a city, and the LORD hath not done it? Surely the Lord GOD will do nothing, but he revealeth his secret unto his servants the prophets. The lion hath roared, who will not fear? the Lord GOD hath spoken, who can but prophesy?" (3:3–8).

Amos 3:7 is nearly always quoted alone. In context it is the hinge of an argument. Each question names an effect that proves a cause. Verse 7 sits between them as the warrant: disaster is coming, it is not random, and it has been announced in advance. Amos is defending his right to say unwelcome things (3:9–11). The academic scholar draws the same line: In context the point is that the prophet cannot help speaking, not that prophets are a permanent institution. The lesson borrows the chain for its own purpose, asking what conclusions we can draw "when a prophet prophesies."

The council. The word rendered "secret" is Hebrew sod, which means a circle of confidants, a council, and by extension what is decided there. The doctrinal connector adds that the Brown-Driver-Briggs lexicon glosses it first as "council, counsel," and only secondarily as secret counsel or intimacy. Jeremiah uses the same word for the test of a true prophet: "who hath stood in the counsel of the Lord, and hath perceived and heard his word?" (Jeremiah 23:18). On this reading a prophet does more than receive information. He is someone who has been admitted to God's council and comes back out carrying its decisions. Behind both is the ancient image of the prophet who overhears the divine assembly. The LDS scholar points to the clearest picture of it: the heavenly assembly visible in Micaiah's vision of "the LORD sitting on his throne, and all the host of heaven standing by him" (1 Kings 22:19–23).

Latter-day Saint scholars have pressed this further. David E. Bokovoy, writing in the Journal of Biblical Literature, argued that the imperatives of Amos 3:13 ("Hear ye, and testify in the house of Jacob") are addressed to members of that council summoned as witnesses, so the council framing runs through the chapter and is not confined to verse 7. William J. Hamblin, in Interpreter, carried the theme into Restoration scripture: Lehi's "numberless concourses of angels," Abraham's "noble and great ones," and the temple endowment. The LDS scholar ranks the two: Bokovoy's argument is philological; Hamblin's is a theological synthesis built on such work, and deserves a different level of confidence. Still, the biblical idea of a prophet admitted to God's council is congenial to Restoration claims in a way it is not to most Protestant readings. The doctrinal connector makes the same comparison with Abraham 3:22–23, where God stands among the intelligences and says, "These I will make my rulers." That is not the same scene as Amos 3:7, but it shares the conviction that God governs through a council and brings human beings into it.

"But" or "until." Joseph Smith's revision of the Bible changes this verse in a small but telling way. JST Amos 3:7 reads, "Surely the Lord God will do nothing until he revealeth the secret unto his servants the prophets." The change from "but" to "until" turns a general statement into a sequence: revelation comes before the act. The KJV reads as an absolute (nothing without revelation); the JST reads as a sequence (revelation precedes action). The JST also changes verse 6 from "shall there be evil in a city, and the Lord hath not done it?" to "the Lord hath not known it?" That pulls back from making God the author of every calamity.

A later hand? First, many redaction critics, Hans Walter Wolff among them, regard 3:7 as a later prose insertion that interrupts the poetic chain and reflects a Deuteronomistic theology of "his servants the prophets." You saw above how the LDS scholar handles that: still scripture, a different kind of evidence.

In modern teaching. In April 1977 President Spencer W. Kimball said that with Joseph Smith "God again spoke to man, revealing anew 'his secret unto his servants the prophets.'" In October 1989, Elder James E. Faust quoted the verse to insist that continuing revelation "does not originate with the prophets; it comes from God," which Doctrine and Covenants 1:38 puts in the Lord's own voice: "whether by mine own voice or by the voice of my servants, it is the same." The lesson quotes Elder Ulisses Soares: "having prophets is a sign of God's love."

Wrestle: does 3:7 promise that prophets never err?

The honest questioner calls this probably the Old Testament verse Latter-day Saints quote most often about modern prophets. The difficulty comes when the verse is read as a broad guarantee. If God does nothing without revealing it to the prophets, then what about teachings and policies Church leaders have later reversed?

The Church's own materials give a qualified answer. The Doctrine and Covenants Student Manual quotes Joseph Smith: "A prophet was a prophet only when he was acting as such." Doctrine and Covenants 1:25 expects error: "inasmuch as they erred it might be made known." And in October 2013 President Dieter F. Uchtdorf said in general conference, "to be perfectly frank, there have been times when members or leaders in the Church have simply made mistakes" ("Come, Join with Us").

The book itself complicates the verse. Amos 7 complicates the verse from within the book. The revealing in 3:7 goes both ways. The prophet does not only receive the council's decisions; he argues with them, and twice he wins (7:1–6). Then a priest of the established sanctuary orders the true prophet home. Faithful readers find in 3:7 a promise that God will not leave his people without warning. Critics see a verse stretched past its setting. Both should notice that Amos himself was the outsider.

Notice that the JST's "until" fits the faithful reading well: it promises warning before action, not a guarantee about everything a prophet says. Session 3 will add the verse's shadow: if God can reveal, he can also stop revealing.

"Publish in the palaces" (3:9–15)

Amos now calls foreign witnesses, the palaces of Ashdod and Egypt, to "behold the great tumults" in Samaria, a city whose rulers "store up violence and robbery in their palaces" (3:9–10). The rescue Amos promises is grim: "As the shepherd taketh out of the mouth of the lion two legs, or a piece of an ear; so shall the children of Israel be taken out that dwell in Samaria in the corner of a bed" (3:12). What is rescued is two legs and a piece of an ear. Then come the witnesses Bokovoy sees in 3:13, the altars of Bethel, and the "houses of ivory" of 3:15, which the ivories of Samaria let you picture.

Think deeply.

  1. Amos lets his listeners cheer as fire falls on six neighbors, then turns to them. When have you enjoyed a sermon, a lesson, or a news story about other people's sins until it reached your own address? What did you do next?
  2. Rabbi Abbahu says Israel, being "known," pays as it goes. Do you experience your covenants more as privilege or as accountability? Where has God collected from you "little by little"?
  3. The cloaks of 2:8 were legal collateral, used legally, at the altar. What advantages do the rules allow you over someone with little leverage, and which of them would you be embarrassed to use in front of God?
  4. If a prophet is someone admitted to God's council, what does that change about how you listen to general conference? What does Amos's own outsider status add to the picture?
  5. "But" or "until": which reading of 3:7 better fits your experience of revelation in your own life, in a family, or in the Church?
  6. The honest questioner asks how 3:7 fits with teachings later reversed. How do you hold trust in prophets and the Church's own teaching that leaders can make mistakes? What helps you, and what has not?

25–30 minSession 2: Seek Ye Me, and Ye Shall Live

Reading assignment: Amos 4, 5, 6

The cows of Bashan (4:1–5)

"Hear this word, ye kine of Bashan, that are in the mountain of Samaria, which oppress the poor, which crush the needy, which say to their masters, Bring, and let us drink" (4:1). The "kine" are the well-fed women of Samaria. Notice what they actually do: they ask their husbands for another drink. Their oppression came through a demand for more, supplied by a system they never examined. Their end is described with hooks and fishhooks and breaches in the city wall (4:2–3).

Then Amos mimics a priest's call to worship and twists it: "Come to Beth-el, and transgress; at Gilgal multiply transgression; and bring your sacrifices every morning, and your tithes after three years... for this liketh you, O ye children of Israel" (4:4–5). The worship is generous and frequent. The problem, as the rest of these chapters will say, is what it sits beside.

"Yet have ye not returned unto me" (4:6–13)

Five times in six verses the same refrain closes a disaster: famine ("cleanness of teeth"), drought that fell on one city and not another, blight and locusts, pestilence and war, and an overthrow "as God overthrew Sodom and Gomorrah... yet have ye not returned unto me, saith the LORD" (4:6–11). The lesson asks what the Lord had hoped would happen after these trials, and adds that "your trials may not have been sent by God." Notice that Amos does not hedge in the same way. In these verses God says "I" each time: "I also have given you," "I have withholden the rain," "I have smitten you." The lesson and the prophet are doing different work. Amos is interpreting a nation's disasters as a summons; the lesson is helping an individual who should not assume every hardship is a punishment. Both agree on the purpose: trials as an opportunity "to turn to Him."

Then the climax: "Therefore thus will I do unto thee, O Israel: and because I will do this unto thee, prepare to meet thy God, O Israel" (4:12). Amos 4:12 belongs in a plan-of-salvation reading. In Amos the meeting is a judgment about to break on the nation. Amulek widens it to a whole life: "this life is the time for men to prepare to meet God" (Alma 34:32).

The chapter ends with a hymn: "he that formeth the mountains, and createth the wind... The LORD, The God of hosts, is his name" (4:13). This is the first of the three hymn fragments the academic scholar flagged in Session 1, each ending "The LORD... is his name," which interrupt their contexts like a liturgical refrain. Whatever their origin, in the book as it stands they do the same thing each time: right after a threat, they name the Creator who can carry it out.

A funeral song for a living nation (5:1–3)

Chapter 5 opens with a lament, the kind sung over the dead: "The virgin of Israel is fallen; she shall no more rise" (5:2). Amos sings it over a kingdom at the height of its prosperity. A city that sends out a thousand soldiers will get back a hundred (5:3).

"Seek ye me, and ye shall live" (5:4–15)

Into the funeral comes the invitation that names this week's lesson: "For thus saith the LORD unto the house of Israel, Seek ye me, and ye shall live: But seek not Beth-el, nor enter into Gilgal, and pass not to Beer-sheba" (5:4–5). And again: "Seek the LORD, and ye shall live" (5:6).

The Talmud makes this one verse a summary of the whole law. The lesson's title, "Seek ye me, and ye shall live" (5:4), stands at the end of a famous Talmudic chain. Moses gave 613 commandments, David reduced them to eleven (Psalm 15), Isaiah, Micah, and Isaiah again to fewer, and "Amos came and established the 613 mitzvot upon one... Seek Me and live." Then Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak objects that "Seek me" might just mean "Seek Me throughout the entire Torah." So the Talmud gives the final honor to Habakkuk 2:4, "the righteous shall live by his faith." The LDS scholar notes the same reversal: the same passage then hands the honor to Habakkuk (Babylonian Talmud, Makkot 24a). Christian readers know that verse from Paul (Romans 1:17); here it summarizes the commandments.

The rabbi draws the lesson from the objection. Notice where the Talmud refuses to go. You seek God through Torah, not apart from it. Amos makes the same move: "seek not Beth-el" (5:5). God is found in the gate, where the court sits (5:10, 12, 15).

Read the chapter with that in mind. Between the two "seek" verses and the third ("Seek good, and not evil, that ye may live," 5:14) come people "who turn judgment to wormwood" (5:7), a second hymn to the maker of "the seven stars and Orion" (5:8), and the gate. "They hate him that rebuketh in the gate, and they abhor him that speaketh uprightly" (5:10). The gate was where disputes were judged. Verse 12 explains why the honest voice there was hated: the powerful "take a bribe, and they turn aside the poor in the gate from their right."

The practical disciple brings the gate home. Most of us do not banish prophets, but we know how to make honest people stop talking. We get defensive when a spouse raises the same concern a third time; we stop asking the friend who tells us the truth; at work we reward good news and sideline whoever flags problems. A family can become "the king's chapel," where certain subjects are never raised. The remedy is in 5:15: "Hate the evil, and love the good, and establish judgment in the gate." Name one person whose criticism you have learned to avoid, ask them this week what you are not seeing, and don't argue.

Look at how 5:15 ends: "it may be that the LORD God of hosts will be gracious unto the remnant of Joseph." That "it may be" is the most hope Amos offers before his final chapter. Read without 9:11–15, Amos is a prophet of near-total doom, offering at most the thin "it may be" of 5:15. The lesson asks you to ponder 5:4 and 5:14–15 and how seeking the Lord "has brought you life." The verses themselves suggest where to look for an answer: not first in a sanctuary, but in the gate.

The day of the LORD is darkness (5:16–20)

Israel expected the "day of the LORD" to be its day of victory. Amos turns it inside out. "Woe unto you that desire the day of the LORD! to what end is it for you? the day of the LORD is darkness, and not light. As if a man did flee from a lion, and a bear met him; or went into the house, and leaned his hand on the wall, and a serpent bit him" (5:18–19). There is no safe room. Obadiah will pick up the same phrase: "the day of the LORD is near upon all the heathen" (Obadiah 1:15).

"I hate, I despise your feast days" (5:21–24)

"I hate, I despise your feast days, and I will not smell in your solemn assemblies. Though ye offer me burnt offerings and your meat offerings, I will not accept them... Take thou away from me the noise of thy songs... But let judgment run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream" (5:21–24).

The practical disciple calls 5:24 Amos's most quotable verse. The Bible Dictionary, as the LDS scholar reports, reads the passage this way: sacrifice offered "as substitutes for personal righteousness" loses its meaning (5:21–27). The rabbi speaks for a people who kept the feasts. So "I hate, I despise your feast days" (5:21) never read to Jews as a rejection of ritual. A people that kept those feasts for millennia heard a warning against worship cut loose from justice. The practical disciple, borrowing the same point from the Mishnah on Jonah, says that fasts, feasts, sabbaths, and sacrifices (Amos 5:21–23) are not condemned for being religious, but for being a substitute for changed behavior.

Wrestle: "Have ye offered unto me sacrifices... in the wilderness?" (5:25–27)

The next verse is harder than the softer readings admit. "Have ye offered unto me sacrifices and offerings in the wilderness forty years, O house of Israel?" (5:25). The question seems to expect the answer no, which contradicts the Torah's detailed wilderness sacrificial system. Perhaps Amos did not know the Priestly legislation, or did not treat it as foundational. The academic scholar also names what most readers do with the passage, and what that costs: Most scholars read the passage as rejecting worship that coexists with injustice, not worship as such, but the language is absolute and that softer reading is brought to it.

So the panel splits. The rabbi and the practical disciple read 5:21–25 as a warning against ritual without justice, and the rabbi can point to the people who actually kept the feasts as his evidence. The academic scholar agrees that this is the majority reading but insists it is a reading, brought to words that sound absolute. Verse 26 adds an image of idols carried in procession ("the tabernacle of your Moloch and Chiun your images"), and verse 27 ends in exile "beyond Damascus." In Session 3 you will see a Dead Sea community that read these very verses as a charter for itself.

"Woe to them that are at ease in Zion" (6:1–14)

"Woe to them that are at ease in Zion, and trust in the mountain of Samaria" (6:1). Read verses 4–6 as one picture: "That lie upon beds of ivory, and stretch themselves upon their couches, and eat the lambs out of the flock, and the calves out of the midst of the stall; That chant to the sound of the viol... That drink wine in bowls, and anoint themselves with the chief ointments: but they are not grieved for the affliction of Joseph."

The scene has a name. The "banquet of them that stretched themselves" (6:7) is in Hebrew the marzeaḥ, known from fourteenth-century BCE Ugarit as a recognized drinking association, linked variously with honoring the dead or a patron deity. Either way, Amos is condemning an established elite institution, with wine "in bowls" and "the chief ointments," whose members "are not grieved for the affliction of Joseph" (6:6).

The practical disciple reads the last clause as the whole charge. None of the listed items is a sin in itself. These people are comfortable and not grieved — the suffering of their own people simply does not register. Amos's test is not whether we are comfortable, but whether our comfort has made us unable to grieve. In a modern ward, being at ease might mean that our ward, family, and calling are all running smoothly and we have stopped noticing who is not fine. And a test you can take tonight: when did something happening to someone outside your household last actually cost you sleep, money, or time? If you cannot remember, Amos 6:6 is addressed to you.

The doctrinal connector follows the phrase into the Book of Mormon. "Woe to them that are at ease in Zion" (Amos 6:1) reappears in Nephi's prophecy of the last days: "wo be unto him that is at ease in Zion!" (2 Nephi 28:24), paired with the cry "All is well in Zion; yea, Zion prospereth" (28:21). Nephi makes that ease a latter-day danger for the covenant people themselves. Against the merchants Amos will condemn in chapter 8, King Benjamin asks, "are we not all beggars?" (Mosiah 4:19).

The rest of the chapter takes the party apart. The revelers "go captive with the first that go captive" (6:7). A plague house is so full of death that a survivor says, "Hold thy tongue: for we may not make mention of the name of the LORD" (6:10). And the nation's victory talk is mocked: "Ye which rejoice in a thing of nought, which say, Have we not taken to us horns by our own strength?" (6:13). As the academic scholar noted at the start of Session 1, that line seems to mock the victory talk of Jeroboam II's expansion. "But, behold, I will raise up against you a nation" (6:14).

Think deeply.

  1. Amos's five refrains say God sent disasters so Israel would return; the lesson says your trials "may not have been sent by God." How do you hold both? When has a hard season turned you toward God, whoever or whatever sent it?
  2. The Talmud tried to reduce all the commandments to "Seek me, and live," then worried it was too easy to misread. What would "seeking God through" your covenants look like this week, as opposed to seeking him apart from them?
  3. "Seek not Beth-el." Is there a holy place, program, or habit you have come to trust more than the God it points to?
  4. Who "rebuketh in the gate" in your life? How have you made it harder for that person to speak, and what would it take to ask them what you are not seeing?
  5. Amos says God hates feast days kept beside injustice. If he spoke this way about a ward, what would he name? What would he name in your own household?
  6. "Not grieved for the affliction of Joseph." Whose affliction are you comfortable not grieving over? What is one thing you could do about it before Sunday?

25–30 minSession 3: A Famine of Hearing

Reading assignment: Amos 7, 8

Locusts and fire: a prophet who intercedes (7:1–6)

Chapters 7–9 are built around five visions. In the first, God forms locusts just as the late crop is sprouting. Amos does not simply report it. "O Lord GOD, forgive, I beseech thee: by whom shall Jacob arise? for he is small. The LORD repented for this: It shall not be, saith the LORD" (7:2–3). In the second, a fire eats up "the great deep." Amos pleads again, and again "The LORD repented for this: This also shall not be, saith the Lord GOD" (7:5–6).

This is the scene the honest questioner pointed to in Session 1: the prophet who receives the council's decisions also argues with them, and twice he wins. Amos 7 is even bolder. A prophet prays, "O Lord GOD, forgive, I beseech thee: by whom shall Jacob arise? for he is small," and the divine plan changes. Some find that more beautiful than an unmoving God; others find it unsettling.

Wrestle: a God who "repents" (7:3, 6)

The King James wording is the problem. "The LORD repented" appears twice here, and again in Jonah: "God repented of the evil, that he had said that he would do unto them; and he did it not" (Jonah 3:10). Yet Numbers 23:19 insists that God is "not... the son of man, that he should repent." And 1 Samuel 15 manages both in one chapter: "the Strength of Israel will not lie nor repent" (15:29), and then "the LORD repented that he had made Saul king" (15:35).

The Joseph Smith Translation. Joseph Smith revised all three, and the exact wording matters. The doctrinal connector gives the readings from Kent P. Jackson's edition, matched by the Church's footnotes:

  • JST Amos 7:3: "And the Lord said concerning Jacob, 'Jacob shall repent for this. Therefore I will not utterly destroy him, saith the Lord.'"
  • JST Amos 7:6: "And the Lord said concerning Jacob, 'Jacob shall repent of his wickedness. Therefore I will not utterly destroy him, saith the Lord God.'"
  • JST Jonah 3:9–10, which you will meet in Session 5, makes the Ninevites the ones who repented.

Every time, the repenting moves from God to the people; God's change of course becomes His answer to human change. That fits the Book of Mormon's insistence that God "changeth not; if so he would cease to be God" (Mormon 9:19), and the Church's footnote at Amos 7:3 cross-references Numbers 23:19: God is not "the son of man, that he should repent." The LDS scholar sees the same thing: The pattern is consistent and reflects a concern about divine changeability. For many Latter-day Saints that settles it: the KJV wording was a problem of translation or theology, and the Prophet fixed it.

The Hebrew. But the Hebrew verb is not the English one. The Hebrew verb, though, is nicham. It means to relent, to be moved to pity, or to change one's course. It does not mean to confess a sin. The doctrinal connector draws a careful conclusion from that: The JST heads off a misreading of the English "repented" more than it corrects the Hebrew. He adds a second caution: the revision is not applied everywhere. The Church's edition has no JST note at Jonah 4:2, where Jonah complains that God "repentest thee of the evil," though the JST does revise the same formula in Joel 2:13. Readers should treat the JST as Joseph Smith's inspired working-through of particular passages, not as a mechanical global edit.

What remains. The JST and the Hebrew point in different theological directions, and a careful reader can hold both without pretending they say the same thing. The Hebrew gives you a God moved by a prophet's prayer; the JST gives you a God whose mercy answers human change. Both keep the prophet's intercession at the center: in each version Amos prays and Jacob is spared. And the JST's "I will not utterly destroy him" also echoes Amos 9:8, "saving that I will not utterly destroy the house of Jacob," tying the visions to the book's closing promise of sifting and gathering.

The plumbline (7:7–9)

The third vision has no intercession. "Behold, the Lord stood upon a wall made by a plumbline, with a plumbline in his hand... Behold, I will set a plumbline in the midst of my people Israel: I will not again pass by them any more" (7:7–8). The lesson's Scripture Helps asks what a plumbline is; in the vision it is the Lord's measure set "in the midst of my people," and this time Amos does not pray. The sentence names the shrines and the dynasty: "I will rise against the house of Jeroboam with the sword" (7:9).

The herdman at the king's chapel (7:10–17)

That last line provokes the book's only story. Amaziah, the priest of Bethel, reports Amos to the king: "Amos hath conspired against thee in the midst of the house of Israel: the land is not able to bear all his words" (7:10). Then he speaks to Amos: "O thou seer, go, flee thee away into the land of Judah, and there eat bread, and prophesy there: But prophesy not again any more at Beth-el: for it is the king's chapel, and it is the king's court" (7:12–13).

Amos answers: "I was no prophet, neither was I a prophet's son; but I was an herdman, and a gatherer of sycomore fruit: And the LORD took me as I followed the flock, and the LORD said unto me, Go, prophesy unto my people Israel" (7:14–15).

"No prophet." The Hebrew sentence has no verb, so it can be read as past ("I was not") or present ("I am not"). Read as present, Amos refuses the title navi, professional prophet; read as past, he simply had no prophetic background before the call. "Prophet's son" most likely means a guild member, like the "sons of the prophets" in the Elisha stories. Either way, his authority rests on a call.

What kind of herdsman? The word for "herdmen" in 1:1 is noqed. The word for "herdmen" in 1:1 is noqed, the same word used for Mesha king of Moab, who paid Israel a huge annual tribute in sheep (2 Kings 3:4). Amos may have been a livestock owner of some standing rather than a poor shepherd. The sycamore fig, the academic scholar adds, grows in low, warm country, not around Tekoa, so "a gatherer of sycomore fruit" suggests seasonal work or holdings elsewhere. The Talmud reached the same conclusion centuries earlier through the Aramaic Targum, which renders Amos's answer: "Because I am the owner of flocks and I have sycamores in the lowland, and I do not come to prophesy for financial gain." From this Rabbi Yochanan concludes that Amos, like Jonah, was wealthy (Babylonian Talmud, Nedarim 38a). Amaziah's "there eat bread" meant, in effect, go earn your living elsewhere. The point: a prophet who needs no wages cannot be dismissed from the king's chapel. Either way, his point is that his authority does not come from any institution.

"O thou seer." Amaziah calls Amos a "seer" as a dismissal. The doctrinal connector sets it beside King Limhi and Ammon in the Book of Mormon, who give the word its full weight: "a seer is greater than a prophet," one through whom "secret things" are "made manifest" (Mosiah 8:15–17). Amaziah meant an insult and named the office. The connector also hears Amaziah's complaint in Samuel the Lamanite, an outsider on the wall as Amos was a Judean in Bethel, who says the people cast out the prophet who testifies of their sins and receive the one who says "Walk after the pride of your own hearts" (Helaman 13:26–27). Amaziah's complaint, "the land is not able to bear all his words" (Amos 7:10), is exactly that reaction.

Institution and prophet. Here two Latter-day Saint voices say something hard about their own reading. The lesson asks what we learn about prophets from 7:10–15, and the obvious answer is that God calls them. Latter-day Saints rightly use this passage to affirm that God calls prophets. Honesty also requires noticing whom Amos stands against: the authorized priest of an established sanctuary, backed by the king. The Restoration reads Amos 3:7 to support a prophetic office within an institution; Amos himself was the outsider rebuking one. The passage resists any reading in which institutional standing alone settles who speaks for God. The honest questioner, as you saw, makes the same point. Either way, the book that gives us 3:7 also shows institutional religion and prophetic speech on opposite sides.

The practical disciple turns it toward ordinary life. Amaziah's response to Amos in 7:10–13 is the same instinct in religious dress as the people who "hate him that rebuketh in the gate" (5:10). Amos's last word to Amaziah is a sentence on him and his family (7:16–17), and it begins by quoting him back: "Thou sayest, Prophesy not against Israel."

"The end is come" (8:1–3)

The fourth vision is a pun. "Behold a basket of summer fruit... Then said the LORD unto me, The end is come upon my people of Israel" (8:1–2). In the fourth vision a basket of summer fruit (qayits) yields the word "end" (qets): "The end is come upon my people of Israel" (8:1–2). The songs of the temple turn to howling, and the dead are cast out "with silence" (8:3).

Short measures (8:4–10)

Then the merchants. "Hear this, O ye that swallow up the needy... Saying, When will the new moon be gone, that we may sell corn? and the sabbath, that we may set forth wheat, making the ephah small, and the shekel great, and falsifying the balances by deceit? That we may buy the poor for silver, and the needy for a pair of shoes; yea, and sell the refuse of the wheat?" (8:4–6).

Notice the order. These merchants are keeping the sabbath. Their worship is intact on the outside. And the business they return to is rigged three ways: the ephah (the measure of grain the customer receives) is made small, the shekel (the weight of silver the customer pays) is made great, and the scale itself is crooked. On top of that they sell the sweepings off the threshing floor passed off as grain. The Law of Moses had already forbidden exactly this (Leviticus 19:35–36). Amos is not inventing a new standard. He is saying that keeping the sabbath while cheating on Monday is not keeping the covenant at all. The six-outlooks essay puts it in five words: Sacred time was an interruption to trade.

The modern versions are quieter. The modern equivalents are quieter than a weighted scale: billing an hour that was really forty minutes; describing a used car or rental unit in language technically true and designed to mislead; padding an expense report; judging a subordinate's work harshly and our own generously — a small ephah for them, a great shekel for us. A self-examination this week: pick one transaction you will make in your job or household and ask whether you would be comfortable if the other party could see everything you know about it.

God's answer is an oath: "Surely I will never forget any of their works" (8:7). The land will "tremble" (8:8), the sun will "go down at noon" (8:9), and feasts will become mourning "as the mourning of an only son" (8:10).

Wrestle: a famine for whom? (8:11–14)

"Behold, the days come, saith the Lord GOD, that I will send a famine in the land, not a famine of bread, nor a thirst for water, but of hearing the words of the LORD: And they shall wander from sea to sea, and from the north even to the east, they shall run to and fro to seek the word of the LORD, and shall not find it" (8:11–12).

The Restoration reading. The lesson applies the verses to the Great Apostasy, and its children's section is titled "The Restoration of the gospel ended the famine of apostasy." In October 1997, Elder Jeffrey R. Holland declared that "God has through His Only Begotten Son lifted the famine of which Amos spoke," and then testified directly of the First Vision.

The setting. Every essay that treats these verses points first to their place in the book. That application is legitimate, but it lands harder once the verse's own setting is clear. In Amos the famine is not random. It is the logical end of a choice Israel has already made twice. In 2:12 Israel "commanded the prophets, saying, Prophesy not." In 7:13 Amaziah said it to Amos's face. The famine of 8:11 gives Israel what it asked for. A people who told the prophets to be quiet get silence. The LDS scholar puts it the same way: The punishment fits: a people who silence the word will one day "run to and fro to seek the word of the LORD, and shall not find it" (8:12). And it follows the merchants. In its own setting it follows directly on merchants who cannot wait for the sabbath to end so they can cheat with short measures, buying "the poor for silver, and the needy for a pair of shoes" (8:4–6).

The critique. The lesson's "also" is careful. The lesson says the verses "could also apply" to the Great Apostasy. The famine is a consequence of rejecting a voice that was speaking, not a long silence spread over the centuries. Critics say that using the verse as a proof-text for a world-wide apostasy lasting more than a thousand years removes it from its context. The academic scholar reads it as a judgment on eighth-century Israel only: the people who told the prophets "Prophesy not" (2:12) will one day look for an oracle and find none.

The answers. Defenders answer that scripture often applies a historical pattern to later times. The New Testament does this with Amos itself, using Amos 9:11–12 for the Gentile mission in Acts 15:16–17. The pattern does hold: people who silence prophets end up without them. The LDS scholar agrees and finds the fit unusually good: The first referent is the northern kingdom's near future; the apostasy reading applies the pattern rather than the original prediction. But the text supports that typology well: in Amos, absence of revelation is the consequence of refusing prophets, which is exactly the structure of the Restoration narrative of apostasy. Still, the honest questioner insists on one thing: But a reader who meets the verse only as an apostasy proof should know that Amos was speaking to people who sold "the poor for a pair of shoes" (2:6; 8:6), and the famine came because of that.

A Jewish reading. Jewish tradition aimed the same verses at itself. A baraita in the Talmud says that when the sages gathered at Yavneh after the Temple fell, they quoted these verses and said, "The Torah is destined to be forgotten from the Jewish people." Then Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai protests, citing Deuteronomy 31:21, that it "shall not be forgotten out of the mouths of their seed." So what does "shall not find it" mean? They will not find clear law and clear teaching in one place (Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 138b–139a). In his reading the famine is not the absence of revelation. It is revelation scattered into dispute, and the remedy is not a new prophet but the study house, the long labor of gathering what was dispersed. That is a real disagreement with the Restoration reading about the remedy, and a striking agreement about the danger.

The chapter's last verses name the shrines the seekers will run to: "They that swear by the sin of Samaria, and say, Thy god, O Dan, liveth; and, The manner of Beer-sheba liveth; even they shall fall, and never rise up again" (8:14).

Capstone: "A famine of hearing," six ways (8:11–12)

Two verses, fewer than forty words in Hebrew, and they have been read as a threat against eighth-century Samaria, a rabbinic warning that the Torah would be forgotten, a medieval description of exile, and a Latter-day Saint description of the Great Apostasy. That density is why they suit the method this essay borrows. Donald W. Parry, a professor of Hebrew Bible at Brigham Young University, sets out six ways of reading in a chapter titled "Ways to Read Isaiah: Six Outlooks": the near view of historical context, the distant view of prophecy, dual or multiple fulfillment, likening, personal reading, and reading for doctrine. He does not rank them. His one stated priority is a sequence: begin with the near view. What follows applies his framework to Amos; it is not his reading of Amos, and the comparison to Qumran pesher in the fourth section is mine, not his.

1. The near view. Read in that setting, the famine of 8:11 is not a general observation about spiritual hunger. It is a sentence passed on a specific people for a specific refusal, and the book has been building the case. You have just traced that case from 2:12 to 7:13 to the merchants of 8:5. The famine is the answer that fits the crime. Verse 14 adds the geography. Dan was at the northern edge of the land and Beersheba at the southern. And 5:5 had already said "pass not to Beer-sheba." The picture in 8:12 is of pilgrims running among busy shrines and finding no word from the LORD at any of them.

Two details of the Hebrew sharpen it. First, the famine is not of the word but "of hearing" (lishmoaʻ) the words of the LORD. The text does not say God's word will cease to exist; it says the people will lose the ability to hear it. Second, the verb for "seek" in 8:12 (levaqqesh) differs from the one in the great invitation of 5:4, "Seek ye me" (dirshuni, from darash). The verbs overlap in meaning, so the contrast should not be pressed too hard. But the objects differ, and that difference is real: in chapter 5 the people are told to seek the LORD; in chapter 8 they seek the word of the LORD and do not find it.

2. The distant view. The text invites it with its opening formula, "Behold, the days come," which in the prophets often marks an indefinite future, and Latter-day Saint teaching has read these verses as reaching well past 722 BC. The Church's Guide to the Scriptures, in its entry "Apostasy," lists Amos 8:11 under the subheading "Apostasy of the early Christian church". In January 1973 Elder Howard W. Hunter, then of the Quorum of the Twelve, published "Spiritual Famine" in the Ensign. He treated the prophecy as describing modern religious confusion and wrote, "Certainly we live in a day of famine, as described by Amos." Then the essay notices the lesson's own wording: "could also apply." The Church's own lesson does not say the verses were only about the Apostasy. It adds a distant reading to the near one.

3. Multiple fulfillment. Parry notes that multiple fulfillment has been called the "layer-cake theory" of prophecy. Amos 8:11–12 may be the clearest case in this week's reading, because readers in different centuries kept finding the famine in their own time. The layers run from before Amos to long after him. Before Amos, 1 Samuel 3:1 says that in Eli's day "there was no open vision." After him the pattern returns: Lamentations 2:9 after 586 BC ("her prophets also find no vision from the LORD"), Psalm 74:9 ("there is no more any prophet"), 1 Maccabees 9:27, the Talmud's teaching that prophecy departed after Haggai, Zechariah, and Malachi (Yoma 9b), the sages at Yavneh, and Radak in the Middle Ages. The Talmud then glosses "the word of the LORD" three ways: as halakha (law), as "the end" (the timing of redemption), and as prophecy. That is itself a multiple-fulfillment reading of a single phrase. Radak, citing ibn Ezra, reasons that if the words of prophecy are not found in the land of Israel, still less will they be found outside it "in the days of exile." The Latter-day Saint reading of the verses as the Great Apostasy belongs on this list as one more layer, not a replacement for the others. The passage describes a pattern, the withdrawal of the word from a people who say "prophesy not," and patterns recur.

Notice that here the six-outlooks essay answers the honest questioner's critique. A layered reading keeps the apostasy application and the eighth-century setting together, and the near view sets the test for any later layer, as the essay says at its close.

4. Likening. Parry's fourth outlook takes its key word from Nephi, who "did liken all scriptures unto us, that it might be for our profit and learning" (1 Nephi 19:23). The essay compares an ancient Jewish form of the same instinct, the Dead Sea Scroll commentaries called pesharim. The Habakkuk commentary claims that God made known to the community's Teacher of Righteousness "all the mysteries of the words of His servants the prophets" (1QpHab VII, trans. G. Vermes, rev. R. A. Kraft). The language sits close to Amos 3:7, "his secret unto his servants the prophets." And remember the verses from Session 2: The Damascus Document quotes Amos 5:26–27 and 9:11 and declares, "The books of the Law are the Tent of the King" (CD VII, trans. Solomon Schechter), turning Amos's exile oracle into a charter for its own community. Pesher claims the prophet was really talking about us. Nephi applies the text "for our profit and learning" without denying it a first audience. Liken too little and Amos stays in Samaria; liken like a pesher and Amos loses Samaria.

Likened carefully, the verses ask something uncomfortable of people who carry the scriptures on their phones. Amos's famine was not a shortage of shrines, priests, or festivals; Bethel was busy. The shortage was of hearing. A household can have scripture in every room and still be in famine by that definition. The lesson asks, "How do you know when you are spiritually hungry?" Amos adds a harder question: how would you know if you had stopped being hungry, and had settled, like the merchants of 8:5, into waiting for the sabbath to be over?

5. A personal reading. Parry supports this outlook with Elder Dallin H. Oaks's 1995 Ensign article "Scripture Reading and Revelation," which says that scripture reading "may also lead to current revelation on whatever else the Lord wishes to communicate." That last phrase is the key to this outlook, and it is why the fifth lens differs from the fourth. Likening still asks what the passage means for us. A personal reading may land somewhere the passage was never pointed. One reader may stop on "run to and fro" and recognize a restless habit of searching that never sits still long enough to hear anything. Neither is exegesis, and neither should be defended as what Amos meant. This outlook is honest only if it says so.

6. Reading for doctrine. The essay draws three doctrines. Revelation is God's to give, and to withhold. "I will send a famine." Amos 3:7 is much quoted in Latter-day Saint teaching; Amos 8:11 is its shadow side, and the two should be read together. Hearing is conditional. Alma teaches the same principle: "he that will harden his heart, the same receiveth the lesser portion of the word" (Alma 12:10), and Nephi warns that from those who say "We have enough... shall be taken away even that which they have" (2 Nephi 28:30). Amos describes the end state of that process at the scale of a whole nation. The object of the search matters. It is that hunting for a word while avoiding the One who speaks it ends in famine, however many sanctuaries one visits. The phrase this week's lesson takes for its title is the cure: "Seek the LORD, and ye shall live" (5:6).

Why keep the six apart. Done in Parry's order, the lenses check one another. The near view keeps the distant view honest: Amos addressed people who silenced prophets, so any later fulfillment should look like a people who stopped listening, not merely an era that lacked records. The layer cake explains how the Talmud, Radak, and Elder Hunter could all be right at once. Doctrine gathers what holds across every layer: God speaks; people can make themselves unable to hear; and the remedy is to seek Him, not only His word.

Think deeply.

  1. Amos argued with God twice and won, and the third time he did not pray. When have you pleaded for someone? When have you stopped pleading, and why?
  2. The Hebrew says God "relented"; the JST says Jacob will repent. Which picture of God helps you pray? Can you hold both, as the honest questioner suggests?
  3. Amaziah was an authorized priest at an established sanctuary, and Amos was an outsider. How do you tell the difference between faithful loyalty to an institution and the instinct that says "prophesy not again any more at Beth-el"?
  4. The merchants kept the sabbath and cheated on Monday. Pick one transaction you will make this week. Would you be comfortable if the other party could see everything you know about it?
  5. The famine in Amos is "of hearing," not of words. When have you had scripture all around you and heard nothing? What changed when you heard again?
  6. Of Parry's six outlooks, which have you used most with 8:11–12, and which least? What does the near view add to the Restoration reading you already knew, and what does the Restoration reading add to the near view?

25–30 minSession 4: The Fallen Booth and the Mount of Esau

Reading assignment: Amos 9 · Obadiah 1

Nowhere to run (Amos 9:1–6)

The fifth vision has no pun and no plea. "I saw the Lord standing upon the altar: and he said, Smite the lintel of the door, that the posts may shake... he that fleeth of them shall not flee away, and he that escapeth of them shall not be delivered" (9:1). Then the most complete escape list in the prophets: "Though they dig into hell, thence shall mine hand take them; though they climb up to heaven, thence will I bring them down" (9:2), and on to the top of Carmel, the bottom of the sea, and captivity itself (9:3–4). The third hymn fragment follows: "The LORD is his name" (9:6).

Keep 9:2 in mind for Session 5. The early rabbis read it as the reason Jonah could not have been trying to escape God at all.

"Are ye not as children of the Ethiopians unto me?" (9:7–10)

"Are ye not as children of the Ethiopians unto me, O children of Israel? saith the LORD. Have not I brought up Israel out of the land of Egypt? and the Philistines from Caphtor, and the Syrians from Kir?" (9:7).

Amos 9:7 cuts deeper still than 3:2. The academic scholar says it goes further and makes the exodus one migration among several that YHWH directed. The Philistines and the Syrians, two of the nations judged in chapter 1, had their own exoduses, and the same God led them. Set the two verses side by side. Together they redefine Israel's special status as responsibility before a universal deity. The LDS scholar: Set beside "You only have I known of all the families of the earth: therefore I will punish you" (3:2), it defines chosenness as accountability rather than privilege. The Bible Dictionary summarizes Amos the same way: covenant status makes Israel's punishment "the heavier" (citing 3:2 and 9:7).

The rabbi shows how hard the verse was to carry. The Talmud softened the verse into a compliment: "just as a Cushite is distinguished by his dark skin, so too, the Jewish people are distinguished by their actions" (Babylonian Talmud, Moed Katan 16b). A fine homily, but it reverses Amos, who says the Exodus is not unique. Read 3:2 and 9:7 together and election means this: Israel is no more God's creature than Cush, but it is bound by covenant, and so more answerable. We have carried both verses ever since without fully reconciling them.

The doctrinal connector finds both ideas in the Book of Mormon: "Know ye not that there are more nations than one?... I remember one nation like unto another" (2 Nephi 29:7–8), and the Lord grants "unto all nations, of their own nation and tongue, to teach his word" (Alma 29:8). In the Restoration, chosenness is a calling to carry something to the others, not a claim to be loved more. That is the issue at the center of Jonah.

Then the sieve: "I will sift the house of Israel among all nations, like as corn is sifted in a sieve, yet shall not the least grain fall upon the earth" (9:9). Verse 8 has already promised "saving that I will not utterly destroy the house of Jacob." The judgment is real, but it is a sifting, not an erasure.

"The tabernacle of David that is fallen" (9:11–15)

Then, without warning, light. "In that day will I raise up the tabernacle of David that is fallen, and close up the breaches thereof; and I will raise up his ruins, and I will build it as in the days of old: That they may possess the remnant of Edom, and of all the heathen, which are called by my name" (9:11–12). Plowmen overtake reapers, the mountains drip with sweet wine, and Israel is planted "and they shall no more be pulled up out of their land" (9:13–15).

A booth. The Hebrew is sukkat David ha-nofelet, David's booth, the flimsy harvest hut. Jews pray this verse in their own booths. Every Sukkot, sitting in our own booths, we add to the grace after meals: "May the Merciful One raise up for us the fallen booth of David" (Siddur Ashkenaz, Birkat HaMazon). The Talmud even names the Messiah bar nafli, "son of the fallen," from this verse (Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 96b–97a).

Wrestle: was the happy ending added? (9:11–15)

The earlier chapters are relentless, and then this. Many scholars think a later hand wrote it. The academic scholar's list of likely additions ends with these verses, which promises to "raise up the tabernacle of David that is fallen" and so assumes the Davidic house has already fallen, most naturally after 586 BCE. The last matters most. Whether the happy ending is a later editor's softening or the prophet's own last word is a central question in the study of Amos.

The LDS scholar lays out both sides. Wolff and many critical scholars assign these verses to a post-exilic hand, partly because a "fallen" booth of David seems to presuppose the end of the Davidic monarchy in 586. Shalom M. Paul (Hermeneia) and Francis I. Andersen and David Noel Freedman (Anchor Bible) are more inclined to read the book as a coherent whole and to keep more of it with Amos. The question is open.

The six-outlooks essay offers a way to read the book either way. Some scholars treat 9:11–15 as a later addition. Taken as the book stands, though, the famine is a judgment with an "in that day" on the far side of it. The Latter-day Saint reading, in which the Restoration ends the famine of apostasy, follows that same arc. It differs in where it places the "in that day."

Edom or Adam? James reads Amos (9:12)

Notice the phrase "possess the remnant of Edom." The New Testament quotes it differently. At the Jerusalem council James quotes Amos 9:11–12 to justify admitting Gentiles (Acts 15:16–17), but in a Greek form: where the Hebrew says Israel will "possess the remnant of Edom," the Greek reads "that the residue of men might seek after the Lord." The difference rests on two small consonantal variants (ʼadam, "humankind," for ʼedom; "seek" for "possess"). The apostles found the Gentile mission in the Greek Bible they had. The rabbi notices the same swap. James quotes the passage at the Jerusalem council (Acts 15:16–17) from a Greek text that reads "the residue of men" where the Hebrew of 9:12 has "the remnant of Edom." And he draws a different lesson from it: That brings us to Edom, a word that carries a weight most Christian readers never feel.

The LDS scholar sees a Latter-day Saint category for what James did. Latter-day Saints, who read the Book of Mormon's practice of "likening" scripture (1 Nephi 19:23) as a legitimate method, have a ready category for this kind of inspired re-reading, and should recognize their own uses of Amos as the same kind of act. That is the six-outlooks essay's point from Session 3, made by a scholar from the other direction. And it carries you straight into Obadiah, a whole book about the remnant of Edom.

Obadiah: who, and when? (Obadiah 1:1–9)

Obadiah, the shortest book in the Hebrew Bible, carries no date, but its content points to one. Edom stood aloof "in the day that the strangers carried away captive his forces... and cast lots upon Jerusalem" (v. 11). Most scholars connect this to the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BCE. The same accusation appears in Psalm 137:7, Lamentations 4:21–22, and Ezekiel 25:12–14 and 35. The Bible Dictionary, the LDS scholar notes, lists two possible occasions: a raid under Jehoram (848–844 BC) or, "more probably," the Babylonian conquest of 586. Verses 1–9 also overlap heavily with Jeremiah 49:7–22. Who borrowed from whom, or whether both drew on a common stock of anti-Edom oracles, is unresolved.

The rabbis had their own answer to "who." The rabbis asked why this one prophecy, and no other, came to Obadiah. Rabbi Yitzchak answered that God chose Obadiah, who lived among two wicked people, Ahab and Jezebel, and "did not learn from their actions," to prophesy against Esau, who lived among two righteous people, Isaac and Rebekah, and did not learn from them either. Then, in Rabbi Meir's name: "Obadiah was an Edomite convert," as the proverb says, "From and within the forest comes the ax to it" (Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 39b; Rashi on Obadiah 1:1). That changes the book. The harshest oracle against Edom comes from a man born Edomite who chose Israel.

The first nine verses mock Edom's pride: "thou that dwellest in the clefts of the rock... Though thou exalt thyself as the eagle, and though thou set thy nest among the stars, thence will I bring thee down" (vv. 3–4). Thieves leave something; grape-gatherers leave gleanings; Edom will be stripped bare (vv. 5–6). Its allies will betray it (v. 7).

Standing on the other side (Obadiah 1:10–14)

"For thy violence against thy brother Jacob shame shall cover thee" (v. 10). Edom is Esau, Jacob's twin. Then the heart of the book: "In the day that thou stoodest on the other side, in the day that the strangers carried away captive his forces, and foreigners entered into his gates, and cast lots upon Jerusalem, even thou wast as one of them. But thou shouldest not have looked on the day of thy brother in the day that he became a stranger; neither shouldest thou have rejoiced over the children of Judah in the day of their destruction" (vv. 11–12).

The practical disciple calls verses 11–14 one of the most precise descriptions of a common sin anywhere in scripture. Edom's crime was not that it attacked Jerusalem. It was that it watched, enjoyed, and then helped itself. The list moves in stages. Standing aloof. Looking on. Rejoicing. Speaking proudly. Entering the gate and taking goods (v. 13). Finally standing "in the crossway, to cut off those of his that did escape" and handing over the survivors (v. 14). Gloating is not presented as a private feeling that harms no one; it is the first step on a slope that ends in looting. Amos had already named Edom's sin in 1:11: he "did pursue his brother with the sword, and did cast off all pity."

It shows up in the small satisfaction when the coworker who got our promotion stumbles publicly, when a relative who criticized our parenting has a child go off the rails, or when a political opponent is caught in scandal. The "I told you so," the forwarded screenshot, the conversation that begins "Did you hear about...?" — these are verse 12 behavior. Taking advantage of someone's crisis to settle an old score or improve our own position is verse 13.

The rabbi notes that this, not ancestry, is what condemns Edom in the book: What condemns Edom is what verses 12–14 list: standing by, gloating, looting, cutting off refugees at the crossroads.

Wrestle: revenge against a brother? (Obadiah 1:15–18)

"For the day of the LORD is near upon all the heathen: as thou hast done, it shall be done unto thee: thy reward shall return upon thine own head" (v. 15). Then: "the house of Jacob shall be a fire, and the house of Joseph a flame, and the house of Esau for stubble... and there shall not be any remaining of the house of Esau" (v. 18). The book's ethic is retaliation, stated plainly.

The ethical question. Deuteronomy 23:7 commands, "Thou shalt not abhor an Edomite; for he is thy brother," and Proverbs 24:17 warns, "Rejoice not when thine enemy falleth." Obadiah condemns Edom for gloating, and much of the book reads like gloating in return. Some readers accept it as the honest cry of victims, which scripture does not tidy up, much like Psalm 137. Others think a total-destruction oracle against a whole people is something a modern conscience should not take on without question.

The historical question. Did Edom really do it? J. R. Bartlett argued in 1982 that the usual picture of Edom as Babylon's eager partner in 587/586 BC overstates the case ("Edom and the Fall of Jerusalem, 587 B.C."). The academic scholar adds more recent work. In "The Edomite Involvement in the Destruction of the First Temple: A Case of Stab-in-the-Back Tradition?" (JSOT, 2011), Juan Manuel Tebes argues that the biblical texts accusing Edom were written well after the events, contain few concrete details, and fit the pattern of "stab-in-the-back" myths familiar from modern defeated societies. Edomite material culture does appear in the late Iron Age Judean Negev, but whether that reflects invasion or gradual migration and trade is disputed. Bert Dicou's Edom, Israel's Brother and Antagonist (1994) shows how Edom, linked to Esau and so to Jacob's brother (v. 10), became the standard enemy in prophetic literature. In this reading, Obadiah is less a war report than a way of turning communal humiliation into a coherent story. The LDS scholar notes the same doubts and lands on the human core: the oracle is in any case an expression of grief and grievance against a brother.

"Not any remaining"? History also complicates "not any remaining." The Edomites, later called Idumeans, did not vanish. The Hasmonean ruler John Hyrcanus forced them to adopt Judaism (Josephus, Antiquities 13.257–58), and Herod the Great descended from them.

So the panel does not settle this one. Victims' honest cry, a conscience that hesitates, a historical charge that may have grown in the retelling, and a people who outlived the curse: all four are true of the same twenty-one verses.

When Edom became Rome (Obadiah 1:19–20)

The rabbi asks Christian readers to feel something they usually miss. In rabbinic literature "Edom" became the code name for Rome, the empire that destroyed the Second Temple. When Rome became Christian, the name moved with it. Don Isaac Abarbanel, himself driven out of Spain in 1492, opens his commentary by asking whether Obadiah's Edom is the small kingdom south of the Dead Sea "or... the Edom that is Rome and the lands of the Christians" (Abarbanel on Obadiah 1).

Look at verse 20: "the captivity of Jerusalem, which is in Sepharad, shall possess the cities of the south." The exile is written into the book. The Targum renders Sepharad as Spain, and Rashi reports that "the exegetes claim that Zarephath is the kingdom called France" (Rashi on Obadiah 1:20). Hence "Sephardim." For centuries Jews read Obadiah as a promise that the exiles in Christian Spain and France would go home. A Christian reading this book should know that for a long time the Jewish reader's Edom was the reader's own church.

"Saviours shall come up on mount Zion" (Obadiah 1:21)

"And saviours shall come up on mount Zion to judge the mount of Esau; and the kingdom shall be the LORD's" (v. 21).

In its setting. The Hebrew moshiʻim is the word used for the judges God "raised up" to deliver Israel (Judges 3:9, 15; Nehemiah 9:27). In context these are deliverers who will govern Edom's territory under God's rule. The verse is about rescue and God's rule after Edom's fall; it says nothing directly about the dead. The final words, "and the kingdom shall be the LORD's," reach past Edom to a claim about who ultimately rules.

In the synagogue. The last verse is in our daily prayers. It is said every morning right after the Song at the Sea, and on Rosh Hashanah among the verses of divine kingship. Rashi ties it to Genesis. Jacob told Esau he would come to him in Seir (Genesis 33:14) and never went. "But when will he go? In the days of the Messiah, as it is said (Obadiah 1:21) And saviours shall go up on Mount Zion" (Rashi on Genesis 33:14). Obadiah 21 finishes the brothers' unfinished business.

In the Restoration. Joseph Smith gave the phrase a new referent. In a sermon of January 21, 1844, in Nauvoo, as recorded by Wilford Woodruff, he connected Malachi's Elijah to Obadiah: "The keys are to be delivered, the spirit of Elijah is to come, the Gospel to be established, the Saints of God gathered, Zion built up, and the Saints to come up as saviors on Mount Zion." He then answered his own question: "But how are they to become saviors on Mount Zion? By building their temples, erecting their baptismal fonts, and going forth and receiving all the ordinances... in behalf of all their progenitors who are dead." The doctrinal reason, the connector notes, is in Doctrine and Covenants 128: "they without us cannot be made perfect—neither can we without our dead be made perfect" (128:15). And President Joseph F. Smith's vision supplies the other side of the veil (Doctrine and Covenants 138:58).

The lesson cites President Gordon B. Hinckley's October 2004 closing remarks. President Gordon B. Hinckley applied that verse to proxy temple ordinances, teaching that "we literally become saviors on Mount Zion," and that "in a small measure, when we engage in proxy work in the temple," we "become as saviors to those on the other side" ("Closing Remarks," October 2004). The qualifier "in a small measure" matters. The saviours of Obadiah 21 are derivative; their work has power only because of the one Savior whose kingdom it finally is: "and the kingdom shall be the LORD's." The rabbi, from outside the tradition, lands on the same clause: whoever the deliverers are, "the kingdom shall be the LORD's," not theirs.

Honest about the move. Every Latter-day Saint voice on the panel names what kind of reading this is. A scholar should say plainly that this is a re-signification, not the verse's original sense. The Restoration reading is an expansion, and it is better to say so. But it is not arbitrary. Edom's crime was the opposite of saving: it "stood in the crossway, to cut off those of his that did escape" (v. 14). The saviours of verse 21 reverse that betrayal and open the way. The LDS scholar finds the same thread: a moshiaʻ acts for people who cannot save themselves, and Obadiah's last word is that the kingdom belongs to the LORD, not to the victors. A book that begins with a brother's betrayal ends, in Restoration usage, with the living acting for those who cannot act.

The honest questioner goes further than anyone. A verse about judging Esau becomes a verse about redeeming the dead, Esau's descendants presumably among them. Whether you see that as inspired reinterpretation or as reading against the text, it is the most generous possible thing to do with Obadiah. It also fits the spirit of Jonah, the book that comes next. The rabbi, for his part, notes plainly that Jewish tradition never made that reading.

The practical disciple sets the end of the book against its middle. The contrast with verses 11–14 is worth sitting with. Edom stood "on the other side" of a brother's catastrophe and took advantage. The disciple is called to stand for those "on the other side" and do for them what they cannot do for themselves. The lesson asks how temple work is "similar—if only in a small way—to what the Savior did for us." Obadiah's own verses suggest a second question: who is the brother you have been watching from the other side?

Think deeply.

  1. Amos 9:7 says the exodus was one of several that God directed. What would change in how you see your own people, ward, or nation if you believed God had led other peoples' journeys too?
  2. If 9:11–15 was added by a later hand, does that make it less true for you, or more? What do you make of a book of doom that its keepers would not let end in doom?
  3. James read "the remnant of Edom" as "the residue of men" and found the Gentile mission there. When has an inspired re-reading of scripture opened a door for you or for someone you love?
  4. Obadiah traces a slope from standing aloof to looking on to rejoicing to looting. Whose failure, if you heard about it tomorrow, would give you a flicker of satisfaction? Pray for that person by name this week.
  5. The rabbi notes that for centuries the Jewish reader's "Edom" was the Christian church. How does it feel to find yourself cast as Edom in someone else's reading? What does that do to how you read Obadiah's vengeance?
  6. A verse about judging Esau became, in the Restoration, a verse about redeeming the dead. How does your own temple or family history work change if you think of it as the opposite of standing "on the other side"?

25–30 minSession 5: Should Not I Spare Nineveh?

Reading assignment: Jonah 1, 2, 3, 4

Jonah son of Amittai (1:1–2)

"Now the word of the LORD came unto Jonah the son of Amittai, saying, Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and cry against it; for their wickedness is come up before me" (1:1–2). You have met this Jonah before. The book's Jonah son of Amittai is otherwise known only from 2 Kings 14:25, where he prophesies Jeroboam II's expansion of Israel's borders, in Amos's own generation. The prophet remembered for good news about Israel's territory is sent to the capital of the empire that would end it. The lesson reminds you that Nineveh was "an enemy of Israel known for its violence and cruelty."

Read the book once straight through before the commentary. It takes about ten minutes.

Wrestle: the fish, the city, and what kind of book this is

The fish. The objection most people meet first is the plainest one: a man cannot survive three days inside a fish (Jonah 1:17). Some well-meaning defenses have made things worse. For about a century, sermons and conservative commentaries told the story of James Bartley, a nineteenth-century whaler supposedly swallowed by a sperm whale and later cut out alive. The historian of science Edward B. Davis traced the story through ship records and newspapers. He found that it falls apart. A reader who defends the fish with a false story is in a weaker spot than one who simply says, "the LORD had prepared a great fish." The text presents the fish as a miracle, not a fact of marine biology. And it is a fish, not a whale. The "great fish" (dag gadol, 1:17; Hebrew 2:1) became a "whale" only through the Greek kētos of Matthew 12:40.

The city. For scholars the fish is not the main difficulty. The book calls the ruler "the king of Nineveh" (3:6), a title Assyrian royal inscriptions do not use. It calls Nineveh "an exceeding great city of three days' journey" (3:3). Nineveh became the imperial capital only under Sennacherib around 705 BCE, and even then its walls ran about twelve kilometers around, a walk of a few hours rather than three days. A 2026 study by Aaron Hornkohl reviews more than fifty proposed late features and concludes that the language belongs "most plausibly" to the sixth or fifth century BCE. Jonah borrows its prophet from 2 Kings 14:25 but not his century. The academic scholar even reads the fasting animals of 3:7–8 as a joke: The decree that animals too must fast in sackcloth (3:7–8) is comedy or imagination.

The other side. The other side has answers. The same encyclopedia article suggests that "three days' journey" may describe the whole district Nineveh governed, not just the walled city. Paul J. N. Lawrence argued in the Tyndale Bulletin (1986) that "king of Nineveh" and "king and nobles" fit an Assyrian setting in the early eighth century. The honest summary is that the evidence leans toward a late, literary composition, but not so far that a historical reading is foolish.

The Church's own sources. They differ, and that is worth knowing. The Bible Dictionary entry on Jonah is more candid than many readers expect. It says the book "does not claim to be from the hand of the prophet; it describes an episode in his life and is due to some later writer," calls it "a beautiful poem," and finds its key in Jonah 3:10–4:11, where the writer opposes "a narrowmindedness that would confine the love of God to a single nation." The Old Testament Student Manual: Kings–Malachi calls Jonah's story "a true one, and not an allegory as some scholars maintain." It quotes Joseph Fielding Smith: "I believe, as did Mr. William J. Bryan, the story of Jonah." LeGrande Davies wrote that whether Jonah is "historical or unhistorical, literal or allegorical, is strictly a matter of personal preference" ("Jonah: Testimony of the Resurrection," 1984). David R. Scott, in BYU Studies (2014), read Jonah as a typological foreshadowing of Christ and stated in a note, "Personally, I prefer a symbolic interpretation of the book of Jonah rather than a literal one." Church leaders have usually spoken of Jonah as a historical person, and Jesus's "sign of the prophet Jonas" (Matthew 12:39–41; Luke 11:29–32) is often taken to settle the matter.

"A greater than Jonas is here." The strongest argument for historicity comes from the New Testament: "For as Jonas was three days and three nights in the whale's belly; so shall the Son of man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth. The men of Nineveh shall rise in judgment with this generation... because they repented at the preaching of Jonas; and, behold, a greater than Jonas is here" (Matthew 12:40–41). The second sentence carries the most weight. A fictional city, the argument goes, cannot rise in judgment against a real generation. The counterargument deserves a hearing too. Teachers often cite characters everyone knows to make a moral point, and the force of the point does not depend on whether the character existed. The honest questioner adds a wrinkle. The time reference also creates a problem for literalists. Jesus was buried Friday evening and rose before dawn Sunday, parts of three days but only two nights. Most interpreters solve this by treating "three days and three nights" as an idiom that counts part of a day as a whole day. Neither reading of Matthew 12 forces the other. The sign works as a type of the Resurrection under both.

Where that leaves you. A reader who settles on either position is in good company. A Latter-day Saint can hold either view in good faith. What the book itself asks is not whether a fish swallowed a man but whether the reader will accept a merciful God. The rabbi adds that the synagogue reads Jonah for a different sign altogether: the sign of Jonah that matters in the synagogue is not the three days in the fish (Matthew 12:40). It is the forty days, the open question, and a God who "repented of the evil" (3:10).

Down, down, down (1:3–16)

"But Jonah rose up to flee unto Tarshish from the presence of the LORD, and went down to Joppa; and he found a ship going to Tarshish: so he paid the fare thereof, and went down into it" (1:3). Then "Jonah was gone down into the sides of the ship; and he lay, and was fast asleep" (1:5). Jonah goes down: down to Joppa, down into the ship, down into its hold (1:3, 5), and eventually down to "the bottoms of the mountains" (2:6).

The practical disciple names three movements in the book, and the first two are here. Fleeing. Jonah does not argue with the assignment. Most avoidance looks like this. We don't refuse the calling; we just never quite get to it. Sleeping. While the sailors pray, throw cargo overboard, and row, Jonah sleeps, and a pagan shipmaster has to wake him: "What meanest thou, O sleeper? arise, call upon thy God" (1:6). The people outside the covenant behave better in chapter 1 than the prophet does — a caution for any of us who assume the neighbor or colleague of another faith, or none, is less attuned to God than we are.

Watch the sailors. They cast lots, they ask Jonah who he is, and when he says "I fear the LORD, the God of heaven, which hath made the sea and the dry land" (1:9), they are terrified. He tells them to throw him overboard. "Nevertheless the men rowed hard to bring it to the land; but they could not" (1:13). They pray to Jonah's God not to hold his blood against them, and when the sea goes calm, "the men feared the LORD exceedingly, and offered a sacrifice unto the LORD, and made vows" (1:16). The pagan sailors, who pray and row hard to spare him, end up fearing "the LORD exceedingly" (1:16), more devout than the prophet.

Why did Jonah run?

The book makes you wait until chapter 4 for Jonah's own answer, but readers have always guessed. Why, the book never says. Readers have proposed nationalism (Assyria would destroy Israel), prophetic credibility (by Deuteronomy 18:22 an unfulfilled prediction marks a false prophet), or resentment that repentance can cancel justice.

The oldest Jewish answer is the most sympathetic. The Mekhilta, the early midrash on Exodus, assumes Jonah knew no one can flee God. Doesn't Amos say, "Though they dig into hell, thence shall mine hand take them" (Amos 9:2)? So Jonah was not fleeing God. He meant to leave the land where prophecy comes, "for the gentiles are close to repentance," and he would not let Nineveh's quick repentance indict Israel, which had not repented. Jeremiah upheld the honor of the Father and of the son. Elijah upheld the Father's honor but not the son's. "Jonah claimed the honor of the son, but not the honor of the Father." Rabbi Yochanan adds that Jonah boarded the ship "only to cast himself into the sea," like Moses, who said "blot me... out of thy book" (Mekhilta de-Rabbi Yishmael, Pischa 1). This Jonah is no bigot. He is a patriot ready to die rather than become the instrument of his people's condemnation. The honest questioner reports the same reading: On that reading Jonah is not a bigot but a patriot afraid of what Nineveh's virtue would mean for his own people.

Medieval readers kept the theme. Radak agrees and says the book was written "as an object lesson for Israel: Here is a gentile people... who readily repent" (Radak on Jonah 1:1). The academic scholar hears a later argument in the same theme: Set beside Persian-period arguments over outsiders (compare Ezra–Nehemiah's marriage reforms), the book reads like one side of a live debate.

From the belly of the fish (1:17–2:10)

"Now the LORD had prepared a great fish to swallow up Jonah. And Jonah was in the belly of the fish three days and three nights" (1:17). A reader's note: KJV Jonah 1:17 is Jonah 2:1 in the Hebrew Bible, so in a Jewish Bible the fish opens chapter 2 and the prayer runs through 2:11. A midrash enjoys the scene: the fish was appointed "from the six days of Creation," Jonah walked into it "just as a man enters the great synagogue," and it carried him to the foundation stone beneath the Temple, where he prayed.

A psalm out of place? The psalm in chapter 2 is a well-known problem. It is a thanksgiving psalm, built from phrases familiar from the Psalter, sung before Jonah is back on dry land. Many scholars have treated it as a later insertion. Others read the mismatch as deliberate: Jonah piously recites "Salvation is of the LORD" (2:9) while still showing no change of heart. The lesson's children's section reads the same prayer as Jonah's repentance (2:1–4, 9). Those are different readings, and the text allows both: Jonah does turn toward God, and chapter 4 shows how much of him has not yet turned.

The temple. The doctrinal connector notices where Jonah's prayer points. From the depths Jonah says, "yet I will look again toward thy holy temple" (2:4), and "my prayer came in unto thee, into thine holy temple" (2:7).

"Their own mercy." Then the line that Elder Matthew S. Holland took as the title of an October 2025 conference talk: "They that observe lying vanities forsake their own mercy" (2:8). Elder Holland read "their own mercy" as a personal gift. He described it as "perfectly suited to you, designed to relieve your individual agonies and heal your particular pains." He called Jonah's cry, as the LDS scholar quotes, "that of a good man in crisis, one largely of his own making." In his notes he observes that the Hebrew word for mercy here is hesed, linking it to President Russell M. Nelson's 2022 teaching on covenant love. The same word comes back in 4:2, where Jonah complains that God is "of great kindness," literally abundant in hesed. Jonah wants covenant mercy for himself in chapter 2 and resents God's giving it to Nineveh in chapter 4. The practical disciple: Most of us know the feeling. It is easy to believe in grace for ourselves and in justice for everyone else.

Descent. Jesus took those three days as the one sign He would give (Matthew 12:39–40), and the Lord's word in Liberty Jail names what the descent points to: "The Son of Man hath descended below them all" (Doctrine and Covenants 122:8). From "the belly of hell" (2:2) Jonah says, "yet hast thou brought up my life from corruption, O LORD my God" (2:6). The LDS scholar notes that LeGrande Davies, in his 1984 Religious Studies Center chapter, chose to read the story typologically as a witness of the Resurrection.

"Yet forty days" (3:1–4)

"And the word of the LORD came unto Jonah the second time" (3:1). This time he goes. He walks a day into the city and cries, "Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown" (3:4). That is the whole sermon. Jonah's oracle is five Hebrew words: "Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown" (3:4).

Overthrown, or turned? The verb, nehpakhet, comes from the root used for the overthrow of Sodom (Genesis 19:25), but the same stem describes a heart "turned" (Hosea 11:8; Exodus 14:5). Nineveh was overturned, just not as Jonah meant. The academic scholar agrees: In the second sense, the prediction comes true.

A false prophet? By the test in Deuteronomy 18:22 ("if the thing follow not, nor come to pass, that is the thing which the LORD hath not spoken"), Jonah looks like a false prophet. Perhaps that is part of his anger. The rabbi answers from Maimonides. What of the theory that Jonah feared being called a false prophet? A prophecy of disaster that does not happen does not discredit the prophet, "because the Holy One, Blessed be He, is slow to anger... it is possible that they will repent... as in the case of the people of Nineveh." Only a promise of good that fails proves a prophet false (Mishneh Torah, Foundations of the Torah 10:4). Deuteronomy 18:22 does not convict Jonah.

Nineveh turns (3:5–10)

"So the people of Nineveh believed God, and proclaimed a fast, and put on sackcloth, from the greatest of them even to the least of them" (3:5). The king leaves his throne for ashes, and his decree reaches even the animals: "let man and beast be covered with sackcloth, and cry mightily unto God: yea, let them turn every one from his evil way, and from the violence that is in their hands. Who can tell if God will turn and repent, and turn away from his fierce anger, that we perish not?" (3:8–9). Then: "And God saw their works, that they turned from their evil way; and God repented of the evil, that he had said that he would do unto them; and he did it not" (3:10).

Their works, not their sackcloth. The Mishnah turned Jonah 3 into public ritual. On a communal fast the ark was carried into the town square and covered with ashes, and an elder said: "Our brothers, it is not stated with regard to the people of Nineveh: And God saw their sackcloth and their fasting. Rather... And God saw their deeds" (Mishnah Taʻanit 2:1; Jonah 3:10). The practical disciple draws on the same text: Sackcloth was the outward sign. What God responded to was that they let go of "the violence that is in their hands." That is the link back to Amos, whose God hated feast days kept beside injustice. The Talmud goes further: On "the violence that is in their hands" (3:8), Shmuel says the king decreed that a man who had built a stolen beam into his palace must tear the palace down and return the beam (Babylonian Talmud, Taʻanit 16a).

Real, or theater? The rabbis did not romanticize Nineveh, though. In the Jerusalem Talmud, Reish Lakish says flatly that "the people of Nineveh repented in trickery," separating calves from their mothers so the bawling would force heaven's hand (Jerusalem Talmud, Taʻanit 2:1). Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer 43 gives Nineveh forty years of reprieve, one for each day of Jonah's warning, before it relapsed. The tradition knows that public repentance can be real and can be theater, and it reads Jonah 3 on the holiest day of the year anyway. History adds its own question. There is also the historical sequel: Nineveh fell about 612 BC, and the book of Nahum celebrates its fall. Did the repentance fail, or did it buy one generation of mercy? The academic scholar is untroubled: That the real Nineveh was later destroyed (Nahum celebrates it) did not trouble the author; the story is about whether God may be merciful, not about Assyrian history.

God "repented" again. You wrestled with this verb in Session 3. The JST changes Jonah 3 too. JST Jonah 3:9: "Who can tell, if we will repent and turn unto God, but he will turn away from us his fierce anger that we perish not?" JST Jonah 3:10: "And God saw their works, that they turned from their evil way and repented. And God turned away the evil that he had said he would bring upon them." The rabbi, for his part, does not want the verb removed. He cites Abraham Joshua Heschel's The Prophets (1962): the prophets' God is a God of pathos, moved by what human beings do. That is why repentance is not a transaction but a relationship. Here the Restoration's JST and the Jewish reading genuinely differ, and both are trying to protect something: the JST, a God who does not change; Heschel, a God who is not unmoved. Both agree that Nineveh's turning is what God answered.

"I knew that thou art a gracious God" (4:1–4)

"But it displeased Jonah exceedingly, and he was very angry. And he prayed unto the LORD, and said, I pray thee, O LORD, was not this my saying, when I was yet in my country? Therefore I fled before unto Tarshish: for I knew that thou art a gracious God, and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness, and repentest thee of the evil" (4:1–2).

At last, Jonah's own reason. The theological center of the book is Jonah 4:2. This is the creed of Exodus 34:6 (also in Joel 2:13) turned into a complaint; Jonah seems to want Exodus 34:7's punishment of the guilty instead. The LDS scholar puts it sharply: Jonah turns Israel's creed against God. And the practical disciple: Jonah's problem is not that he doubts God. He knows exactly who God is. That is the problem. He fled to Tarshish not because he feared Nineveh would reject him, but because he feared it would accept the message, and God would forgive people Jonah did not want forgiven.

What Jonah left out. The rabbi asks you to hear the verse as a Yom Kippur congregation does, after a whole day of chanting Exodus 34:6, "merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abundant in goodness and truth." Jonah throws the same formula at God, "a gracious God, and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness, and repentest thee of the evil," and a listener notices the swap. "And truth" (ve-ʼemet) is gone, and "repentest thee of the evil" has taken its place. In Jonah's version, mercy has swallowed truth.

Then Jonah asks to die (4:3). Jonah's anger in chapter 4 is not that the message failed. It is that it worked. The practical disciple calls this third movement Sulking. Chapter 4 is the hardest. God asks only, "Doest thou well to be angry?" (4:4). This is the elder brother who "was angry, and would not go in" (Luke 15:28), and the laborers told, "Is thine eye evil, because I am good?" (Matthew 20:15). It is us when a family member who caused years of pain is now well-liked at church, or a ward member whose past we know is called to a position of trust.

The gourd and the last question (4:5–11)

Jonah goes east of the city and builds a booth, "till he might see what would become of the city" (4:5). He sits east of the city "till he might see what would become of the city" (4:5), still hoping, it seems, for fire. God prepares a gourd for shade, and "Jonah was exceeding glad of the gourd" (4:6). Then God prepares a worm, and the gourd withers; then "a vehement east wind," and Jonah again wishes to die. "Doest thou well to be angry for the gourd? And he said, I do well to be angry, even unto death" (4:7–9). The lesson's Scripture Helps asks what the gourd meant.

The book ends with God's question. "Thou hast had pity on the gourd, for the which thou hast not laboured, neither madest it grow; which came up in a night, and perished in a night: And should not I spare Nineveh, that great city, wherein are more than sixscore thousand persons that cannot discern between their right hand and their left hand; and also much cattle?" (4:10–11). God contrasts Jonah's pity for a plant (qiqayon, often identified as the castor-oil plant) that grew and died overnight with his own pity for "more than sixscore thousand persons that cannot discern between their right hand and their left hand; and also much cattle" (4:11). Jonah never replies.

Filling the silence. Readers have always rushed into that silence. Yvonne Sherwood's A Biblical Text and Its Afterlives (2000) traces how readers have filled that silence: Jonah as a type of Christ's three days in the tomb, Jonah as the stubborn Jew of Christian polemic, Jonah as the Yom Kippur model of repentance. Uriel Simon, author of the JPS commentary on the book, reads all of Jonah as a struggle between justice and mercy, in which the prophet must learn that "the world can exist only through the unfathomable amalgam of justice and mercy" (Simon, "Jonah's Lesson in Divine Mercy"). The late Midrash Jonah, which Simon cites, supplies the answer: Jonah falls on his face and says, "Conduct Your world according to the attribute of mercy." And the synagogue answers for him: The Shulchan Arukh adds that the reader closes not on Jonah 4:11 but on Micah 7:18–20, "Who is a God like unto thee, that pardoneth iniquity" (Shulchan Arukh, Orach Chayim 622:2). We do not leave the congregation hanging on Jonah's unanswered question. We answer it ourselves.

The Latter-day Saint voices answer it with another book of scripture. The lesson suggests comparing Jonah with the sons of Mosiah. The sons of Mosiah, sent to the Lamanites, "could not bear that any human soul should perish" (Mosiah 28:3). Jonah could bear it quite well. The doctrinal connector adds Ammon, who went to his nation's enemies and said to their king, "Nay, but I will be thy servant" (Alma 17:25). The contrast is real. For the LDS scholar the Book of Mormon gives what Jonah withholds: not a ruling on the fish, but a canon in which the question of 4:11 has been answered yes.

Others leave the question open on purpose. The book ends without Jonah's answer to God's last question (4:11), and that silence leaves the question to the reader. The book does not resolve that tension. It leaves it with the reader. And the rabbi refuses to let anyone pick only one side of the story: At that hour we are Nineveh, fasting and hoping God will see our deeds and not our sackcloth. We are also Jonah, who would rather the Ninevehs of the world got what they deserve. The book will not let us be only one of them.

Where the panel ends

The honest questioner, after every hard question: The fish, God's relenting, the prophet's authority, and the curse on Edom each allow more than one faithful response, and each has been read by serious people in opposite ways. What the text does not allow is pretending the questions are not there. The doctrinal connector gathers the three books into one calling: Taken together, the three books describe what that calling requires: admission to God's council (Amos), mercy that reaches past one's own nation (Jonah), and a reversal of Edom's betrayal by opening the way for others, including the dead (Obadiah). The rabbi names what Christian readers tend to miss: First, for Jews these are not texts about other people; we aimed them at ourselves. The six-outlooks essay, which closed Session 3 on the remedy of seeking him, reminds you that the book does not end in famine. And the practical disciple leaves you with a list to answer specifically, with names and dates, not in general, ending with this one: Whom do I not want God to forgive (Jonah 4:1–2)? What would it mean to answer God's final question in Jonah 4:11 honestly?

Think deeply.

  1. Jonah fled, slept, and sulked. Which of the three is your usual way of avoiding something God has asked of you? What assignment are you quietly sailing away from right now?
  2. The Church's own sources range from "a true one, and not an allegory" to "strictly a matter of personal preference." How much does the historicity of the fish matter to your faith? What does the book ask of you either way?
  3. The sailors behave better than the prophet. Who outside your faith has shown you more of God's character than you showed them? What did you learn?
  4. The Mishnah says God saw Nineveh's deeds, not its sackcloth; Reish Lakish says the repentance was a trick. What is the "stolen beam" in your house, the thing real repentance would require you to tear out and give back?
  5. Jonah quoted God's mercy as a complaint and left out "and truth." Whom do you not want God to forgive? What would it cost you to answer God's last question in 4:11 honestly?
  6. The rabbi says that on Yom Kippur "we are Nineveh" and "we are also Jonah." In what part of your life are you Nineveh, hoping for mercy? In what part are you Jonah, hoping for fire?
