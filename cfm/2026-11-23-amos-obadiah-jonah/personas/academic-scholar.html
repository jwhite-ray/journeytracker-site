Three Books, Three Centuries: Amos, Obadiah, and Jonah in Critical Perspective

Written by an AI persona in the voice of a non-Latter-day-Saint Hebrew Bible academic — not a real scholar.

The canon gives these three books a shared setting. Amos is dated to "the days of Jeroboam the son of Joash king of Israel" (Amos 1:1), and the Jonah of the story is the Jonah son of Amittai who, according to 2 Kings 14:25, predicted Jeroboam II's territorial expansion. Read critically, though, the three books cover roughly three centuries of Israelite history. Amos contains some of the oldest prophetic material in the Bible, reworked for generations. Obadiah responds to the catastrophe of 586 BCE. Jonah is, by most current estimates, a Persian-period narrative that uses an eighth-century prophet as its main character.

Amos: a herdsman in a boom economy

The setting under Jeroboam II

The early eighth century was a good time to be rich in the northern kingdom. Assyrian pressure under Adad-nirari III had weakened Damascus; the Tell al-Rimah stela, usually dated to about 797 BCE, records tribute from "Joash the Samarian," almost certainly Jeroboam II's father. With Aram reduced, Jeroboam II could push Israel's borders outward; Amos 6:13 seems to mock the resulting victory talk ("Have we not taken to us horns by our own strength?").

Archaeology fills in the economy that Amos attacks. The Samaria ostraca are potsherds recording deliveries of "old wine" and "washed oil" to the capital from surrounding villages and estates. They are generally placed in the reign of Jeroboam II or his father, and a 2020 algorithmic handwriting study by Shira Faigenbaum-Golovin, Israel Finkelstein, and colleagues concluded that they were most likely written by just two scribes in Samaria: a small, centralized palace bureaucracy. The excavations at Samaria also turned up hundreds of fragments of carved ivory, mostly furniture inlays in Phoenician and North Syrian styles. Their exact date and place of manufacture are debated, but they show the kind of luxury Amos has in mind with "beds of ivory" (6:4) and "the houses of ivory shall perish" (3:15).

The charges in the text are specific. The wealthy "sold the righteous for silver, and the poor for a pair of shoes" (2:6). Pledged garments that Exodus 22:26 says must be returned by sundown become bedding "by every altar" (2:8). Grain merchants cannot wait for the new moon and the Sabbath to end so they can sell, "making the ephah small, and the shekel great, and falsifying the balances by deceit" (8:5). The complaints are fraud, debt slavery, and courts ("the gate," 5:10, 12, 15) captured by the rich.

How the system worked is disputed. A model associated with Bernard Lang's essay "The Social Organization of Peasant Poverty in Biblical Israel," describes "rent capitalism": an urban elite absorbing peasant land through loans and foreclosure, turning smallholders into tenants and debt-slaves. Critics argue that the model imports categories from much later economies; Walter Houston's Contending for Justice is a careful treatment of how prophetic critique relates to the actual economy. The archaeology confirms wealth concentrated in the capital, not the mechanics of dispossession.

The banquet and the earthquake

The "banquet of them that stretched themselves" (6:7) is in Hebrew the marzeaḥ, known from fourteenth-century BCE Ugarit as a recognized drinking association, linked variously with honoring the dead or a patron deity. Either way, Amos is condemning an established elite institution, with wine "in bowls" and "the chief ointments," whose members "are not grieved for the affliction of Joseph" (6:6).

The superscription dates the book "two years before the earthquake" (1:1), and Zechariah 14:5 still remembered "the earthquake in the days of Uzziah" centuries later. At Hazor, Yigael Yadin found collapse debris in Stratum VI, an eighth-century layer beneath the burnt Assyrian destruction of 732 BCE. In 2000, Steven A. Austin, Gordon W. Franz, and Eric G. Frost argued in International Geology Review that damage at several sites reflects a single great earthquake around 750 BCE. Austin is a young-earth creationist, and others treat the precise date and magnitude more cautiously, but a major mid-eighth-century quake is reasonably well supported. It may also explain why Amos's words were kept: a prophet who said the land would "tremble" (8:8) looked vindicated.

A book built in layers

Few scholars think Amos wrote the book as we have it; the question is how much later material it contains. Hans Walter Wolff's Hermeneia commentary (1977) proposed six stages of growth, ending with exilic and post-exilic additions. Jörg Jeremias (OTL, 1998) also traces a book produced in stages, in which later Judean tradents reread a northern prophet's words in light of Samaria's fall in 722 BCE and what followed. Shalom M. Paul's Hermeneia volume (1991) is the main defense of near-unity: he argues that the book is substantially Amos's own, with at most a few minor additions. Francis I. Andersen and David Noel Freedman's massive Anchor Bible commentary (1989) also leans toward reading the book as a coherent whole.

The passages most often identified as additions are a recognizable group:

  • The Judah oracle (2:4–5), whose generic Deuteronomistic language ("despised the law of the LORD") lacks the concrete war crimes charged elsewhere.
  • The Tyre and Edom oracles (1:9–12), shorter and formulaic, with charges against Edom that fit the sixth century better than the eighth.
  • The hymn fragments (4:13; 5:8–9; 9:5–6), each ending "The LORD... is his name," which interrupt their contexts like a liturgical refrain.
  • The ending (9:11–15), which promises to "raise up the tabernacle of David that is fallen" and so assumes the Davidic house has already fallen, most naturally after 586 BCE.

The last matters most. The earlier chapters are relentless: "The end is come upon my people of Israel" (8:2), in a wordplay between qayits (summer fruit) and qets (end); "he that fleeth of them shall not flee away" (9:1). Read without 9:11–15, Amos is a prophet of near-total doom, offering at most the thin "it may be" of 5:15. Whether the happy ending is a later editor's softening or the prophet's own last word is a central question in the study of Amos.

"No prophet" and the herdsman's resume

In the book's only narrative (7:10–17), Amaziah calls Amos a "seer" (ḥozeh) and tells him to go earn his living in Judah. Amos answers: "I was no prophet, neither was I a prophet's son" (7:14). The Hebrew sentence has no verb, so it can be read as past ("I was not") or present ("I am not"). Read as present, Amos refuses the title navi, professional prophet; read as past, he simply had no prophetic background before the call. "Prophet's son" most likely means a guild member, like the "sons of the prophets" in the Elisha stories.

The resume itself is unusual. The word for "herdmen" in 1:1 is noqed, the same word used for Mesha king of Moab, who paid Israel a huge annual tribute in sheep (2 Kings 3:4). Amos may have been a livestock owner of some standing rather than a poor shepherd. The "sycomore" is the sycamore fig, which grows in low, warm country and not on the high ground around Tekoa. So "a gatherer of sycomore fruit" suggests seasonal work or holdings elsewhere. Either way, his point is that his authority does not come from any institution.

Cult, chosenness, and the divine council

In Amos 5:21–25 God says, "I hate, I despise your feast days... Though ye offer me burnt offerings... I will not accept them," and then asks, "Have ye offered unto me sacrifices and offerings in the wilderness forty years?" (5:25). The question seems to expect the answer no, which contradicts the Torah's detailed wilderness sacrificial system. Perhaps Amos did not know the Priestly legislation, or did not treat it as foundational. Most scholars read the passage as rejecting worship that coexists with injustice, not worship as such, but the language is absolute and that softer reading is brought to it.

Two verses about Israel's election pull against each other. "You only have I known of all the families of the earth: therefore I will punish you" (3:2) turns election into accountability. "Are ye not as children of the Ethiopians unto me?... Have not I brought up Israel out of the land of Egypt? and the Philistines from Caphtor, and the Syrians from Kir?" (9:7) goes further and makes the exodus one migration among several that YHWH directed. Together they redefine Israel's special status as responsibility before a universal deity.

Amos 3:7 says the Lord "revealeth his secret unto his servants the prophets." The word translated "secret" is sod, a confidential plan but also the council itself, the assembly of divine beings where decisions are made. Jeremiah 23:18, 22 distinguishes true prophets as those who have "stood in the counsel [sod] of the LORD." Behind both is the ancient image of the prophet who overhears the divine assembly. The surrounding cause-and-effect questions (3:3–8) build to "the Lord GOD hath spoken, who can but prophesy?" In context the point is that the prophet cannot help speaking, not that prophets are a permanent institution. Likewise the "famine... of hearing the words of the LORD" in 8:11–12 is a judgment on eighth-century Israel: the people who told the prophets "Prophesy not" (2:12) will one day look for an oracle and find none.

Obadiah: grief turned into a war oracle

Obadiah, the shortest book in the Hebrew Bible, carries no date, but its content points to one. Edom is charged with standing aloof "in the day that the strangers carried away captive his forces, and foreigners entered into his gates, and cast lots upon Jerusalem" (v. 11), then gloating, looting, and standing "in the crossway, to cut off those of his that did escape" (v. 14). Most scholars connect this to the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BCE. The same accusation appears in Psalm 137:7, Lamentations 4:21–22, and Ezekiel 25:12–14 and 35. Obadiah 1–9 also overlaps closely with Jeremiah 49:7–22; who borrowed from whom, or whether both drew on a common stock of anti-Edom oracles, is unresolved. Ehud Ben Zvi's A Historical-Critical Study of the Book of Obadiah (1996) reads it as a written work for post-monarchic readers rather than a transcript of a speech.

The historical accusation itself has been questioned. Archaeologists of Iron Age Edom, such as Piotr Bienkowski, have reconstructed a kingdom whose material culture does appear in the late Iron Age Judean Negev, but whether that reflects invasion or gradual migration and trade is disputed. In "The Edomite Involvement in the Destruction of the First Temple: A Case of Stab-in-the-Back Tradition?" (JSOT, 2011), Juan Manuel Tebes argues that the biblical texts accusing Edom were written well after the events, contain few concrete details, and fit the pattern of "stab-in-the-back" myths familiar from modern defeated societies. Bert Dicou's Edom, Israel's Brother and Antagonist (1994) shows how Edom, linked to Esau and so to Jacob's brother (v. 10), became the standard enemy in prophetic literature. In this reading, Obadiah is less a war report than a way of turning communal humiliation into a coherent story.

The book's ethic is retaliation, stated plainly: "as thou hast done, it shall be done unto thee" (v. 15). The "saviours" (moshiʻim) who "come up on mount Zion to judge the mount of Esau" (v. 21) take their title from the deliverer-judges of the book of Judges (compare Nehemiah 9:27). The final words, "and the kingdom shall be the LORD's," reach past Edom to a claim about who ultimately rules.

Jonah: a late, clever story about an early prophet

Date and genre

Jonah borrows its prophet from 2 Kings 14:25 but not his century. Its Hebrew mixes classical forms with features of Late Biblical Hebrew and Aramaic. A 2026 study by Aaron Hornkohl reviews more than fifty proposed late features and concludes that the language belongs "most plausibly" to the sixth or fifth century BCE. Its Assyria is remote: it speaks of a "king of Nineveh" (3:6), a title Assyrian royal inscriptions do not use, and calls Nineveh "an exceeding great city of three days' journey" (3:3). Nineveh became the imperial capital only under Sennacherib around 705 BCE, and even then its walls ran about twelve kilometers around, a walk of a few hours rather than three days. The decree that animals too must fast in sackcloth (3:7–8) is comedy or imagination. Nineveh fell in 612 BCE; for a Persian-period audience it was a city out of the past.

Scholars have called the book a parable, a satire, a didactic novella, and a midrash on Exodus 34:6–7. None requires the story to have happened, and all recognize its careful construction. Jonah goes down: down to Joppa, down into the ship, down into its hold (1:3, 5), and eventually down to "the bottoms of the mountains" (2:6). The pagan sailors, who pray and row hard to spare him, end up fearing "the LORD exceedingly" (1:16), more devout than the prophet. The five-word Hebrew oracle in 3:4, "Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown," uses the verb hafakh, the word for Sodom's overthrow, which can also mean "turned" or "changed." In the second sense, the prediction comes true. Phyllis Trible's Rhetorical Criticism (1994) analyzes these patterns at length; Jack M. Sasson's Anchor Bible commentary (1990) and Uriel Simon's JPS commentary (1999) are standard philological treatments.

The psalm in chapter 2 is a well-known problem. It is a thanksgiving psalm, built from phrases familiar from the Psalter, sung before Jonah is back on dry land. Many scholars have treated it as a later insertion. Others read the mismatch as deliberate: Jonah piously recites "Salvation is of the LORD" (2:9) while still showing no change of heart. The "great fish" (dag gadol, 1:17; Hebrew 2:1) became a "whale" only through the Greek kētos of Matthew 12:40.

The argument at the end

The theological center of the book is Jonah 4:2. Jonah explains why he ran: "I knew that thou art a gracious God, and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness, and repentest thee of the evil." This is the creed of Exodus 34:6 (also in Joel 2:13) turned into a complaint; Jonah seems to want Exodus 34:7's punishment of the guilty instead. Why, the book never says. Readers have proposed nationalism (Assyria would destroy Israel), prophetic credibility (by Deuteronomy 18:22 an unfulfilled prediction marks a false prophet), or resentment that repentance can cancel justice. Set beside Persian-period arguments over outsiders (compare Ezra–Nehemiah's marriage reforms), the book reads like one side of a live debate.

The book ends on an unanswered question. God contrasts Jonah's pity for a plant (qiqayon, often identified as the castor-oil plant) that grew and died overnight with his own pity for "more than sixscore thousand persons that cannot discern between their right hand and their left hand; and also much cattle" (4:11). Jonah never replies. Yvonne Sherwood's A Biblical Text and Its Afterlives (2000) traces how readers have filled that silence: Jonah as a type of Christ's three days in the tomb, Jonah as the stubborn Jew of Christian polemic, Jonah as the Yom Kippur model of repentance. That the real Nineveh was later destroyed (Nahum celebrates it) did not trouble the author; the story is about whether God may be merciful, not about Assyrian history.
