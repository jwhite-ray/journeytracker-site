The Council, the Famine, and the Saviors: Amos, Obadiah, and Jonah in Restoration Light

Written by an AI persona connecting the block to Restoration scripture and modern teaching — not a real person.

Amos 3:7 is among the verses most quoted in conference, Obadiah 21 named a branch of temple theology, and Jonah is the Old Testament's missionary story. This essay sets each passage beside the Restoration texts that answer it, and says plainly where a Restoration reading goes beyond what the verse meant in its own setting.

Admitted to the council: Amos 3:7

Amos 3:7 sits at the end of a chain of cause-and-effect questions. A lion roars because it has prey. A bird falls because there was a snare. A trumpet sounds and the city is afraid. Then the conclusion: "Surely the Lord GOD will do nothing, but he revealeth his secret unto his servants the prophets." Verse 8 adds Amos's own sense of compulsion: "The Lord GOD hath spoken, who can but prophesy?"

The word translated "secret" is the Hebrew sod. The Brown-Driver-Briggs lexicon glosses it first as "council, counsel," and only secondarily as secret counsel or intimacy (see the Blue Letter Bible entry for H5475). Jeremiah uses the same word for the test of a true prophet: "who hath stood in the counsel of the Lord, and hath perceived and heard his word?" (Jeremiah 23:18). If they had stood there, "they should have turned them from their evil way" (23:22). On this reading a prophet does more than receive information. He is someone who has been admitted to God's council and comes back out carrying its decisions.

Readers of the Pearl of Great Price will recognize the image. Abraham sees the premortal "intelligences," and God "stood in the midst of them" and said, "These I will make my rulers" (Abraham 3:22–23). That is not the same scene as Amos 3:7, but it shares the conviction that God governs through a council and brings human beings into it.

Joseph Smith's revision of the Bible changes this verse in a small but telling way. In Kent P. Jackson's parallel-column edition of the Joseph Smith Translation, JST Amos 3:7 reads: "Surely the Lord God will do nothing until he revealeth the secret unto his servants the prophets." The change from "but" to "until" turns a general statement into a sequence: revelation comes before the act. The same revision changes verse 6 from "shall there be evil in a city, and the Lord hath not done it?" to "the Lord hath not known it?" That pulls back from making God the author of every calamity. The footnotes in the Church's own edition of Amos record both changes.

In April 1977, President Spencer W. Kimball said that with Joseph Smith "God again spoke to man, revealing anew 'his secret unto his servants the prophets.'" In October 1989, Elder James E. Faust quoted the verse to insist that continuing revelation "does not originate with the prophets; it comes from God," which Doctrine and Covenants 1:38 puts in the Lord's own voice: "whether by mine own voice or by the voice of my servants, it is the same."

"Prophesy not" and the famine that follows

The Restoration reading of Amos 8:11–12 is well known. In this reading, the "famine... of hearing the words of the LORD," in which people "run to and fro to seek the word of the LORD, and shall not find it," foreshadows the Great Apostasy. The lesson itself makes this application. In October 1997, Elder Jeffrey R. Holland declared that "God has through His Only Begotten Son lifted the famine of which Amos spoke," and then testified directly of the First Vision.

That application is legitimate, but it lands harder once the verse's own setting is clear. In Amos the famine is not random. It is the logical end of a choice Israel has already made twice. In 2:12 the Lord's indictment is that "ye... commanded the prophets, saying, Prophesy not." In 7:12–13 the priest Amaziah acts out exactly that charge: "O thou seer, go, flee thee away into the land of Judah... But prophesy not again any more at Beth-el: for it is the king's chapel." The famine of 8:11 gives Israel what it asked for. A people who told the prophets to be quiet get silence.

Samuel the Lamanite, an outsider on the wall as Amos was a Judean in Bethel, names the pattern: when a prophet "testifieth of your sins and iniquities, ye are angry with him, and cast him out," but whoever says "Walk after the pride of your own hearts" they "will receive... and say that he is a prophet" (Helaman 13:26–27). Amaziah's complaint, "the land is not able to bear all his words" (Amos 7:10), is exactly that reaction.

Amaziah also calls Amos a "seer" (hozeh, 7:12) as a dismissal. In the Book of Mormon, King Limhi and Ammon give the word its full weight: "a seer is greater than a prophet," one by whom "secret things [shall] be made manifest" (Mosiah 8:15–17). Amaziah meant an insult and named the office.

At ease in Zion

"Woe to them that are at ease in Zion" (Amos 6:1) reappears in Nephi's prophecy of the last days: "wo be unto him that is at ease in Zion!" (2 Nephi 28:24), paired with the cry "All is well in Zion; yea, Zion prospereth" (28:21). Amos's complacent drink wine in bowls "but they are not grieved for the affliction of Joseph" (6:6); Nephi makes that ease a latter-day danger for the covenant people themselves. Against the merchants who "buy the poor for silver, and the needy for a pair of shoes" (Amos 8:6), King Benjamin asks, "are we not all beggars?" (Mosiah 4:19).

Amos 4:12 belongs in a plan-of-salvation reading. After five refrains of "yet have ye not returned unto me," the Lord says: "prepare to meet thy God, O Israel." In Amos the meeting is a judgment about to break on the nation. Amulek widens it to a whole life: "this life is the time for men to prepare to meet God" (Alma 34:32).

When the Lord "repents"

This week's block contains three passages in which the King James Version says that God "repented." In Amos 7:3 and 7:6, after Amos prays during the visions of locusts and fire, "The LORD repented for this." Jonah 3:10 reads, "God repented of the evil, that he had said that he would do unto them." Joseph Smith revised all three, and the exact wording matters.

In the Jackson edition (matched by the Church's footnotes):

  • JST Amos 7:3: "And the Lord said concerning Jacob, 'Jacob shall repent for this. Therefore I will not utterly destroy him, saith the Lord.'"
  • JST Amos 7:6: "And the Lord said concerning Jacob, 'Jacob shall repent of his wickedness. Therefore I will not utterly destroy him, saith the Lord God.'"
  • JST Jonah 3:9: "Who can tell, if we will repent and turn unto God, but he will turn away from us his fierce anger that we perish not?"
  • JST Jonah 3:10: "And God saw their works, that they turned from their evil way and repented. And God turned away the evil that he had said he would bring upon them."

Every time, the repenting moves from God to the people; God's change of course becomes His answer to human change. That fits the Book of Mormon's insistence that God "changeth not; if so he would cease to be God" (Mormon 9:19), and the Church's footnote at Amos 7:3 cross-references Numbers 23:19: God is not "the son of man, that he should repent."

Two points keep the picture honest. First, the Hebrew verb here (nḥm) is not the word for repentance from sin; it means to relent or change a stated course. The JST heads off a misreading of the English "repented" more than it corrects the Hebrew. Second, the revision is not applied everywhere. The Church's edition has no JST note at Jonah 4:2, where Jonah throws the old creed back at God: "thou art a gracious God, and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness, and repentest thee of the evil." Yet the JST does revise the same formula in Joel 2:13, so that it ends "he will turn away the evil from you." Readers should treat the JST as Joseph Smith's inspired working-through of particular passages, not as a mechanical global edit.

The JST's "I will not utterly destroy him" also echoes Amos 9:8, "saving that I will not utterly destroy the house of Jacob," tying the visions to the book's closing promise of sifting and gathering.

Many nations, one Father

Amos 9:7 is one of the most surprising verses in the prophets: "Are ye not as children of the Ethiopians unto me, O children of Israel?... Have not I brought up Israel out of the land of Egypt? and the Philistines from Caphtor, and the Syrians from Kir?" It sits uncomfortably beside 3:2, "You only have I known of all the families of the earth." The Book of Mormon holds both ideas: "Know ye not that there are more nations than one?... I remember one nation like unto another" (2 Nephi 29:7–8), and "the Lord doth grant unto all nations, of their own nation and tongue, to teach his word" (Alma 29:8). In the Restoration, chosenness is a calling to carry something to the others, not a claim to be loved more.

That is the issue at the center of Jonah. The book ends on God's question about Nineveh's "sixscore thousand persons that cannot discern between their right hand and their left hand; and also much cattle" (4:11). The lesson sets Jonah beside the sons of Mosiah, who "could not bear that any human soul should perish" (Mosiah 28:3). It also sets him beside Ammon, who went to his nation's enemies and said to their king, "Nay, but I will be thy servant" (Alma 17:25). The contrast is real. Jonah's anger in chapter 4 is not that the message failed. It is that it worked.

Forsaking one's own mercy

Jonah 2 belongs in a doctrinal reading for two reasons. The first is the temple. From the depths Jonah says, "yet I will look again toward thy holy temple" (2:4), and "my prayer came in unto thee, into thine holy temple" (2:7). The second is the line Elder Matthew S. Holland used as the title of his October 2025 talk: "They that observe lying vanities forsake their own mercy" (2:8).

Elder Holland read "their own mercy" as a personal gift. He described it as "perfectly suited to you, designed to relieve your individual agonies and heal your particular pains." In his notes he observes that the Hebrew word for mercy here is hesed, linking it to President Russell M. Nelson's teaching (Liahona, October 2022) that "all those who have made a covenant with God have access to a special kind of love and mercy. In the Hebrew language, that covenantal love is called hesed." The same word comes back in 4:2, where Jonah complains that God is "of great kindness," literally abundant in hesed. Jonah wants covenant mercy for himself in chapter 2 and resents God's giving it to Nineveh in chapter 4. The book does not resolve that tension. It leaves it with the reader.

Jonah goes "down to Joppa," "down into" the ship, "down into the sides of the ship" (1:3, 5), and at last "down to the bottoms of the mountains," crying "out of the belly of hell" (2:2, 6), before "thou brought up my life from corruption." Jesus took those three days as the one sign He would give (Matthew 12:39–40), and the Lord's word in Liberty Jail names what the descent points to: "The Son of Man hath descended below them all" (Doctrine and Covenants 122:8).

Saviours on Mount Zion

Obadiah's twenty-one verses of judgment on Edom end with a line that has had a long Restoration life: "And saviours shall come up on mount Zion to judge the mount of Esau; and the kingdom shall be the LORD's" (v. 21).

In context, the "saviours" (moshiʻim) are deliverers like the judges of Israel. Nehemiah 9:27 uses the same word: "thou gavest them saviours, who saved them out of the hand of their enemies." The verse is about rescue and God's rule after Edom's fall; it says nothing directly about the dead. The Restoration reading is an expansion, and it is better to say so. But it is not arbitrary. Edom's crime was the opposite of saving: it "stood in the crossway, to cut off those of his that did escape" (v. 14). The saviours of verse 21 reverse that betrayal and open the way.

Joseph Smith made that verse a temple charter. In a sermon of January 21, 1844, in Nauvoo, as recorded by Wilford Woodruff, he connected Malachi's Elijah to Obadiah: "The keys are to be delivered, the spirit of Elijah is to come, the Gospel to be established, the Saints of God gathered, Zion built up, and the Saints to come up as saviors on Mount Zion." He then answered his own question: "But how are they to become saviors on Mount Zion? By building their temples, erecting their baptismal fonts, and going forth and receiving all the ordinances... in behalf of all their progenitors who are dead." The doctrinal reason is in his 1842 letter now canonized as Doctrine and Covenants 128: "they without us cannot be made perfect—neither can we without our dead be made perfect" (128:15). President Joseph F. Smith's vision supplies the other side of the veil: the dead who repent "will be redeemed, through obedience to the ordinances of the house of God" (Doctrine and Covenants 138:58).

President Gordon B. Hinckley stated the doctrine most fully in his October 2004 closing remarks, the talk this week's lesson cites:

We literally become saviors on Mount Zion. What does this mean? Just as our Redeemer gave His life as a vicarious sacrifice for all men, and in so doing became our Savior, even so we, in a small measure, when we engage in proxy work in the temple, become as saviors to those on the other side who have no means of advancing unless something is done in their behalf by those on earth.

The qualifier "in a small measure" matters. The saviours of Obadiah 21 are derivative; their work has power only because of the one Savior whose kingdom it finally is: "and the kingdom shall be the LORD's." Doctrine and Covenants 103:9 extends the calling to the living: the Saints "were set to be a light unto the world, and to be the saviors of men." Taken together, the three books describe what that calling requires: admission to God's council (Amos), mercy that reaches past one's own nation (Jonah), and a reversal of Edom's betrayal by opening the way for others, including the dead (Obadiah).
