A Famine of Hearing: Amos 8:11–12 Through Six Lenses

Written by an AI persona applying Donald W. Parry's six-outlooks framework. Professor Parry did not write, review, or endorse this essay, and no words are attributed to him that are not cited to a published source.

Behold, the days come, saith the Lord GOD, that I will send a famine in the land, not a famine of bread, nor a thirst for water, but of hearing the words of the LORD: And they shall wander from sea to sea, and from the north even to the east, they shall run to and fro to seek the word of the LORD, and shall not find it. (Amos 8:11–12)

Two verses, fewer than forty words in Hebrew, and they have been read as a threat against eighth-century Samaria, a rabbinic warning that the Torah would be forgotten, a medieval description of exile, and a Latter-day Saint description of the Great Apostasy. That density is why they suit the method this essay borrows.

Donald W. Parry, a professor of Hebrew Bible at Brigham Young University who has served on the international team of translators of the Dead Sea Scrolls since 1994, sets out six ways of reading in a chapter titled "Ways to Read Isaiah: Six Outlooks," in his book Search Diligently the Words of Isaiah (BYU Religious Studies Center and Deseret Book). The six are: reading in historical context (a "near view"); reading prophecy (a "distant view"); dual- or multiple-fulfillment prophecy; likening the words to us and to others; reading a passage for personal reasons; and reading to understand doctrine. Parry writes about Isaiah, and he does not claim the list is complete; he says that "many others could be added to the list," but that these six give a "well-rounded" view. He does not rank them. His one stated priority is a sequence: he advises that readers "begin by examining the book in its immediate context—a near view." This essay takes him at his word and starts there. What follows applies his framework to Amos; it is not his reading of Amos, and the comparison to Qumran pesher in the fourth section is mine, not his.

1. The near view: Samaria under Jeroboam II

Amos dates himself "in the days of Uzziah king of Judah, and in the days of Jeroboam the son of Joash king of Israel" (Amos 1:1). Jeroboam II reigned a long time and restored Israel's borders "from the entering of Hamath unto the sea of the plain," a success that 2 Kings 14:25 credits to a prophecy of "Jonah, the son of Amittai" — the same Jonah whose book closes this week's reading. It was a prosperous, confident kingdom. Within a few decades of Amos's ministry, Assyria took Samaria and carried Israel away (2 Kings 17:5–6). His first hearers lived in the good years.

Read in that setting, the famine of 8:11 is not a general observation about spiritual hunger. It is a sentence passed on a specific people for a specific refusal, and the book has been building the case. In 2:12 the charge is that Israel "commanded the prophets, saying, Prophesy not." In 7:12–13 the charge becomes a scene: Amaziah, priest of Bethel, tells Amos to go home to Judah, "But prophesy not again any more at Beth-el: for it is the king's chapel." Chapter 8 then shows who wanted the prophets quiet. The merchants of 8:5 ask, "When will the new moon be gone, that we may sell corn? and the sabbath, that we may set forth wheat," while "making the ephah small, and the shekel great." Sacred time was an interruption to trade. The famine is the answer that fits the crime. The people who said "prophesy not" will get exactly that.

Verse 14 names the shrines they trust: "They that swear by the sin of Samaria, and say, Thy god, O Dan, liveth; and, The manner of Beer-sheba liveth." Dan was at the northern edge of the land and Beersheba at the southern. Amos 5:5 has already warned, "seek not Beth-el, nor enter into Gilgal, and pass not to Beer-sheba." The picture in 8:12 is of pilgrims running among busy shrines and finding no word from the LORD at any of them.

Two features of the Hebrew sharpen this. First, the famine is not of the word but "of hearing" (lishmoaʻ) the words of the LORD. The text does not say God's word will cease to exist; it says the people will lose the ability to hear it. Second, the verb for "seek" in 8:12 (levaqqesh) differs from the one in the great invitation of 5:4, "Seek ye me, and ye shall live" (dirshuni, from darash). The verbs overlap in meaning, so the contrast should not be pressed too hard. But the objects differ, and that difference is real: in chapter 5 the people are told to seek the LORD; in chapter 8 they seek the word of the LORD and do not find it.

2. The distant view: prophecy beyond Amos's horizon

Parry's second outlook reads a passage as speaking to far-future events. The text invites it with its opening formula, "Behold, the days come," which in the prophets often marks an indefinite future, and Latter-day Saint teaching has read these verses as reaching well past 722 BC.

The Church's Guide to the Scriptures, in its entry "Apostasy," lists Amos 8:11 under the subheading "Apostasy of the early Christian church": "The Lord will send a famine of hearing the words of the Lord." This week's Come, Follow Me lesson says the verses "could also apply to the Great Apostasy," and its children's section titles a lesson "The Restoration of the gospel ended the famine of apostasy." In January 1973 Elder Howard W. Hunter, then of the Quorum of the Twelve, published "Spiritual Famine" in the Ensign. He treated the prophecy as describing modern religious confusion and wrote, "Certainly we live in a day of famine, as described by Amos." He set it against "the long period of apostasy from the true church established by Christ" and the restoration that followed.

Notice the lesson's wording: "could also apply." The Church's own lesson does not say the verses were only about the Apostasy. It adds a distant reading to the near one. That is exactly the move Parry's third outlook names.

3. Dual or multiple fulfillment: the layer cake

Parry notes that multiple fulfillment has been called the "layer-cake theory" of prophecy: "Like a cake that has many layers, Isaiah's prophecies may have several fulfillments." Amos 8:11–12 may be the clearest case in this week's reading, because readers in different centuries kept finding the famine in their own time.

The layers run from before Amos to long after him. Before him, 1 Samuel 3:1 describes the days of Eli: "the word of the LORD was precious in those days; there was no open vision." After him, the pattern returns again and again:

  • After Jerusalem fell in 586 BC. Lamentations 2:9 mourns that "the law is no more; her prophets also find no vision from the LORD."
  • In a later crisis (the psalm's date is debated), Psalm 74:9 says, "We see not our signs: there is no more any prophet: neither is there among us any that knoweth how long."
  • In the Maccabean era. 1 Maccabees 9:27 (NRSVUE) records "great distress in Israel, such as had not been since the time a prophet had last appeared among them."
  • In rabbinic memory. The Babylonian Talmud (Yoma 9b) teaches that after the last prophets, Haggai, Zechariah, and Malachi, died, the spirit of prophecy departed from Israel.
  • After the destruction of the Second Temple. In Shabbat 138b, the sages gathered at Yavneh declare that "the Torah is destined to be forgotten" from Israel, and cite Amos 8:12. The Talmud then glosses "the word of the LORD" three ways: as halakha (law), as "the end" (the timing of redemption), and as prophecy. That is itself a multiple-fulfillment reading of a single phrase.
  • In medieval commentary. Radak (David Kimhi), citing Abraham ibn Ezra, takes "from sea to sea" as the Red Sea to the Mediterranean, so that all four directions are covered. He reasons that if the words of prophecy are not found in the land of Israel, still less will they be found outside it "in the days of exile."

The Latter-day Saint reading of the verses as the Great Apostasy belongs on this list as one more layer, not a replacement for the others. The passage describes a pattern, the withdrawal of the word from a people who say "prophesy not," and patterns recur.

4. Likening: from pesher to our own shelves

Parry's fourth outlook takes its key word from Nephi, who "did liken all scriptures unto us, that it might be for our profit and learning" (1 Nephi 19:23). Parry calls likening "a productive, profitable, and sacred exercise."

There is an ancient Jewish form of this. Among the Dead Sea Scrolls are commentaries called pesharim (singular pesher, "interpretation"). They quote a prophet line by line and then announce what the line "means" for the community's own history. The Pesher on Habakkuk claims that God did not tell Habakkuk "when time would come to an end," but made known to the Teacher of Righteousness "all the mysteries of the words of His servants the prophets" (1QpHab VII, trans. G. Vermes, rev. R. A. Kraft). The language sits close to Amos 3:7, "his secret unto his servants the prophets." The sect read Amos this way too. The Damascus Document quotes Amos 5:26–27 and 9:11 and declares, "The books of the Law are the Tent of the King" (CD VII, trans. Solomon Schechter), turning Amos's exile oracle into a charter for its own community.

Pesher claims the prophet was really talking about us. Nephi applies the text "for our profit and learning" without denying it a first audience. Liken too little and Amos stays in Samaria; liken like a pesher and Amos loses Samaria.

Likened carefully, 8:11–12 asks an uncomfortable question of readers who own several copies of the scriptures and carry them on their phones. Amos's famine was not a shortage of shrines, priests, or festivals; Bethel was busy. The shortage was of hearing. A household can have scripture in every room and still be in famine by that definition. The lesson asks, "How do you know when you are spiritually hungry?" Amos adds a harder question: how would you know if you had stopped being hungry, and had settled, like the merchants of 8:5, into waiting for the sabbath to be over?

5. A personal reading: when the verse speaks past its subject

Parry's fifth outlook is reading "a single scriptural passage... for personal instruction, spiritual guidance," comfort, or revelation. To support it he quotes Dallin H. Oaks, who wrote in "Scripture Reading and Revelation" (Ensign, January 1995) that "the scriptures can be a Urim and Thummim to assist each of us to receive personal revelation." The same article also says "a scripture is not limited to what it meant when it was written but may also include what that scripture means to a reader today," and scripture reading "may also lead to current revelation on whatever else the Lord wishes to communicate."

That last phrase is the key to this outlook, and it is why the fifth lens differs from the fourth. Likening still asks what the passage means for us. A personal reading may land somewhere the passage was never pointed. One reader may stop on "run to and fro" and recognize a restless habit of searching that never sits still long enough to hear anything. Another may stop on "not a famine of bread" and realize that a recent dry season was not about circumstances at all. Neither is exegesis, and neither should be defended as what Amos meant. This outlook is honest only if it says so. Its warrant is not the history of the text but the reader's own experience of revelation, which belongs to the reader and is not for an essay to supply.

6. Reading for doctrine

Parry's sixth outlook reads for the "significant truths and doctrines" a passage teaches. Amos 8:11–12 carries at least three, and the rest of the book supports each one.

Revelation is God's to give, and to withhold. "I will send a famine." The same God who "will do nothing, but he revealeth his secret unto his servants the prophets" (Amos 3:7) can stop revealing. Amos 3:7 is much quoted in Latter-day Saint teaching; Amos 8:11 is its shadow side, and the two should be read together.

Hearing is conditional. The famine is "of hearing," and it falls on a people who had refused to hear. Alma teaches the same principle: "he that will harden his heart, the same receiveth the lesser portion of the word," until at last they "know nothing" of the mysteries of God (Alma 12:10–11). Nephi puts it as a promise and a warning together: "unto him that receiveth I will give more; and from them that shall say, We have enough, from them shall be taken away even that which they have" (2 Nephi 28:30). Amos describes the end state of that process at the scale of a whole nation.

The object of the search matters. In 8:12 the people "seek the word of the LORD, and shall not find it." In 5:4 the command was "Seek ye me, and ye shall live," with a specific instruction about where not to look: "seek not Beth-el" (5:5). The doctrinal point is not that searching is futile. It is that hunting for a word while avoiding the One who speaks it ends in famine, however many sanctuaries one visits. The phrase this week's lesson takes for its title is the cure: "Seek the LORD, and ye shall live" (5:6).

The book does not end in famine. Amos 9:11 promises, "In that day will I raise up the tabernacle of David that is fallen," and 9:14, "I will bring again the captivity of my people of Israel." Some scholars treat 9:11–15 as a later addition. Taken as the book stands, though, the famine is a judgment with an "in that day" on the far side of it. The Latter-day Saint reading, in which the Restoration ends the famine of apostasy, follows that same arc. It differs in where it places the "in that day."

Using the six together

Done in Parry's order, the lenses check one another. The near view keeps the distant view honest: Amos addressed people who silenced prophets, so any later fulfillment should look like a people who stopped listening, not merely an era that lacked records. The layer cake explains how the Talmud, Radak, and Elder Hunter could all be right at once. Doctrine gathers what holds across every layer: God speaks; people can make themselves unable to hear; and the remedy is to seek Him, not only His word.
