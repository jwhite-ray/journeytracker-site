Where the Text Pushes Back: Hard Questions in Amos, Obadiah, and Jonah

Written by an AI persona that surfaces the hard questions honestly — not a real person.

These three books are short, and every one of them contains something that has troubled careful readers for a long time. A man lives three days inside a fish. A prophet curses a neighboring nation down to the last survivor. God "repents." A verse about prophets that Latter-day Saints quote often sits in a book that also shows God changing course after a prophet argues with him. What follows states each problem at full strength, lays out the range of answers, and leaves open what the text leaves open.

The fish, the city, and the question of genre

The objection most people meet first is the plainest one: a man cannot survive three days inside a fish (Jonah 1:17). Some well-meaning defenses have made things worse. For about a century, sermons and conservative commentaries told the story of James Bartley, a nineteenth-century whaler supposedly swallowed by a sperm whale and later cut out alive. The historian of science Edward B. Davis traced the story through ship records and newspapers. He found that it falls apart: the records he examined do not support it ("A Whale of a Tale: Fundamentalist Fish Stories," 1991). A reader who defends the fish with a false story is in a weaker spot than one who simply says, "the LORD had prepared a great fish." The text presents the fish as a miracle, not a fact of marine biology. The Hebrew says dag gadol, "great fish," not "whale."

For scholars the fish is not the main difficulty. The book calls the ruler "the king of Nineveh" (3:6), a title Assyrian kings did not use; elsewhere the Bible calls them "the king of Assyria." In 3:7 the decree goes out from "the king and his nobles," which some commentators think sounds more like a later Persian-period court. The book also says that "Nineveh was an exceeding great city of three days' journey" (3:3). The Encyclopedia of the Bible's article on Nineveh gives the city wall as about seven and three-quarter miles around. You could walk that in an afternoon. Many critical scholars combine these details with late features of the Hebrew and date the book after the exile. On that view it is a didactic story, not a report. Jack M. Sasson's Anchor Bible commentary (1990) is the standard technical treatment.

The other side has answers. The same encyclopedia article suggests that "three days' journey" may describe the whole district Nineveh governed, not just the walled city. Paul J. N. Lawrence argued in the Tyndale Bulletin (1986) that "king of Nineveh" and "king and nobles" fit an Assyrian setting in the early eighth century. That is the era 2 Kings 14:25 assigns to a real Jonah son of Amittai. The honest summary is that the evidence leans toward a late, literary composition, but not so far that a historical reading is foolish.

The Church's own reference works differ. The Bible Dictionary entry "Jonah" says the book is not presented as Jonah's own writing and was probably written by a later author. It reads the book as a challenge to a narrow view that would limit God's love to one nation. The Old Testament Student Manual: Kings–Malachi calls Jonah's story "a true one, and not an allegory as some scholars maintain." It quotes Joseph Fielding Smith: "I believe, as did Mr. William J. Bryan, the story of Jonah." His main reason was that Jesus believed it (Doctrines of Salvation, 2:314–15). A chapter published by BYU's Religious Studies Center took a looser view. LeGrande Davies wrote that whether Jonah is "historical or unhistorical, literal or allegorical, is strictly a matter of personal preference" ("Jonah: Testimony of the Resurrection," 1984). A reader who settles on either position is in good company.

"A greater than Jonas is here"

The strongest argument for historicity comes from the New Testament: "For as Jonas was three days and three nights in the whale's belly; so shall the Son of man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth. The men of Nineveh shall rise in judgment with this generation, and shall condemn it: because they repented at the preaching of Jonas; and, behold, a greater than Jonas is here" (Matthew 12:40–41; compare 16:4; Luke 11:29–32). Joseph Fielding Smith's reasoning is straightforward. If Jesus treated Jonah as real, so should we. The second sentence carries the most weight. A fictional city, the argument goes, cannot rise in judgment against a real generation.

The counterargument deserves a hearing too. Teachers often cite characters everyone knows to make a moral point, and the force of the point does not depend on whether the character existed. "Even the people of Nineveh repented" works as a rebuke either way. The time reference also creates a problem for literalists. Jesus was buried Friday evening and rose before dawn Sunday. That covers parts of three days but only two nights, while the same Gospel elsewhere says he would rise "the third day" (Matthew 16:21). Most interpreters solve this by treating "three days and three nights" as an idiom that counts part of a day as a whole day. Neither reading of Matthew 12 forces the other. The sign works as a type of the Resurrection under both.

A God who repents, and a prophet who resents it

The KJV says plainly that "God repented of the evil, that he had said that he would do unto them; and he did it not" (Jonah 3:10). It says the same in Amos: "The LORD repented for this: It shall not be, saith the LORD" (Amos 7:3, 6). Yet Numbers 23:19 insists that God is "not... the son of man, that he should repent." And 1 Samuel 15 manages both in one chapter: "the Strength of Israel will not lie nor repent" (15:29), and then "the LORD repented that he had made Saul king" (15:35).

Joseph Smith's revision noticed the problem. The Latter-day Saint edition's footnotes give JST Amos 7:3 as "the Lord said, concerning Jacob, Jacob shall repent for this, therefore I will not utterly destroy him." On this reading Israel does the repenting, not God. JST Jonah 3:10 makes the Ninevites the ones who "repented," and "God turned away the evil that he had said he would bring upon them." For many Latter-day Saints that settles it: the KJV wording was a problem of translation or theology, and the Prophet fixed it.

The Hebrew verb, though, is nicham. It means to relent, to be moved to pity, or to change one's course. It does not mean to confess a sin. And the book of Jonah needs God to relent. In 4:2 Jonah throws Exodus 34:6 back at God and adds a line of his own: "I knew that thou art a gracious God, and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness, and repentest thee of the evil." Jeremiah 18:7–8 states it as a rule: "If that nation, against whom I have pronounced, turn from their evil, I will repent of the evil that I thought to do unto them." Amos 7 is even bolder. A prophet prays, "O Lord GOD, forgive, I beseech thee: by whom shall Jacob arise? for he is small," and the divine plan changes. Some find that more beautiful than an unmoving God; others find it unsettling. The JST and the Hebrew point in different theological directions, and a careful reader can hold both without pretending they say the same thing.

Jonah's oracle, "Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown" (3:4), did not come to pass. By the test in Deuteronomy 18:22 ("if the thing follow not, nor come to pass, that is the thing which the LORD hath not spoken"), Jonah looks like a false prophet. Perhaps that is part of his anger. Others point out that the Hebrew verb for "overthrown," nehpakhet, can also mean "turned around," so the prophecy came true in a way Jonah did not intend. Rabbinic tradition offers a third reading, more sympathetic to Jonah. The Mekhilta of Rabbi Ishmael, commenting on Exodus 12:1, says Jonah fled because the Gentiles were "close to repentance." If they repented quickly, their response would condemn Israel, which had not repented. On that reading Jonah is not a bigot but a patriot afraid of what Nineveh's virtue would mean for his own people. There is also the historical sequel: Nineveh fell about 612 BC, and the book of Nahum celebrates its fall. Did the repentance fail, or did it buy one generation of mercy? The book does not answer. It ends with God's question (4:11), and Jonah never replies.

Amos 3:7 and the problem of fallible prophets

"Surely the Lord GOD will do nothing, but he revealeth his secret unto his servants the prophets" (Amos 3:7) is probably the Old Testament verse Latter-day Saints quote most often about modern prophets. This week's lesson opens with it. The word translated "secret" is sod, which means the council, the private deliberations of God's court. Jeremiah 23:18 and 22 use it the same way, asking who "hath stood in the counsel of the LORD." In context, Amos is defending his own disturbing message. The surrounding chain of cause and effect (a lion roars only over prey, a trap springs only when tripped) ends with a prophet who speaks only because God has spoken. "The lion hath roared, who will not fear? the Lord GOD hath spoken, who can but prophesy?" (3:8).

The difficulty comes when the verse is read as a broad guarantee. If God does nothing without revealing it to the prophets, then what about teachings and policies Church leaders have later reversed? The Church's own materials give a qualified answer. The Doctrine and Covenants Student Manual quotes Joseph Smith: "A prophet was a prophet only when he was acting as such." Doctrine and Covenants 1:25 expects that the Lord's servants will err: "inasmuch as they erred it might be made known." In October 2013 President Dieter F. Uchtdorf said in general conference, "to be perfectly frank, there have been times when members or leaders in the Church have simply made mistakes" ("Come, Join with Us").

Amos 7 complicates the verse from within the book. The revealing in 3:7 goes both ways. The prophet does not only receive the council's decisions; he argues with them, and twice he wins (7:1–6). Then Amaziah, the official priest at "the king's chapel," tells the true prophet to go home (7:12–13). Amos answers, "I was no prophet, neither was I a prophet's son" (7:14). The Hebrew has no verb, so it could be "I am no prophet," a rejection of the professional guild, or "I was no prophet" until God called him. Either way, the book that gives us 3:7 also shows institutional religion and prophetic speech on opposite sides. Faithful readers find in 3:7 a promise that God will not leave his people without warning. Critics see a verse stretched past its setting. Both should notice that Amos himself was the outsider.

A famine for whom?

The lesson reads Amos 8:11–12 ("a famine... of hearing the words of the LORD") as applying "also" to the Great Apostasy, and the Church's Topics entry "Apostasy" lists it among its references. The lesson's "also" is careful. In Amos the famine falls on the northern kingdom of the eighth century BC. That kingdom was "making the ephah small, and the shekel great" (8:5) and swearing by "Thy god, O Dan" (8:14). The people would "run to and fro to seek the word of the LORD, and shall not find it" because they had already told the prophets, "Prophesy not" (2:12; 7:16). The famine is a consequence of rejecting a voice that was speaking, not a long silence spread over the centuries.

Critics say that using the verse as a proof-text for a world-wide apostasy lasting more than a thousand years removes it from its context. Defenders answer that scripture often applies a historical pattern to later times. The New Testament does this with Amos itself, using Amos 9:11–12 for the Gentile mission in Acts 15:16–17. The pattern does hold: people who silence prophets end up without them. But a reader who meets the verse only as an apostasy proof should know that Amos was speaking to people who sold "the poor for a pair of shoes" (2:6; 8:6), and the famine came because of that.

Obadiah: revenge against a brother?

Obadiah's twenty-one verses against Edom end with "there shall not be any remaining of the house of Esau" (v. 18). The charges are specific. Edom stood by when Jerusalem fell, gloated, looted, and waited "in the crossway, to cut off those of his that did escape" and hand them over (vv. 11–14; compare Psalm 137:7). The moral principle is clear: "as thou hast done, it shall be done unto thee" (v. 15).

Two hard questions arise. The first is ethical. Deuteronomy 23:7 commands, "Thou shalt not abhor an Edomite; for he is thy brother," and Proverbs 24:17 warns, "Rejoice not when thine enemy falleth." Obadiah condemns Edom for gloating, and much of the book reads like gloating in return. Some readers accept it as the honest cry of victims, which scripture does not tidy up, much like Psalm 137. Others think a total-destruction oracle against a whole people is something a modern conscience should not take on without question. The second question is historical. J. R. Bartlett argued in 1982 that the usual picture of Edom as Babylon's eager partner in 587/586 BC overstates the case ("Edom and the Fall of Jerusalem, 587 B.C."). If that is right, Obadiah may express Judah's sense of betrayal more than an exact historical record. History also complicates "not any remaining." The Edomites, later called Idumeans, did not vanish. The Hasmonean ruler John Hyrcanus forced them to adopt Judaism (Josephus, Antiquities 13.257–58), and Herod the Great descended from them.

Latter-day Saint reading turns the last verse in a different direction. In the Hebrew, the "saviours" (moshiʻim) who "come up on mount Zion to judge the mount of Esau" (v. 21) are deliverers like the judges in Nehemiah 9:27. President Gordon B. Hinckley applied the phrase to proxy temple work: "We literally become saviors on Mount Zion" ("Closing Remarks," October 2004). A verse about judging Esau becomes a verse about redeeming the dead, Esau's descendants presumably among them. Whether you see that as inspired reinterpretation or as reading against the text, it is the most generous possible thing to do with Obadiah. It also fits the spirit of Jonah, the book that comes next.

Leaving the questions open

Jonah 4:11 ends the block with a question no one answers: "should not I spare Nineveh?" The fish, God's relenting, the prophet's authority, and the curse on Edom each allow more than one faithful response, and each has been read by serious people in opposite ways. What the text does not allow is pretending the questions are not there.
