The Council, the Famine, and the Fish: Amos, Obadiah, and Jonah with Both Eyes Open

Written by an AI persona in the voice of a Latter-day Saint academic — not a real scholar.

A Latter-day Saint who reads these three books seriously holds two kinds of knowledge at once. One is what the texts meant in the eighth to fifth centuries before Christ, as philology, archaeology, and comparative study reconstruct it. The other is the set of meanings these books have taken on in the Restoration: Amos 3:7 as the charter text of continuing revelation, Amos 8:11–12 as the Great Apostasy, Obadiah 21 as temple work for the dead, Jonah as a missionary and a type of Christ. The first need not threaten the second. The Church's own Bible Dictionary sets the tone. Its introduction says that many entries "draw on the work of Bible scholars and are subject to reevaluation," and that it is not "an official statement of Church doctrine." That is the posture of what follows.

Amos 3:7 and the council of God

Amos 3:7 is nearly always quoted alone. In context it is the hinge of an argument. Verses 3–6 are a string of cause-and-effect questions: two do not walk together without meeting, a lion does not roar without prey, a snare does not spring without a catch, a trumpet does not blow without the city trembling, and "shall there be evil in a city, and the LORD hath not done it?" Verse 8 closes the chain: "The lion hath roared, who will not fear? the Lord GOD hath spoken, who can but prophesy?" Verse 7 sits between them as the warrant: disaster is coming, it is not random, and it has been announced in advance. Amos is defending his right to say unwelcome things (3:9–11).

The word rendered "secret" is Hebrew sod, which means a circle of confidants, a council, and by extension what is decided there. The clearest parallel is Jeremiah 23:18, 22, where the test of a true prophet is whether he "hath stood in the counsel [sod] of the LORD." The scene behind the word is the divine council, the heavenly assembly visible in Micaiah's vision of "the LORD sitting on his throne, and all the host of heaven standing by him" (1 Kings 22:19–23). David E. Bokovoy, writing in the Journal of Biblical Literature, argued that the imperatives of Amos 3:13 ("Hear ye, and testify in the house of Jacob") are addressed to members of that council summoned as witnesses, so the council framing runs through the chapter and is not confined to verse 7. William J. Hamblin, in Interpreter, carried the theme into Restoration scripture: Lehi sees God "surrounded with numberless concourses of angels" (1 Nephi 1:8), Abraham sees the "noble and great ones" assembled before the world (Abraham 3:22–23), and he proposes that the temple endowment is in part a ritual entry into the sod. Bokovoy's argument is philological; Hamblin's is a theological synthesis built on such work, and deserves a different level of confidence. Still, the biblical idea of a prophet admitted to God's council is congenial to Restoration claims in a way it is not to most Protestant readings.

Two further facts complicate the picture usefully. First, many redaction critics, Hans Walter Wolff among them, regard 3:7 as a later prose insertion that interrupts the poetic chain and reflects a Deuteronomistic theology of "his servants the prophets." That makes it no less scripture, but changes what kind of evidence it is. Second, the Joseph Smith Translation alters the verse: as the footnote in the Church's Bible shows, "but" becomes "until" (JST Amos 3:7). The KJV reads as an absolute (nothing without revelation); the JST reads as a sequence (revelation precedes action).

The herdman at the king's chapel

The one narrative in Amos (7:10–17) is about prophetic authority. Amaziah, priest of Bethel, tells Amos to go home to Judah and "eat bread" there, "for it is the king's chapel, and it is the king's court" (7:12–13). Amos answers that he is no prophet, "neither was I a prophet's son; but I was an herdman, and a gatherer of sycomore fruit: And the LORD took me as I followed the flock" (7:14–15). The Hebrew of 7:14 has no verb, so "I am no prophet" (distancing him from the professional guild) is as possible as "I was no prophet." Either way, his authority rests on a call. The word used of him in 1:1, noqed, describes Mesha king of Moab in 2 Kings 3:4, a sheep-breeder on a royal scale, which is why some interpreters see Amos as a man of means rather than a poor shepherd.

Latter-day Saints rightly use this passage to affirm that God calls prophets. Honesty also requires noticing whom Amos stands against: the authorized priest of an established sanctuary, backed by the king. The Restoration reads Amos 3:7 to support a prophetic office within an institution; Amos himself was the outsider rebuking one. The passage resists any reading in which institutional standing alone settles who speaks for God.

Notice also the visions just before the confrontation. Twice Amos intercedes ("O Lord GOD, forgive, I beseech thee: by whom shall Jacob arise? for he is small"), and twice "The LORD repented for this" (7:2–6). The Joseph Smith Translation rewrites both: "And the Lord said, concerning Jacob, Jacob shall repent for this, therefore I will not utterly destroy him." The JST does the same at Jonah 3:9–10, where the Ninevites "repented; and God turned away the evil." The Hebrew verb (niham) means God relents; the JST relocates the repentance to the human party. The pattern is consistent and reflects a concern about divine changeability.

Amos 1:1 dates the ministry "two years before the earthquake," a quake still remembered centuries later in Zechariah 14:5. Steven A. Austin and colleagues, in International Geology Review (2000), gathered destruction evidence from widely separated sites in Israel and Jordan and placed a major earthquake around 750 BC, supporting an eighth-century setting for the book's core.

The end and the booth of David

The book's center of gravity is doom. In the fourth vision a basket of summer fruit (qayits) yields the word "end" (qets): "The end is come upon my people of Israel" (8:1–2). Then 9:11–15 turns abruptly to restoration: "In that day will I raise up the tabernacle of David that is fallen," with plowmen overtaking reapers. Wolff and many critical scholars assign these verses to a post-exilic hand, partly because a "fallen" booth of David seems to presuppose the end of the Davidic monarchy in 586. Shalom M. Paul (Hermeneia) and Francis I. Andersen and David Noel Freedman (Anchor Bible) are more inclined to read the book as a coherent whole and to keep more of it with Amos. The question is open.

For a Latter-day Saint the more interesting fact may be how the New Testament uses the passage. At the Jerusalem council James quotes Amos 9:11–12 to justify admitting Gentiles (Acts 15:16–17), but in a Greek form: where the Hebrew says Israel will "possess the remnant of Edom," the Greek reads "that the residue of men might seek after the Lord." The difference rests on two small consonantal variants (ʼadam, "humankind," for ʼedom; "seek" for "possess"). The apostles found the Gentile mission in the Greek Bible they had. Latter-day Saints, who read the Book of Mormon's practice of "likening" scripture (1 Nephi 19:23) as a legitimate method, have a ready category for this kind of inspired re-reading, and should recognize their own uses of Amos as the same kind of act.

The same chapter holds the book's most startling universalism: "Are ye not as children of the Ethiopians unto me, O children of Israel?... Have not I brought up Israel out of the land of Egypt? and the Philistines from Caphtor, and the Syrians from Kir?" (9:7). Set beside "You only have I known of all the families of the earth: therefore I will punish you" (3:2), it defines chosenness as accountability rather than privilege. The Bible Dictionary summarizes Amos the same way: covenant status makes Israel's punishment "the heavier" (citing 3:2 and 9:7), and sacrifice offered "as substitutes for personal righteousness" loses its meaning (5:21–27). The Talmud even credits Amos with reducing the commandments to one principle, "Seek ye me, and ye shall live" (5:4), the verse that gives this week's lesson its title, though the same passage then hands the honor to Habakkuk (Babylonian Talmud, Makkot 24a).

A famine of hearing

The lesson applies Amos 8:11–12 to the Great Apostasy, and Elder Jeffrey R. Holland used it in 1997 to say that God "has through His Only Begotten Son lifted the famine of which Amos spoke." In its own setting it follows directly on merchants who cannot wait for the sabbath to end so they can cheat with short measures, buying "the poor for silver, and the needy for a pair of shoes" (8:4–6). And it comes in a book whose prophet has just been ordered out of Bethel and told to "prophesy not again any more" (7:13; cf. 2:12, "ye... commanded the prophets, saying, Prophesy not"). The punishment fits: a people who silence the word will one day "run to and fro to seek the word of the LORD, and shall not find it" (8:12).

The first referent is the northern kingdom's near future; the apostasy reading applies the pattern rather than the original prediction. But the text supports that typology well: in Amos, absence of revelation is the consequence of refusing prophets, which is exactly the structure of the Restoration narrative of apostasy.

Obadiah 21: from deliverers to proxies

Obadiah's twenty-one verses indict Edom for standing by, gloating, looting, and handing over fugitives "in the day that the strangers carried away captive his forces" (v. 11–14). Amos had already condemned Edom because "he did pursue his brother with the sword" (Amos 1:11). The Bible Dictionary lists the possible occasions: a raid under Jehoram (848–844 BC) or, "more probably," the Babylonian conquest of 586. Verses 1–9 share so much with Jeremiah 49:7–22 that one depends on the other or both on a common source. Some historians have noted that hard archaeological evidence for Edom's role in 586 is thin and that the bitter tradition about Edom may have grown in the retelling; the oracle is in any case an expression of grief and grievance against a brother.

The final verse reads, "And saviours shall come up on mount Zion to judge the mount of Esau; and the kingdom shall be the LORD's." The Hebrew moshiʻim is the word used for the judges God "raised up" to deliver Israel (Judges 3:9, 15; Nehemiah 9:27). In context these are deliverers who will govern Edom's territory under God's rule.

Joseph Smith gave the phrase a new referent. In a discourse of January 21, 1844, reported by Wilford Woodruff, he asked how the Saints were "to become Saviors on Mount Zion," and answered, "by building thair temples erecting their Baptismal fonts," and receiving ordinances on behalf of the dead. President Gordon B. Hinckley put it this way in October 2004: "We literally become saviors on Mount Zion." The Church's footnote to Obadiah 21 sends readers to D&C 103:9–10, where the Saints are to be "saviors of men."

A scholar should say plainly that this is a re-signification, not the verse's original sense. Yet a thread connects them: a moshiaʻ acts for people who cannot save themselves, and Obadiah's last word is that the kingdom belongs to the LORD, not to the victors. A book that begins with a brother's betrayal ends, in Restoration usage, with the living acting for those who cannot act.

Jonah: what kind of book, and does it matter?

The Bible Dictionary entry on Jonah is more candid than many readers expect. It says the book "does not claim to be from the hand of the prophet; it describes an episode in his life and is due to some later writer," calls it "a beautiful poem," and finds its key in Jonah 3:10–4:11, where the writer opposes "a narrowmindedness that would confine the love of God to a single nation." That matches mainstream scholarship, which commonly dates the book after the exile on grounds of its Hebrew and treats it as a didactic story; Jack M. Sasson's Anchor Bible commentary and Uriel Simon's JPS commentary are standard guides. The book's Jonah son of Amittai is otherwise known only from 2 Kings 14:25, where he prophesies Jeroboam II's expansion of Israel's borders, in Amos's own generation. The prophet remembered for good news about Israel's territory is sent to the capital of the empire that would end it.

Latter-day Saint scholarship spans the range. LeGrande Davies, in a 1984 Religious Studies Center volume, reported that scholars divide over historicity and chose to read the story typologically as a witness of the Resurrection. David R. Scott, in BYU Studies (2014), read Jonah as a typological foreshadowing of Christ and stated in a note, "Personally, I prefer a symbolic interpretation of the book of Jonah rather than a literal one." Church leaders have usually spoken of Jonah as a historical person, and Jesus's "sign of the prophet Jonas" (Matthew 12:39–41; Luke 11:29–32) is often taken to settle the matter. Others note that an analogy can draw on a story everyone knows regardless of its genre. A Latter-day Saint can hold either view in good faith. What the book itself asks is not whether a fish swallowed a man but whether the reader will accept a merciful God.

Read that way, the theology is sharp. Jonah's oracle is five Hebrew words: "Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown" (3:4). The verb, nehpakhet, comes from the root used for the overthrow of Sodom (Genesis 19:25), but the same stem describes a heart "turned" (Hosea 11:8; Exodus 14:5). Nineveh was overturned, just not as Jonah meant. The king's "Who can tell if God will turn and repent" (3:9) is answered by "God saw their works" (3:10), and then Jonah turns Israel's creed against God, quoting Exodus 34:6 as a complaint: "I knew that thou art a gracious God, and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness, and repentest thee of the evil" (4:2). The book ends with God's unanswered question about "sixscore thousand persons that cannot discern between their right hand and their left hand; and also much cattle" (4:11). Matthew S. Holland's October 2025 address draws on Jonah 2:8, "They that observe lying vanities forsake their own mercy," and calls Jonah's cry "that of a good man in crisis, one largely of his own making."

The lesson's suggested comparison is apt. The sons of Mosiah, sent to the Lamanites, "could not bear that any human soul should perish" (Mosiah 28:3). Jonah could bear it quite well. The Book of Mormon gives Latter-day Saints a missionary narrative that answers the question Jonah leaves open,: not a ruling on the fish, but a canon in which the question of 4:11 has been answered yes.
