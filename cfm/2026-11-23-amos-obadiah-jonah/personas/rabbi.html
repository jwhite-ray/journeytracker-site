The Book We Read With an Empty Stomach: Amos, Obadiah, and Jonah in the Synagogue

Written by an AI persona giving a Jewish reading in the voice of a rabbi — not a real rabbi.

For Jews, Jonah is a late-afternoon text. By the Yom Kippur minchah service the congregation has fasted nearly a full day and has chanted God's thirteen attributes of mercy again and again. Then, as the light fails, a reader chants all four chapters of a story about a prophet who did not want people forgiven. The Talmud already lists it: on Yom Kippur afternoon "they read as the haftara the book of Jonah" (Babylonian Talmud, Megillah 31a). The Shulchan Arukh adds that the reader closes not on Jonah 4:11 but on Micah 7:18–20, "Who is a God like unto thee, that pardoneth iniquity" (Shulchan Arukh, Orach Chayim 622:2). We do not leave the congregation hanging on Jonah's unanswered question. We answer it ourselves.

That habit is the key to all three books: Jewish tradition prayed them, argued with them, and aimed them at itself.

Amos: the herdsman who could not be bought

Amaziah tells Amos, "go... into the land of Judah, and there eat bread, and prophesy there" (Amos 7:12). In other words, go earn your living somewhere else. Amos answers, "I was no prophet, neither was I a prophet's son; but I was an herdman, and a gatherer of sycomore fruit" (7:14). The Talmud reads that answer through the Aramaic Targum: "Because I am the owner of flocks and I have sycamores in the lowland, and I do not come to prophesy for financial gain." From this Rabbi Yochanan concludes that Amos, like Jonah, was wealthy (Babylonian Talmud, Nedarim 38a). The point: a prophet who needs no wages cannot be dismissed from the king's chapel.

On "they sold the righteous for silver, and the poor for a pair of shoes" (2:6), Rashi gives the plain sense. Judges take bribes and squeeze a poor farmer off the plot that lies between their fields so they can join them up (Rashi on Amos 2:6). Radak asks why exile came for this "fourth" sin when Israel had committed graver ones. His answer: as with the generation of the Flood, God drew the line at chamas, violence and robbery, "and even more so because the chamas came at the hands of the judges, who were responsible for upholding justice" (Radak on Amos 2:6). For Radak, Amos's social critique is not ethics set beside the covenant. Corruption in the courts is the sin that fills the measure.

Midrash also hears an older crime in the verse. Joseph's brothers sold him for twenty pieces of silver, and "each one of them took two pieces of silver (apiece) to purchase shoes for their feet, as it is said... Because they have sold the righteous for silver" (Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer 38). On this reading Samaria is not inventing a new decadence. Israel is still selling its brother.

Known, and therefore billed

"You only have I known of all the families of the earth: therefore I will punish you" (3:2). The Talmud records sectarian opponents throwing this verse at Rav Safra: who raises his wrath against his beloved? When he cannot answer, Rabbi Abbahu supplies a parable. A man lends money to his friend and to his enemy. From the friend he collects little by little; from the enemy he collects all at once (Babylonian Talmud, Avodah Zarah 4a). Being known does not exempt Israel from the reckoning. It means Israel pays as it goes.

Amos 9:7 cuts deeper still: "Are ye not as children of the Ethiopians unto me, O children of Israel?... the Philistines from Caphtor, and the Syrians from Kir?" The Talmud softened the verse into a compliment: "just as a Cushite is distinguished by his dark skin, so too, the Jewish people are distinguished by their actions" (Babylonian Talmud, Moed Katan 16b). A fine homily, but it reverses Amos, who says the Exodus is not unique. Read 3:2 and 9:7 together and election means this: Israel is no more God's creature than Cush, but it is bound by covenant, and so more answerable. We have carried both verses ever since without fully reconciling them.

"Seek me" — one commandment, or all of them?

The lesson's title, "Seek ye me, and ye shall live" (5:4), stands at the end of a famous Talmudic chain. Moses gave 613 commandments, David reduced them to eleven (Psalm 15), Isaiah, Micah, and Isaiah again to fewer, and "Amos came and established the 613 mitzvot upon one... Seek Me and live." Then Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak objects that "Seek me" might just mean "Seek Me throughout the entire Torah." So he gives the honor to Habakkuk 2:4, "the righteous shall live by his faith" (Babylonian Talmud, Makkot 24a). Christian readers know that verse from Paul (Romans 1:17); here it summarizes the commandments.

Notice where the Talmud refuses to go. You seek God through Torah, not apart from it. Amos makes the same move: "seek not Beth-el" (5:5). God is found in the gate, where the court sits (5:10, 12, 15). So "I hate, I despise your feast days" (5:21) never read to Jews as a rejection of ritual. A people that kept those feasts for millennia heard a warning against worship cut loose from justice.

The famine of the word, read at Yavneh

The lesson applies Amos 8:11–12 to the Great Apostasy. Jewish tradition aimed the same verses at itself.

A baraita in the Talmud says that when the sages gathered at Yavneh after the Temple fell, they quoted these verses and said, "The Torah is destined to be forgotten from the Jewish people." "The word of the LORD" means halakha, the end of days, and prophecy, and "all these will be lost." Then Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai protests: "Heaven forfend that the Torah should be forgotten from the Jewish people," for "it shall not be forgotten out of the mouths of their seed" (Deuteronomy 31:21). What, then, does "shall not find it" mean? They will not find clear law and clear teaching in one place (Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 138b–139a).

In his reading the famine is not the absence of revelation. It is revelation scattered into dispute, and the remedy is not a new prophet but the study house, the long labor of gathering what was dispersed.

The fallen booth

Amos ends, against all its doom, with "I will raise up the tabernacle of David that is fallen" (9:11). The Hebrew is sukkat David ha-nofelet, David's booth, the flimsy harvest hut. Every Sukkot, sitting in our own booths, we add to the grace after meals: "May the Merciful One raise up for us the fallen booth of David" (Siddur Ashkenaz, Birkat HaMazon). The Talmud even names the Messiah bar nafli, "son of the fallen," from this verse (Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 96b–97a). James quotes the passage at the Jerusalem council (Acts 15:16–17) from a Greek text that reads "the residue of men" where the Hebrew of 9:12 has "the remnant of Edom." That brings us to Edom, a word that carries a weight most Christian readers never feel.

Obadiah: the axe handle from the forest

The rabbis asked why this one prophecy, and no other, came to Obadiah. Rabbi Yitzchak answered that God chose Obadiah, who lived among two wicked people, Ahab and Jezebel, "but did not learn from their actions." He would prophesy against Esau, who lived among two righteous people, Isaac and Rebekah, and also did not learn from them. (This is the Obadiah of 1 Kings 18.) Then, in Rabbi Meir's name: "Obadiah was an Edomite convert," as the proverb says, "From and within the forest comes the ax to it" (Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 39b; Rashi on Obadiah 1:1).

That changes the book. The harshest oracle against Edom comes from a man born Edomite who chose Israel. What condemns Edom is what verses 12–14 list: standing by, gloating, looting, cutting off refugees at the crossroads. "As thou hast done, it shall be done unto thee" (v. 15).

When Edom became Rome, and then the Church

In rabbinic literature "Edom" became the code name for Rome, the empire that destroyed the Second Temple. When Rome became Christian, the name moved with it. The nineteenth-century commentator Malbim states the tradition plainly: the Romans "were called by the name Edom," and as "their well-known religion spread... all who adhered to it came to be called by the name Edom" (Malbim on Obadiah 1:1). Don Isaac Abarbanel, himself driven out of Spain in 1492, opens his commentary by asking whether Obadiah's Edom is the small kingdom south of the Dead Sea "or... the Edom that is Rome and the lands of the Christians" (Abarbanel on Obadiah 1).

The exile is written into the book. Verse 20 mentions "the captivity of Jerusalem, which is in Sepharad." The Targum renders Sepharad as Spain, and Rashi reports that "the exegetes claim that Zarephath is the kingdom called France" (Rashi on Obadiah 1:20). Hence "Sephardim." For centuries Jews read Obadiah as a promise that the exiles in Christian Spain and France would go home. A Christian reading this book should know that for a long time the Jewish reader's Edom was the reader's own church.

The last verse is in our daily prayers. "Saviours shall come up on mount Zion to judge the mount of Esau; and the kingdom shall be the LORD's" (v. 21) is said every morning right after the Song at the Sea, and on Rosh Hashanah it is among the verses of divine kingship in the Malkhuyot (Koren Shalem Siddur, Pesukei DeZimra; Koren Rosh HaShana Mahzor, Musaf). Rashi ties it to Genesis. Jacob told Esau he would come "unto my lord unto Seir" (Genesis 33:14) and never went. "But when will he go? In the days of the Messiah, as it is said (Obadiah 1:21) And saviours shall go up on Mount Zion" (Rashi on Genesis 33:14). Obadiah 21 finishes the brothers' unfinished business.

Latter-day Saints read the "saviours" (moshiʻim, the word used for the judges who delivered Israel) as people performing temple ordinances for the dead. Jewish tradition never made that reading. I would only note what our liturgy keeps at the center: whoever the deliverers are, "the kingdom shall be the LORD's," not theirs.

Jonah: the prophet who stood up for the son

Why did Jonah run? The Mekhilta, the early midrash on Exodus, assumes Jonah knew no one can flee God. Doesn't Amos say, "Though they dig into hell, thence shall mine hand take them" (Amos 9:2)? Instead, Jonah meant to leave the land where prophecy comes, "for the gentiles are close to repentance," and he would not let Nineveh's quick repentance indict Israel, which had not repented. Then the key distinction. Jeremiah upheld the honor of the Father and of the son. Elijah upheld the Father's honor but not the son's. "Jonah claimed the honor of the son, but not the honor of the Father." Rabbi Yochanan adds that Jonah boarded the ship "only to cast himself into the sea," like Moses, who said "blot me... out of thy book" (Mekhilta de-Rabbi Yishmael, Pischa 1). This Jonah is no bigot. He is a patriot ready to die rather than become the instrument of his people's condemnation.

Radak agrees and says the book was written "as an object lesson for Israel: Here is a gentile people... who readily repent" (Radak on Jonah 1:1). What of the theory that Jonah feared being called a false prophet? Maimonides settles it. A prophecy of disaster that does not happen does not discredit the prophet, "because the Holy One, Blessed be He, is slow to anger... it is possible that they will repent... as in the case of the people of Nineveh." Only a promise of good that fails proves a prophet false (Mishneh Torah, Foundations of the Torah 10:4). Deuteronomy 18:22 does not convict Jonah.

Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer 10 adds the fun: the fish was appointed "from the six days of Creation," Jonah walked into it "just as a man enters the great synagogue," and it carried him to the foundation stone beneath the Temple, where he prayed. (A reader's note: KJV Jonah 1:17 is Jonah 2:1 in the Hebrew Bible, so in a Jewish Bible the fish opens chapter 2 and the prayer runs through 2:11.)

Repentance with the hands

The Mishnah turned Jonah 3 into public ritual. On a communal fast the ark was carried into the town square and covered with ashes, and an elder said: "Our brothers, it is not stated with regard to the people of Nineveh: And God saw their sackcloth and their fasting. Rather... And God saw their deeds" (Mishnah Taʻanit 2:1; Jonah 3:10). On "the violence that is in their hands" (3:8), Shmuel says the king decreed that a man who had built a stolen beam into his palace must tear the palace down and return the beam (Babylonian Talmud, Taʻanit 16a).

The rabbis did not romanticize Nineveh, though. In the Jerusalem Talmud, Reish Lakish says flatly that "the people of Nineveh repented in trickery," separating calves from their mothers so the bawling would force heaven's hand (Jerusalem Talmud, Taʻanit 2:1). Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer 43 gives Nineveh forty years of reprieve, one for each day of Jonah's warning, before it relapsed. The tradition knows that public repentance can be real and can be theater, and it reads Jonah 3 on the holiest day of the year anyway.

What Jonah left out

Now hear Jonah 4:2 as a Yom Kippur congregation hears it. All day we have chanted Exodus 34:6, "merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abundant in goodness and truth." Jonah throws the same formula at God, "a gracious God, and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness, and repentest thee of the evil," and a listener notices the swap. "And truth" (ve-ʼemet) is gone, and "repentest thee of the evil" has taken its place. In Jonah's version, mercy has swallowed truth.

Uriel Simon, author of the JPS commentary on the book, reads all of Jonah as a struggle between justice and mercy, in which the prophet must learn that "the world can exist only through the unfathomable amalgam of justice and mercy" (Simon, "Jonah's Lesson in Divine Mercy"). The book stops on God's question about 120,000 people "that cannot discern between their right hand and their left hand; and also much cattle" (4:11), and Jonah never answers. The late Midrash Jonah, which Simon cites, supplies the answer: Jonah falls on his face and says, "Conduct Your world according to the attribute of mercy." The synagogue supplies its own by adding Micah 7:18. At that hour we are Nineveh, fasting and hoping God will see our deeds and not our sackcloth. We are also Jonah, who would rather the Ninevehs of the world got what they deserve. The book will not let us be only one of them.

What Christian readers tend to miss

First, for Jews these are not texts about other people; we aimed them at ourselves. Second, "Edom" is not a neutral ancient name. Third, the sign of Jonah that matters in the synagogue is not the three days in the fish (Matthew 12:40). It is the forty days, the open question, and a God who "repented of the evil" (3:10). As Abraham Joshua Heschel argued in The Prophets (1962), the prophets' God is a God of pathos, moved by what human beings do. That is why repentance is not a transaction but a relationship.
