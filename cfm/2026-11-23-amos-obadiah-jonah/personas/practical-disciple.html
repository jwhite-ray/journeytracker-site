Short Measures and Long Grudges: What Amos, Obadiah, and Jonah Ask of an Ordinary Week

Written by an AI persona on living the week's text — not a real person.

Most of us will never stand at Bethel and denounce a king, never sail for Tarshish, never see a city of a hundred and twenty thousand put on sackcloth. But almost all of us will, this week, sell something, buy something, sit through a meeting, hear bad news about someone we don't like, and get asked to do something we would rather not do. Read with those ordinary days in mind, these three short books turn out to be uncomfortably specific. Amos is about what happens at the market and the city gate. Obadiah is about what happens in your heart when a rival falls. Jonah is about what happens when God is kinder than you wanted Him to be.

The ephah and the shekel: honesty where nobody is checking

Amos's most quotable verse is 5:24, "let judgment run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream." Its most practical passage is 8:4–6, and it is worth reading slowly:

Saying, When will the new moon be gone, that we may sell corn? and the sabbath, that we may set forth wheat, making the ephah small, and the shekel great, and falsifying the balances by deceit? That we may buy the poor for silver, and the needy for a pair of shoes; yea, and sell the refuse of the wheat? (Amos 8:5–6)

Notice the order. These merchants are keeping the sabbath. They are not skipping the holy day; they are simply waiting it out, impatient for it to end so they can get back to business. Their worship is intact on the outside. And the business they return to is rigged three ways: the ephah (the measure of grain the customer receives) is made small, the shekel (the weight of silver the customer pays) is made great, and the scale itself is crooked. On top of that, they "sell the refuse of the wheat" — the sweepings off the threshing floor passed off as grain. The Law of Moses had already forbidden exactly this ("Ye shall do no unrighteousness in judgment, in meteyard, in weight, or in measure. Just balances, just weights... shall ye have," Leviticus 19:35–36; compare Deuteronomy 25:13–15). Amos is not inventing a new standard. He is saying that keeping the sabbath while cheating on Monday is not keeping the covenant at all.

The modern equivalents are quieter than a weighted scale: billing an hour that was really forty minutes; describing a used car or rental unit in language technically true and designed to mislead; padding an expense report; judging a subordinate's work harshly and our own generously — a small ephah for them, a great shekel for us. Selling "the refuse of the wheat" might be delivering the bare minimum on a contract while implying it is the full product.

Amos 2:8 adds a detail that brings it closer to home: "they lay themselves down upon clothes laid to pledge by every altar." The Law said a poor man's cloak taken as collateral had to be returned by sundown, because "it is his raiment for his skin: wherein shall he sleep?" (Exodus 22:26–27). The wealthy in Amos's Israel kept the cloaks and stretched out on them at worship. The question for us is not whether we hold anyone's cloak, but whether we press every advantage the rules allow against people who have little leverage: the late fee charged to someone we know is struggling, the deposit withheld on a technicality, the contractor paid late because we can.

A self-examination this week: pick one transaction you will make in your job or household and ask whether you would be comfortable if the other party could see everything you know about it.

At ease in Zion

"Woe to them that are at ease in Zion" (Amos 6:1) is often quoted as a warning against spiritual laziness, and it is that. But the passage is more pointed than that. Read verses 4–6 as a single picture: ivory beds, lamb and veal from the best of the flock, music, wine "in bowls," fine ointments, "but they are not grieved for the affliction of Joseph."

None of the listed items is a sin in itself. Lying on a comfortable bed is not condemned anywhere in the Law. The charge is the last clause. These people are comfortable and not grieved — the suffering of their own people simply does not register. Earlier the Lord speaks to the "kine of Bashan," the well-fed women of Samaria, "which oppress the poor, which crush the needy, which say to their masters, Bring, and let us drink" (4:1). Their oppression came through a demand for more, supplied by a system they never examined.

Being "at ease in Zion" today might mean our ward, family, and calling are all running smoothly and we have stopped noticing who is not fine: the name on the ministering list we have avoided for months because it is awkward, the neighbor whose marriage is visibly failing, the coworker whose hours were cut and to whom we said "that's tough" and moved on. Amos's test is not whether we are comfortable, but whether our comfort has made us unable to grieve.

A practical check: when did something happening to someone outside your household last actually cost you sleep, money, or time? If you cannot remember, Amos 6:6 is addressed to you.

"They hate him that rebuketh in the gate"

Amos 5:10 diagnoses a second problem: "They hate him that rebuketh in the gate, and they abhor him that speaketh uprightly." The gate was where disputes were judged. Verse 12 explains why the honest voice there was hated: the powerful "take a bribe, and they turn aside the poor in the gate from their right." Amaziah's response to Amos in 7:10–13 is the same instinct in religious dress: "the land is not able to bear all his words... prophesy not again any more at Beth-el: for it is the king's chapel." Even earlier, the Lord lists among Israel's sins that they "commanded the prophets, saying, Prophesy not" (2:12).

Most of us do not banish prophets, but we know how to make honest people stop talking. We get defensive when a spouse raises the same concern a third time; we stop asking the friend who tells us the truth; at work we reward good news and sideline whoever flags problems. A family can become "the king's chapel," where certain subjects are never raised. Amos's alternative is to "establish judgment in the gate" (5:15) — build places where truth can be said and heard. Name one person whose criticism you have learned to avoid, ask them this week what you are not seeing, and don't argue.

Obadiah: the sin of standing on the other side

Obadiah is a single chapter, and most of it is judgment on Edom. But verses 11–14 contain one of the most precise descriptions of a common sin anywhere in scripture. Edom's crime was not that it attacked Jerusalem. It was that it watched, enjoyed, and then helped itself:

In the day that thou stoodest on the other side... even thou wast as one of them. But thou shouldest not have looked on the day of thy brother in the day that he became a stranger; neither shouldest thou have rejoiced over the children of Judah in the day of their destruction; neither shouldest thou have spoken proudly in the day of distress. (Obadiah 1:11–12)

The list moves in stages. First, standing aloof ("on the other side"). Then looking on. Then rejoicing. Then speaking proudly. Then entering the gate and taking goods (v. 13). Finally standing at the crossroads to cut off the escapees and hand them over (v. 14). Gloating is not presented as a private feeling that harms no one; it is the first step on a slope that ends in looting. And the target is a brother — Edom is Esau, Jacob's twin (v. 10; compare Amos 1:11, where Edom "did pursue his brother with the sword, and did cast off all pity").

It shows up in the small satisfaction when the coworker who got our promotion stumbles publicly, when a relative who criticized our parenting has a child go off the rails, or when a political opponent is caught in scandal. The Proverbs already warned against it: "Rejoice not when thine enemy falleth, and let not thine heart be glad when he stumbleth" (Proverbs 24:17). Obadiah traces what happens when we ignore that warning. The "I told you so," the forwarded screenshot, the conversation that begins "Did you hear about...?" — these are verse 12 behavior. Taking advantage of someone's crisis to settle an old score or improve our own position is verse 13.

Obadiah 1:15 states the principle bluntly: "as thou hast done, it shall be done unto thee: thy reward shall return upon thine own head." And the book ends with a different picture of what to do with a brother's disaster: "saviours shall come up on mount Zion" (1:21). President Gordon B. Hinckley applied that verse to proxy temple ordinances, teaching that "we literally become saviors on Mount Zion," and that "in a small measure, when we engage in proxy work in the temple," we "become as saviors to those on the other side" ("Closing Remarks," October 2004). The contrast with verses 11–14 is worth sitting with. Edom stood "on the other side" of a brother's catastrophe and took advantage. The disciple is called to stand for those "on the other side" and do for them what they cannot do for themselves.

A self-examination: who is the person whose failure, if you heard about it tomorrow, would give you a flicker of satisfaction? That is the person to pray for by name this week — and, if there is any way to do it, to help.

Jonah: fleeing, sleeping, sulking

The lesson observes that Nineveh was "an enemy of Israel known for its violence and cruelty," and invites us to compare Jonah with the sons of Mosiah, who "could not bear that any human soul should perish" (Mosiah 28:3) and went willingly to the Lamanites. The comparison is a good one because Jonah's problem is not that he doubts God. He knows exactly who God is. That is the problem.

He tells us so himself, after Nineveh repents and is spared: "I knew that thou art a gracious God, and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness, and repentest thee of the evil" (Jonah 4:2). That is a near-quotation of the Lord's own self-description to Moses (Exodus 34:6). Jonah quotes it as a complaint. He fled to Tarshish not because he feared Nineveh would reject him, but because he feared it would accept the message, and God would forgive people Jonah did not want forgiven.

There are three distinct movements here, and each has a modern form.

Fleeing. Jonah does not argue with the assignment. He just goes the other direction — "down to Joppa," "down into" the ship, "down into the sides of the ship" (1:3, 5). Most avoidance looks like this. We don't refuse the calling; we just never quite get to it. We don't say no to the hard conversation with a teenager or an aging parent; we keep finding reasons it isn't the right time.

Sleeping. While the pagan sailors pray, throw cargo overboard, and row hard to save his life (1:5, 13), Jonah is "fast asleep." The shipmaster has to wake him: "arise, call upon thy God" (1:6). The people outside the covenant behave better in chapter 1 than the prophet does — a caution for any of us who assume the neighbor or colleague of another faith, or none, is less attuned to God than we are.

Sulking. Chapter 4 is the hardest. Jonah succeeds — a whole city turns "from their evil way, and from the violence that is in their hands" (3:8) — and he is "very angry" (4:1). He sits east of the city "till he might see what would become of the city" (4:5), still hoping, it seems, for fire. The Lord asks twice, "Doest thou well to be angry?" (4:4, 9).

This is the elder brother who "was angry, and would not go in" (Luke 15:28), and the laborers told, "Is thine eye evil, because I am good?" (Matthew 20:15). It is us when a family member who caused years of pain is now well-liked at church, or a ward member whose past we know is called to a position of trust. The book ends without Jonah's answer to God's last question (4:11), and that silence leaves the question to the reader.

Jonah's own psalm from the belly of the fish contains the line that Elder Matthew S. Holland built a recent conference talk around: "They that observe lying vanities forsake their own mercy" (Jonah 2:8). Elder Holland called Jonah's cry "that of a good man in crisis, one largely of his own making," and urged listeners to remember that "you have immediate access to divine help and healing despite your human flaws" ("Forsake Not Your Own Mercy," October 2025). The irony is that Jonah gladly receives mercy in chapter 2 and resents it for others in chapter 4. Most of us know the feeling. It is easy to believe in grace for ourselves and in justice for everyone else.

Finally, a reminder from Jewish tradition about what Nineveh's repentance actually consisted of. The Mishnah, describing public fasts in times of drought, has an elder remind the people that the text does not say God saw Nineveh's sackcloth and fasting, but that "God saw their deeds, for they had repented from their evil ways" (Mishnah Taʻanit 2:1, translated by Daniel Nevins; Jonah 3:10). Sackcloth was the outward sign. What God responded to was that they let go of "the violence that is in their hands." That is the link back to Amos: fasts, feasts, sabbaths, and sacrifices (Amos 5:21–23) are not condemned for being religious, but for being a substitute for changed behavior.

Questions to carry into the week

These are meant to be answered specifically, with names and dates, not in general:

  • Where in my work or finances am I making "the ephah small, and the shekel great" (Amos 8:5)? What would it cost to fix it this week?
  • Whose affliction am I comfortable not grieving over (Amos 6:6)? What is one thing I could do about it before Sunday?
  • Who in my life "rebuketh in the gate" (Amos 5:10), and how have I made it harder for them to speak?
  • Whose failure would I secretly enjoy (Obadiah 1:12)? Have I prayed for that person by name?
  • What assignment am I quietly sailing away from (Jonah 1:3)?
  • Whom do I not want God to forgive (Jonah 4:1–2)? What would it mean to answer God's final question in Jonah 4:11 honestly?
