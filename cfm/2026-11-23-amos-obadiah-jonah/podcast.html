Context

NATHAN: These three sit together among the shorter prophets, the collection often called the Book of the Twelve. And the canon gives them a shared setting. Amos 1:1 dates him "in the days of Uzziah king of Judah, and in the days of Jeroboam the son of Joash king of Israel, two years before the earthquake." And Jonah son of Amittai appears in 2 Kings 14:25, prophesying that Jeroboam the Second would restore Israel's borders "from the entering of Hamath unto the sea of the plain."

ELI: So Amos and Jonah share a king.

NATHAN: Same reign, at least on the surface. The panel's LDS scholar has a sharp line on it: the prophet remembered for good news about Israel's territory is sent to the capital of the empire that would end it.

ELI: Oof. What was the north like under Jeroboam?

NATHAN: Rich, if you were at the top. The academic scholar walks through it. Assyria had weakened Damascus; the Tell al-Rimah stela records tribute from "Joash the Samarian," almost certainly Jeroboam's father. With Aram reduced, Israel pushed outward. The Samaria ostraca record deliveries of "old wine" and "washed oil" to the capital, likely written by just two scribes. And Samaria turned up hundreds of fragments of carved ivory, the kind of luxury Amos means by "beds of ivory."

THE DISCIPLE: Which matters for chapter 6. None of what's listed there is a sin in itself. Ivory beds, lamb and veal, wine "in bowls." The charge is the last clause: "but they are not grieved for the affliction of Joseph."

ELI: Who was Amos himself?

NATHAN: He's from among "the herdmen of Tekoa," in Judah. A southerner preaching in the north. The word in 1:1, noqed, is also used for Mesha king of Moab, a sheep-breeder on a royal scale. So Amos may have been a man of some standing, not a poor shepherd.

ELI: And the earthquake?

NATHAN: Zechariah 14:5 still remembers "the earthquake in the days of Uzziah" centuries later. A 2000 study argued for one great quake around 750 BCE, and the academic says a major mid-century quake is reasonably well supported, though others are cautious on the details. It may even explain why Amos's words were kept. A prophet who said the land would "tremble" looked vindicated.

ELI: Then a generation later, Assyria takes Samaria.

NATHAN: Second Kings 17. Now jump forward. Obadiah, the shortest book in the Hebrew Bible, has no date, but it charges Edom with standing aloof when "foreigners entered into his gates, and cast lots upon Jerusalem." Most scholars connect that to the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem in 586. And Edom is Esau, Jacob's twin. That's why Obadiah keeps saying "thy brother."

ELI: And Jonah?

NATHAN: Here's the twist. The academic's point is that, read critically, these books span roughly three centuries. Amos holds some of the oldest prophetic material in the Bible. Obadiah answers 586. And Jonah, by most current estimates, is a Persian-period story using an eighth-century prophet as its main character. We'll come back to that.

Content

ELI: Walk us through Amos.

NATHAN: It opens with a trap. "For three transgressions of Damascus, and for four, I will not turn away the punishment thereof." Then Gaza, Tyre, Edom, Ammon, Moab. Each an enemy, each a real war crime.

ELI: And the audience in Bethel is cheering.

NATHAN: Then Judah, the rival kingdom. More cheering. Then 2:6: "For three transgressions of Israel, and for four." The circle closes on the listeners. And Israel's crimes aren't battlefield atrocities. They "sold the righteous for silver, and the poor for a pair of shoes." They lay down "upon clothes laid to pledge by every altar."

THE DISCIPLE: That cloak detail is the one I'd sit with. The Law said a poor man's pledged cloak had to be returned by sundown, because "wherein shall he sleep?" The wealthy kept the cloaks and stretched out on them at worship. Most of us don't hold anybody's cloak. But we can press every advantage the rules allow against people with little leverage. The late fee on someone we know is struggling. The deposit withheld on a technicality.

NATHAN: Then chapter 3. "You only have I known of all the families of the earth: therefore I will punish you for all your iniquities." The rabbi cites a Talmudic parable. A man lends to his friend and to his enemy. From the friend he collects little by little; from the enemy, all at once. Being known means you pay as you go.

ELI: And then the verse every Latter-day Saint knows.

NATHAN: "Surely the Lord GOD will do nothing, but he revealeth his secret unto his servants the prophets." In context, it sits in a chain of questions: "Will a lion roar in the forest, when he hath no prey?" And it lands on verse 8: "The lion hath roared, who will not fear? the Lord GOD hath spoken, who can but prophesy?"

ELI: So Amos is defending why he's saying such unwelcome things.

NATHAN: Exactly. And "secret" is sod: a council, the circle of confidants, and what's decided there. Jeremiah 23 uses it for the test of a true prophet, who "hath stood in the counsel of the LORD." And the Joseph Smith Translation changes one word. "But" becomes "until." The LDS scholar puts it neatly: the KJV reads as an absolute, the JST as a sequence. Revelation comes before the act.

ELI: Chapter 4 has the refrain.

NATHAN: Five times, after disaster upon disaster: "yet have ye not returned unto me, saith the LORD." Then, "prepare to meet thy God, O Israel." And 5:4, the lesson's title: "Seek ye me, and ye shall live." With directions on where not to look: "seek not Beth-el, nor enter into Gilgal." The rabbi gives us a Talmud passage. Moses gave 613 commandments, David reduced them to eleven, and "Amos came and established the 613 mitzvot upon one... Seek Me and live."

ELI: (warmly) That's beautiful.

NATHAN: (chuckling) And then the Talmud hands the honor to Habakkuk instead, because "Seek me" might just mean seek Me through the whole Torah. And in Amos, God is found "in the gate," where the court sits.

ELI: And the day of the LORD.

NATHAN: People in Bethel wanted it. Amos says, "the day of the LORD is darkness, and not light." And the climax: "But let judgment run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream."

THE DISCIPLE: That's the most quotable verse. But the most practical passage is the one Eli opened with. The business is rigged three ways. The ephah, the grain the customer receives, is made small. The shekel, the silver the customer pays, is made great. And the scale itself is crooked. Then they "sell the refuse of the wheat," the sweepings off the floor.

ELI: What does that look like now?

THE DISCIPLE: Quieter than a weighted scale. Billing an hour that was really forty minutes. Describing a used car in language that's technically true and built to mislead. Padding an expense report. Or judging a subordinate's work harshly and our own generously. A small ephah for them, a great shekel for us.

ELI: (wincing) That last one stings.

THE DISCIPLE: My check for the week: pick one transaction and ask whether you'd be comfortable if the other party could see everything you know about it.

NATHAN: Chapters 7 through 9 hold five visions. Locusts, and Amos prays, "O Lord GOD, forgive, I beseech thee: by whom shall Jacob arise? for he is small." And "The LORD repented for this." Fire, and the same again. Then a plumbline, and no reprieve: "I will not again pass by them any more." Then a basket of summer fruit, qayits, which yields the word "end," qets. And finally the Lord "standing upon the altar."

ELI: And in the middle, the one story in the book.

NATHAN: Then the famine: "not a famine of bread, nor a thirst for water, but of hearing the words of the LORD." They "run to and fro to seek the word of the LORD, and shall not find it." NATHAN: Twenty-one verses. It opens with pride: "Though thou exalt thyself as the eagle, and though thou set thy nest among the stars, thence will I bring thee down, saith the LORD."

Then entering the gate and taking goods. Finally standing at the crossroads to cut off the people trying to escape. Gloating isn't a private feeling that harms no one. It's the first step on a slope that ends in looting.

ELI: And it's a brother.

THE DISCIPLE: It's a brother. It's the small satisfaction when the coworker who got our promotion stumbles publicly. The "I told you so," the forwarded screenshot, the conversation that starts "Did you hear about...?" That's verse 12 behavior.

NATHAN: The rabbi adds a startling tradition. The Talmud says, in Rabbi Meir's name, that Obadiah was himself an Edomite convert. The harshest oracle against Edom comes from a man born Edomite. Jews say that verse every morning. Latter-day Saints know it from temple work. President Hinckley said in October 2004, "We literally become saviors on Mount Zion."

THE DISCIPLE: And he says that in proxy work we "become as saviors to those on the other side." Edom stood "on the other side" of a brother's catastrophe and took advantage. Same phrase, opposite posture.

ELI: Now Jonah.

NATHAN: "Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city." And Jonah "rose up to flee unto Tarshish." Then the book keeps sending him down. "Went down to Joppa." "Went down into" the ship. "Gone down into the sides of the ship." In the psalm, "I went down to the bottoms of the mountains."

THE DISCIPLE: That's what most avoidance looks like. Jonah doesn't argue with the assignment. He just goes the other direction. We don't refuse the calling; we just never quite get to it. We don't say no to the hard conversation with a teenager or an aging parent. We just keep finding reasons it isn't the right time.

ELI: And he sleeps through the storm.

THE DISCIPLE: While the pagan sailors pray, throw cargo overboard, and row hard to save his life. The shipmaster has to wake him: "What meanest thou, O sleeper? arise, call upon thy God." The people outside the covenant behave better in chapter 1 than the prophet does.

NATHAN: Then the fish. "The LORD had prepared a great fish." The Hebrew is dag gadol, great fish. It became a whale only through the Greek of Matthew 12:40. Then the psalm: "Out of the belly of hell cried I, and thou heardest my voice." And the line Elder Matthew S. THE DISCIPLE: He called Jonah's cry "that of a good man in crisis, one largely of his own making." And the irony is that Jonah gladly receives mercy in chapter 2 and resents it for others in chapter 4. It's easy to believe in grace for ourselves and justice for everyone else.

NATHAN: Chapter 3. Five Hebrew words: "Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown." And the city repents, the king in sackcloth, even the animals. "Let them turn every one from his evil way, and from the violence that is in their hands."

THE DISCIPLE: The rabbi and I both reach for the Mishnah here. On a public fast an elder would remind people the text doesn't say God saw Nineveh's sackcloth. It says "God saw their deeds." What God answered was that they let go of the violence in their hands.

NATHAN: Then chapter 4. "It displeased Jonah exceedingly, and he was very angry." NATHAN: The rabbi tells us Jews read the whole book on Yom Kippur afternoon, after fasting nearly all day. And they don't end on 4:11. They add Micah 7:18: "Who is a God like unto thee, that pardoneth iniquity." They don't leave the congregation hanging on Jonah's question. They answer it themselves.

Controversies

ELI: Okay. The hard questions. Start with the fish.

NATHAN: The plain objection: a man can't survive three days in a fish. And the honest questioner points out some defenses made it worse. For about a century, sermons told of James Bartley, a whaler supposedly swallowed and cut out alive. The historian Edward B. Davis traced it, and the records don't support it. Defending the fish with a false story is weaker than just saying, "the LORD had prepared a great fish."

ELI: So it's a miracle claim, not marine biology.

NATHAN: And for scholars the fish isn't the main issue. The book says "the king of Nineveh," a title Assyrian inscriptions don't use. It calls Nineveh "an exceeding great city of three days' journey," but its walls ran about twelve kilometers around, a walk of a few hours. Add late features of the Hebrew, and most critical scholars read it as a didactic story. Others defend an early setting. The honest questioner's summary: the evidence leans toward a late, literary composition, but not so far that a historical reading is foolish.

ELI: And the Church's own materials?

NATHAN: They differ. The Bible Dictionary says the book is "due to some later writer" and finds its point in opposing "a narrowmindedness that would confine the love of God to a single nation." The Old Testament Student Manual calls the story "a true one, and not an allegory," quoting Joseph Fielding Smith. And LeGrande Davies, in a 1984 Religious Studies Center volume, called the question "strictly a matter of personal preference."

ELI: Disciple, what does a regular member do with that range?

THE DISCIPLE: I'd notice that every one of those readers still has to face chapter 4. Whichever way you land on the fish, the gourd is still there. The LDS scholar says it well: what the book asks isn't whether a fish swallowed a man but whether the reader will accept a merciful God. I can lose a week arguing about the fish and never ask myself whom I don't want forgiven.

NATHAN: But the strongest argument for historicity comes from Jesus. "For as Jonas was three days and three nights in the whale's belly; so shall the Son of man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth." And, "The men of Nineveh shall rise in judgment with this generation." Joseph Fielding Smith's reasoning was straightforward: Jesus believed it.

ELI: That's a serious argument.

NATHAN: It is. The counterargument is that teachers cite stories everyone knows, and "even Nineveh repented" works as a rebuke either way. The honest questioner concludes the sign works as a type of the Resurrection under both readings.

THE DISCIPLE: And the doctrinal connector draws the line from Jonah's descent to Doctrine and Covenants 122: "The Son of Man hath descended below them all." That's the sign I want to carry. A Savior who went lower than Jonah did.

ELI: Next. God repents.

NATHAN: Three times in our block. Twice in Amos 7, "The LORD repented for this." And Jonah 3:10: "God repented of the evil, that he had said that he would do unto them; and he did it not." But Numbers 23:19 says God is not "the son of man, that he should repent." Joseph Smith noticed. The JST moves the repenting every time. Amos 7:3 becomes "Jacob shall repent for this, therefore I will not utterly destroy him." In Jonah 3:10 the Ninevites repent and "God turned away the evil."

THE DISCIPLE: But the Hebrew?

NATHAN: The verb is nicham. To relent, to be moved to pity, to change course. Not to confess a sin. The doctrinal connector says the JST heads off a misreading of the English more than it corrects the Hebrew, and it isn't a global edit; there's no JST note at Jonah 4:2. The honest questioner says some find a God who relents more beautiful than an unmoving one, others find it unsettling, and a careful reader can hold the JST and the Hebrew without pretending they say the same thing.

THE DISCIPLE: What moves me is Amos's prayer itself. "By whom shall Jacob arise? for he is small." He's interceding for the people who will throw him out of Bethel. If I'm honest, my prayers for people who annoy me don't sound like that.

NATHAN: And that ties to whether Jonah was a false prophet. Nineveh wasn't overthrown, and Deuteronomy 18:22 says a word that doesn't come to pass isn't from the LORD. But the rabbi cites Maimonides: a prophecy of disaster that doesn't happen doesn't discredit the prophet, because people may repent, "as in the case of the people of Nineveh." And the verb for "overthrown," nehpakhet, can also mean "turned." Nineveh was overturned. Just not the way Jonah meant.

ELI: (laughing) He got his prophecy fulfilled and hated it.

NATHAN: The rabbi also catches something in 4:2. Jonah quotes Exodus 34:6 but drops "and truth," putting "repentest thee of the evil" in its place. In Jonah's version, mercy has swallowed truth. And why did he run? The Mekhilta says because the Gentiles were "close to repentance," and their quick repentance would condemn Israel. On that reading he's a patriot, not a bigot.

THE DISCIPLE: (slowly) I find that more convicting, not less. My resentments almost never feel like bigotry from the inside. They feel like loyalty. To my family, my side, my version of what happened.

ELI: Obadiah. Isn't the whole book just revenge?

NATHAN: The honest questioner presses that. It ends with "there shall not be any remaining of the house of Esau." Yet Deuteronomy 23:7 says, "Thou shalt not abhor an Edomite; for he is thy brother." Obadiah condemns Edom for gloating, and much of the book reads like gloating back. And historically, J. R. Bartlett argued that the picture of Edom as Babylon's eager partner overstates the case.

ELI: And the rabbi?

NATHAN: He adds something most Christian readers never feel. In rabbinic literature, Edom became the code name for Rome, and when Rome became Christian, the name moved with it. Abarbanel, driven out of Spain in 1492, asks whether Obadiah means the little kingdom or "the Edom that is Rome and the lands of the Christians."

ELI: That reframes it.

THE DISCIPLE: It does. And it's why I don't read Obadiah to find my enemy. Some take the book as the honest cry of victims, like Psalm 137, and that's fair. But the question it puts to my week is whose failure, if I heard about it tomorrow, would give me a flicker of satisfaction. That's the person to pray for by name. And if there's any way, to help.

NATHAN: The honest questioner calls the Latter-day Saint reading of verse 21 the most generous possible thing to do with Obadiah: a verse about judging Esau becomes a verse about redeeming the dead. The LDS scholar and the doctrinal connector both say plainly that it's a re-signification, not the verse's original sense.

ELI: Now the famine. Amos 8 and the Apostasy.

NATHAN: The lesson says the verses "could also apply" to the Great Apostasy, and the six-outlooks essay stresses that "also." Elder Jeffrey R. Holland said in 1997 that God "has through His Only Begotten Son lifted the famine of which Amos spoke." The critique is that in Amos the famine falls on the eighth-century north, a people who told the prophets "Prophesy not." It's the consequence of rejecting a voice that was speaking. The six-outlooks essay calls it a layer cake. Readers in different eras kept finding the famine in their own time: Lamentations, Psalm 74, First Maccabees. The rabbi shows the sages at Yavneh, after the Temple fell, citing these verses to say the Torah would be forgotten. The Restoration reading is one more layer, not a replacement.

THE DISCIPLE: And the six-outlooks essay asks something I can't shake. Amos's famine wasn't a shortage of shrines. Bethel was busy. It was a shortage of hearing. A household can have scripture in every room and on every phone and still be in famine by that definition.

ELI: (ruefully) That's me with my phone.

THE DISCIPLE: The lesson asks how you know when you're spiritually hungry. The harder question is how you'd know if you'd stopped being hungry.

NATHAN: "Are ye not as children of the Ethiopians unto me, O children of Israel?... Have not I brought up Israel out of the land of Egypt? and the Philistines from Caphtor, and the Syrians from Kir?"
