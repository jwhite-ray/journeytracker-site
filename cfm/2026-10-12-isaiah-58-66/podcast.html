Two Maps of the Same Nine Chapters

ELI: Nathan, put us on the map.

NATHAN: There are two maps, and they matter more this week than any week of Isaiah. The critical map goes back to Bernhard Duhm's commentary in 1892. Chapters 1 through 39 are tied mostly to Isaiah of Jerusalem in the eighth century BC. Chapters 40 through 55, "Second Isaiah," speak to exiles in Babylon around the time of Cyrus. And chapters 56 through 66, "Third Isaiah," are set back in Judah after the return, in the Persian period.

ELI: And why do scholars put these chapters there?

THE QUESTIONER: This is the part people get wrong. The case isn't just "scholars don't believe in prophecy." It rests on what the chapters assume. Listen to 64, verses 10 and 11: "Zion is a wilderness, Jerusalem a desolation. Our holy and our beautiful house, where our fathers praised thee, is burned up with fire." That's not dread of a future disaster. It's a prayer said standing in the rubble.

NATHAN: And the community is fighting with itself. 66, verse 5: "Your brethren that hated you, that cast you out for my name's sake, said, Let the LORD be glorified." Same God, two sides. The panel's academic scholar walks through the theories. Paul Hanson read these chapters as protest literature from a visionary group shut out by the priests running the rebuilt temple. Brooks Schramm pushed back and argued the targets were syncretistic cults. The conflict was real; the neat sociology probably outruns the evidence.

ELI: Fun detail I didn't know: "holier than thou" comes from this argument. 65, verse 5.

Nazareth

Death at a Hundred

NATHAN: Chapter 65. "For, behold, I create new heavens and a new earth." The verb is baraʼ, the creation verb of Genesis 1. And then the details are startlingly ordinary. NATHAN: The rabbi says Maimonides reads it exactly that way. The "new heavens" are perpetual gladness in place of grief, and the world continues according to its pattern. For a Jew, the rabbi writes, "new heavens" means this earth repaired, not this earth replaced.
