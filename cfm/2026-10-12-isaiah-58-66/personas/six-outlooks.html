Six Ways Into Beauty for Ashes: Isaiah 61:1–3

Written by an AI persona applying Donald W. Parry's six-outlooks framework. Professor Parry did not write, review, or endorse this essay, and no words are attributed to him that are not cited to a published source.

Donald W. Parry, a Hebrew Bible professor at BYU and a member of the international team that edited the Dead Sea Scrolls (BYU Speeches speaker page), opens a chapter of his book Search Diligently the Words of Isaiah (BYU Religious Studies Center, 2023) by laying out "six outlooks, or six ways to read Isaiah." They are: reading Isaiah in his historical context (a "near view"); reading prophecy (a "distant view"); dual- or multiple-fulfillment prophecy; likening Isaiah's words to ourselves and others; reading a passage for personal reasons; and reading Isaiah to understand doctrine. His claim is that a passage read all six ways yields what he calls "layer upon layer of truth and knowledge."

What follows applies that framework to one passage. Where it reports Parry, it reports his chapter; where it goes further, the reading is this essay's own.

Why Isaiah 61:1–3

Isaiah 61:1–3 gives every one of the six outlooks a real object to work on. It has a recoverable ancient audience ("them that mourn in Zion," among "the old wastes," 61:3–4). It has a named later fulfillment: Jesus reads it aloud in Nazareth and says "This day is this scripture fulfilled in your ears" (Luke 4:21). It has at least two more fulfillments claimed in scripture, one of them in the spirit world (D&C 138). It was already being likened in antiquity, a century before Jesus, by the Qumran community. And its central image, the exchange of one thing "for" another, is a compact statement of the doctrine of atonement. Here is the text as the KJV prints it:

The Spirit of the Lord GOD is upon me; because the LORD hath anointed me to preach good tidings unto the meek; he hath sent me to bind up the brokenhearted, to proclaim liberty to the captives, and the opening of the prison to them that are bound; To proclaim the acceptable year of the LORD, and the day of vengeance of our God; to comfort all that mourn; To appoint unto them that mourn in Zion, to give unto them beauty for ashes, the oil of joy for mourning, the garment of praise for the spirit of heaviness; that they might be called trees of righteousness, the planting of the LORD, that he might be glorified. (Isaiah 61:1–3)

1. The near view: who is "me," and who is mourning?

Parry's advice is to start here: "to better understand Isaiah, readers should begin by examining the book in its immediate context." With Isaiah 61, that raises a real dispute. Most critical scholars assign chapters 56–66 to a later prophet or school ("Third Isaiah," or Trito-Isaiah) working after the return from Babylon, in a Jerusalem still partly in ruins (Wikipedia, "Isaiah 61", citing Coggins in The Oxford Bible Commentary). Many Latter-day Saint scholars, who treat Isaiah as a single book, read these chapters as Isaiah of Jerusalem speaking ahead to that same post-exilic situation. On either view, the text itself describes the same audience. Verse 4 promises that "they shall build the old wastes" and "repair the waste cities." Isaiah 64:10–11 describes "Zion… a wilderness" and "our holy and our beautiful house… burned up with fire." The mourners in 61:3 are people living among the ash heaps of a ruined city.

The speaker is a first-person "me" who claims the Spirit and an anointing. Within this very block, God has just promised, "My spirit that is upon thee, and my words which I have put in thy mouth, shall not depart out of thy mouth" (59:21). The language also recalls the servant of the earlier chapters: "I have put my spirit upon him" (42:1). Yet "servant" never appears in 61:1–3; the speaker acts as the servant without the title (Wikipedia, "Isaiah 61"). The verb "anointed" is the Hebrew mashach, the root of mashiach, "messiah." In Israel's practice, kings and priests were anointed; a prophet is anointed rarely (Elijah is told to anoint Elisha, 1 Kings 19:16). The near-view reading, then, is a prophet claiming a royal-priestly commission to announce good news to a demoralized community, and promising that they themselves will be called "the Priests of the LORD" (61:6).

The near view also explains the vocabulary. "Liberty" is deror, the technical word of the jubilee: "proclaim liberty throughout all the land" (Leviticus 25:10; compare Jeremiah 34:8 and Ezekiel 46:17, "the year of liberty"). To a community where debt and mortgaged land were live problems (compare Nehemiah 5:1–5), "the acceptable year of the LORD" would have sounded like a jubilee, a year of release.

2. The distant view: Nazareth

Parry's second outlook reads Isaiah's words as aimed at events "centuries or even millennia" later. In Luke 4:16–21, Jesus stands in the Nazareth synagogue, is handed "the book of the prophet Esaias," reads, and then "closed the book… and sat down," saying, "This day is this scripture fulfilled in your ears." The Church's lesson page calls this "one of the Savior's most straightforward declarations that He was the Anointed One" (Come, Follow Me, October 12–18). The verb carries the claim: the one the LORD "hath anointed" (mashach) is the Messiah, the Anointed One, which is what the Greek title Christ means.

Two textual details matter. First, Luke's wording follows the Greek Septuagint rather than the Hebrew. "Recovering of sight to the blind" comes from the Greek of Isaiah 61:1, and "to set at liberty them that are bruised" is not from Isaiah 61 at all; it comes from the Greek of Isaiah 58:6, "to let the oppressed go free" (intertextual.bible, "The Two Passages of Isaiah Behind Jesus' Sermon"). Jesus, or Luke, joins the true fast of chapter 58 to the anointed messenger of chapter 61. The Hebrew phrase behind "the opening of the prison," peqach-qoach, uses a verb normally used of opening eyes, which helps explain why the Greek translators heard sight in it.

Second, Jesus stops reading in the middle of verse 2. He reads "To preach the acceptable year of the Lord" (Luke 4:19) and closes the book before "and the day of vengeance of our God." Whatever else that pause means, it means that on that Sabbath the anointing was announced as release, not as reckoning.

3. Multiple fulfillment: a prophecy that keeps arriving

Parry's third outlook allows that a prophecy can be "fulfilled two or more times," and he cites the image of a "layer-cake" from Madsen and Hopkin's Opening Isaiah: A Harmony. Isaiah 61:1–3 is a clear case, because scripture itself records the layers.

The first layer is the near view: a ruined Jerusalem promised rebuilding (61:4). The second is Nazareth. The third is in Doctrine and Covenants 138, where President Joseph F. Smith sees the Son of God appear among the righteous dead, "declaring liberty to the captives who had been faithful" (D&C 138:18), and then sending messengers "to declare the acceptable day of the Lord and proclaim liberty to the captives who were bound" (138:31). The vision even places Isaiah there, as the one "who declared by prophecy that the Redeemer was anointed" to open the prison (138:42). In Parry's own chapter, a list he quotes from Elder Jeffrey R. Holland connects Isaiah 61:1 with "Christ visits the spirits in Prison" (Parry, "Ways to Read Isaiah"). The "prison," which on the near view was literal captivity or debt slavery, becomes the spirit prison.

The fourth layer is inside Isaiah itself. The half-verse Jesus did not read comes back two chapters later, in the mouth of the warrior in red garments: "For the day of vengeance is in mine heart, and the year of my redeemed is come" (63:4), a passage the Doctrine and Covenants applies to the Second Coming (D&C 133:46–53). Read as multiple fulfillment, the pause in Luke 4 marks a gap between comings: the year of release was proclaimed at Nazareth; the day of vengeance waits for the one "that cometh from Edom" (63:1).

4. Likening: from Nephi to Qumran

Parry grounds his fourth outlook in Nephi: "I will liken his words unto my people… and ye may liken them unto you and unto all men" (2 Nephi 11:2, 8; see also 1 Nephi 19:23–24). His examples are questions a reader puts to themselves: how am I like the people Isaiah addressed?

Isaiah 61 was being likened well before Nazareth. A Dead Sea Scroll from Qumran Cave 11, known as 11Q13 or 11QMelchizedek and dated around 100 BC, takes up the jubilee law of Leviticus 25 together with Deuteronomy 15 and Isaiah 61:1, and reads them as describing the last days and its own community: the "captives" to whom liberty is proclaimed are the faithful of the sect, and the one who proclaims it is a heavenly Melchizedek who releases them from their iniquities (Wikipedia, "11Q13"; Logan Williams, "Melchizedek, the Son of Man, and Eschatological Jubilee," Journal for the Study of the New Testament, 2023, link). The text also draws on Isaiah 52:7, the messenger who "bringeth good tidings," the same verb (basser) as "to preach good tidings" in 61:1.

This kind of interpretation is called pesher, after the Hebrew word for "interpretation" that Qumran commentators used to introduce their readings. Parry does not use the term in his chapter; the comparison is this essay's. But it sharpens what likening is and is not. Pesher claims that the text's real meaning is about us. Likening, as Nephi describes it, makes a more modest claim: the words belong first to the house of Israel, and we may apply them to ourselves because we stand in a similar place. For a modern reader, likening Isaiah 61 means asking the questions Parry models. Where am I living among old wastes? Who around me is one of "them that are bound"? Am I someone who mourns in Zion, or someone sent to comfort them?

5. Personal reading: what the verse says to you this week

Parry's fifth outlook is the most open-ended. A reader may come to a passage "for personal instruction, spiritual guidance, devotionalistic purposes, or to receive personal revelation." He supports this with President Dallin H. Oaks's statement that scripture reading can lead to revelation "on whatever else the Lord wishes to communicate to the reader at that time." This outlook frees a reading from the passage's original subject: a phrase like "beauty for ashes" can speak to a widow or to someone grieving a lost career without either situation appearing anywhere in Isaiah 61.

Parry gives a sample list of verses for particular needs, and three of them come from this week's chapters: "Then shalt thou call, and the LORD shall answer" (58:9) for unanswered prayer; "we are the clay, and thou our potter" (64:8) for times when we feel too self-important; and "As one whom his mother comforteth, so will I comfort you" (66:13) for those who need divine comfort. Isaiah 61:1–3 belongs on such a list, and the lesson page asks directly: "How has He given beauty in place of ashes?" The answer will be specific to each reader, and may have nothing to do with Zion or jubilee. That is what this outlook is for.

6. Doctrine: the grammar of exchange

Parry ends with doctrine, and it is the outlook where he does his most text-level work. He counts Hebrew roots: the verb for atonement (kpr) five times in Isaiah, "Redeemer, to redeem" (gaʼal) twenty-five, "Savior, salvation, to save" (yashaʻ) twenty, "to ransom" (padah) four, and argues that "a careful study of each Isaianic passage" using them "will yield significant gospel understanding." Several of those occurrences fall in this block: "the Redeemer shall come to Zion" (59:20); "I the LORD am thy Saviour and thy Redeemer" (60:16); "the year of my redeemed" (63:4); "thou, O LORD, art our father, our redeemer" (63:16); and, right after our passage, "he hath clothed me with the garments of salvation" (61:10).

Isaiah 61:1–3 does not use any of the four words, yet it may be the clearest picture of what they mean. The doctrine lies in a small preposition repeated three times. In Hebrew, tachat means "instead of" or "in place of": "beauty for ashes, the oil of joy for mourning, the garment of praise for the spirit of heaviness." The first pair is a wordplay. "Beauty" is peʼer, a turban or headdress, and "ashes" is ʼefer. The two words share almost the same letters, so the mourner's ashes on the head are replaced by a festive headdress that sounds nearly the same. Oil, the substance of the speaker's anointing in verse 1, is now poured on the mourners. The maʻateh tehillah, the "garment of praise," replaces a faint, dimmed spirit. The one anointed in verse 1 passes his anointing on in verse 3.

This is the doctrine of redemption in its simplest form: something ruined is taken away, and something given is put in its place. The jubilee language says the same thing in economic terms. Under deror, a person who lost their land does not earn it back; it is returned to them because the year has come (Leviticus 25:10, 13). Then the passage ends by saying what the mourners become: "trees of righteousness, the planting of the LORD, that he might be glorified" (61:3). The righteousness in that phrase is planted by the LORD, not grown by the mourners themselves. That is worth setting next to the much-quoted lament later in the block, "all our righteousnesses are as filthy rags" (64:6). They do not contradict each other: Isaiah 64 describes what a people's own righteousness amounts to on its own; Isaiah 61 describes what righteousness becomes when God plants it. The garment of 61:3 and the rags of 64:6 are both made of cloth. What differs is who provides the clothing, as 61:10 confirms: "he hath clothed me."

Finally, the proportions are doctrinal too. The text proclaims a "year" of favor and a "day" of vengeance (61:2), and it does the same at 63:4 ("the day of vengeance… the year of my redeemed"). Isaiah does not deny judgment. But in both places he gives mercy the longer measure of time, and at Nazareth Jesus read only the year.

Reading with all six at once

The outlooks are not a ranking. Parry says he would begin with the near view, which keeps the passage from becoming a mere proof text: Isaiah 61 was good news for people sitting in real ashes before it was anything else. The later layers carry the same words to Nazareth, the spirit world, and the Second Coming; likening and personal reading carry them into the reader's week. The doctrinal reading explains why they survive every move: in each setting, the Anointed One gives something in exchange for something ruined. Parry's closing image is of a book whose truths a reader can search for a lifetime "without ever exhausting" them. Three verses of Isaiah 61, read six ways, are a fair test of that claim.
