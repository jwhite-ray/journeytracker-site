The Fast That Costs Something: Isaiah 58–66 for an Ordinary Week

Written by an AI persona on living the week's text — not a real person.

Isaiah 58 opens with a complaint most churchgoers have made at least once, quietly: "Wherefore have we fasted, say they, and thou seest not? wherefore have we afflicted our soul, and thou takest no knowledge?" (58:3). These people are not lazy or irreligious. The LORD himself says they "seek me daily" and "take delight in approaching to God" (58:2). They keep the schedule. They skip the meals. And they feel ignored.

Much of this block is vision, but it is bracketed by plain instructions about what to do with your bread, your finger, your mouth, your Sunday, and your house. What follows takes the plain parts one at a time, with questions meant to be answered rather than admired.

What the fast was hiding

God's answer to the complaint is specific and a little embarrassing: "Behold, in the day of your fast ye find pleasure, and exact all your labours. Behold, ye fast for strife and debate, and to smite with the fist of wickedness" (58:3–4). The fasters are still driving their workers on the fast day. They are hungry and irritable and fighting. The ritual is intact; the week around it is not.

Jewish congregations still hear this passage on the most solemn fast of their year: Isaiah 57:14–58:14 is the morning haftarah for Yom Kippur, read to people who have not eaten, and its point is that abstaining from food is not the point (My Jewish Learning, "Yom Kippur Haftarah: Isaiah 57:14–58:14"). Latter-day Saints have a monthly version of that day, and the same risk. Fast Sunday can be the most short-tempered day of the month in a household: a parent snapping at children in the car, quiet resentment toward a spouse who "isn't really fasting," an argument the hunger makes worse.

  • Who bore the cost of my last fast besides me? Was anyone at home or at work treated worse because I was hungry?
  • Did I "exact all [my] labours," pressing the people who owe me something (employees, children, a contractor) while asking God to press less on me?

The fast God chose has names and addresses

The definition in 58:6–7 is a list of verbs with objects: loose the bands, undo the burdens, "deal thy bread to the hungry," "bring the poor that are cast out to thy house," cover the naked, and "hide not thyself from thine own flesh." That last phrase is the one most easily skipped. "Thine own flesh" is kin. Isaiah does not let the generous helper of strangers off the hook for the relative he avoids: the brother whose calls go to voicemail, the aging parent visited less often than the ministering assignment, the adult child whose choices are easier not to discuss.

For Latter-day Saints, "deal thy bread to the hungry" has an institutional form, the fast offering, and the chapter practically asks you to look at the number. President Spencer W. Kimball put it bluntly at a 1974 welfare session: "I think we should be very generous and give, instead of the amount we saved by our two meals of fasting, perhaps much, much more—ten times more where we are in a position to do it" (Spencer W. Kimball, address at the Welfare Services Meeting, April 6, 1974). Note the qualifier he kept: "where we are in a position to do it." It is a question for the comfortable household, not a guilt instrument for the family already counting coins.

A concrete exercise: write down what your household actually spent on its last two ordinary meals, honestly priced, not the cheapest imaginable version. Compare that to your last fast offering. Then ask whether you are "in a position" for more.

President Henry B. Eyring's talk titled from 58:6 tells of a grandson who found long fasts hard, then chose to keep fasting when a school friend lost a cousin, because he understood his sacrifice could reach someone in need. Eyring's claim is that the offering reaches further than the stomach: "Your fast offering will do more than help feed and clothe bodies. It will heal and change hearts" (Henry B. Eyring, "Is Not This the Fast That I Have Chosen?" April 2015). The practical lesson from the grandson: a fast with a named purpose holds up better than a fast with only a duration. Before the next one, name a person.

"The putting forth of the finger"

The promises of 58:8–12 are conditional, and one condition sounds strange to modern ears: "If thou take away from the midst of thee the yoke, the putting forth of the finger, and speaking vanity" (58:9). In the ancient Near East, pointing the finger was the gesture of formal accusation. The laws of Hammurabi (§127) punish anyone who points the finger at a priestess or another man's wife and cannot prove the charge, and John Walton's background commentary reads Isaiah's phrase against that law as malicious slander (Walton, Matthews, and Chavalas, The IVP Bible Background Commentary: Old Testament, p. 638, as cited at intertextual.bible).

Look at the finger's neighbors in the verse. The yoke is economic oppression; the finger and "speaking vanity" are social oppression. Isaiah treats a reputation destroyed by talk as the same kind of burden as a debt that cannot be paid. The modern finger is often typed: the ward group chat, the family thread, the side conversation after a meeting at work, the "I'm just concerned about her" that is not really concern.

The block keeps circling back to this. In 65:5 the LORD calls people who say "Stand by thyself, come not near to me; for I am holier than thou" "a smoke in my nose." In 66:5 the faithful are told that "your brethren that hated you, that cast you out for my name's sake, said, Let the LORD be glorified." The excluders were religious and used pious language while they did it. It is worth checking both seats: Have I been cast out by someone who said "Let the LORD be glorified"? Have I done the casting?

  • In the last month, have I told a story about someone that I could not prove, or would not tell in front of them?
  • Is there a person I have, in effect, told to "stand by thyself"?

Repairer of the breach

The reward for that kind of fast is a title: "thou shalt be called, The repairer of the breach, The restorer of paths to dwell in" (58:12). A breach is a hole in a wall, the place where the enemy gets in. In a family it is usually a specific break with a specific date: the funeral where something was said, the loan never repaid, the wedding someone skipped. Isaiah gives the name not to the person who was right but to the one who repairs.

Many critical scholars set chapters 56–66 after the return from Babylon, in a disappointed, divided community living among literally ruined walls; many Latter-day Saint readers hold to a single Isaiah. Either way the image is of people surrounded by rubble who are told that some of them will be known for stacking stones back up.

A concrete step: identify one breach you could do something about this week without needing the other person to move first. A short note, a call, an invitation, an apology for your share only.

Calling the Sabbath a delight

The chapter closes by turning to the Sabbath: "If thou turn away thy foot from the sabbath, from doing thy pleasure on my holy day; and call the sabbath a delight... not doing thine own ways, nor finding thine own pleasure, nor speaking thine own words: Then shalt thou delight thyself in the LORD" (58:13–14). "Speaking thine own words" is concrete. It includes the Sunday afternoon spent rehearsing Monday's problems out loud, and the dinner conversation that is really a staff meeting.

President Russell M. Nelson described moving from lists of Sabbath dos and don'ts to one question: "What sign do I want to give to God?" (Russell M. Nelson, "The Sabbath Is a Delight," April 2015). The question moves the decision from rules to relationship, which is what Isaiah's word "delight" does. Nobody delights in a list.

  • What do I do on Sunday that is technically permitted but is really Monday leaking backward (email, budgeting, worrying about work out loud)?
  • Would my children, if asked, describe our Sundays as a delight or as a list of things they cannot do?

When truth falls in the street

Chapter 59 describes a whole culture: "justice standeth afar off: for truth is fallen in the street, and equity cannot enter. Yea, truth faileth; and he that departeth from evil maketh himself a prey" (59:14–15). The last clause is the honest part. Where truth is lying in the street, the person who refuses to join in becomes a target. Isaiah does not promise that honesty will pay at work. He says it may cost you.

Most workplace dishonesty is not dramatic fraud but small accommodation to a culture where everyone does it: the timesheet that rounds up, the status report that says "on track" when it isn't, the sick day that wasn't, the meeting where you nod at a claim you know is false because correcting it would make you the difficult one. Verse 13 speaks of "conceiving and uttering from the heart words of falsehood," a precise description of the lie rehearsed before it is spoken.

Then the turn: the LORD "saw that there was no man, and wondered that there was no intercessor" (59:16). The scandal is not only that people lied but that no one stood in the gap. Sometimes the disciple's job at work is to be the one person in the room who says the true thing calmly, and to accept being, for a while, a prey. Where in my work is the truth lying in the street, and have I stepped over it? If I picked it up, what would it actually cost me?

Beauty for ashes, when someone is grieving

Isaiah 61:1–2 is the passage Jesus read in Nazareth (Luke 4:16–21), and the next verse holds one of the most tangible promises in scripture: "to give unto them beauty for ashes, the oil of joy for mourning, the garment of praise for the spirit of heaviness" (61:3). In Hebrew it is wordplay, peʼer for ʼepher, nearly the same letters rearranged. The peʼer is properly a headdress or turban, and the picture is of the ashes on a mourner's head replaced by a festive head covering (Albert Barnes, Notes on the Bible, on Isaiah 61:3).

That matters for anyone ministering to someone in grief, because every exchange in the verse is physical: a head covering, oil, a garment. Isaiah's comfort comes with objects, not explanations. The Savior does the deep work of binding up "the brokenhearted" (61:1), but the people around a mourner can do small physical things. Bring the meal. Do the laundry. Sit at the funeral. Then remember that the ashes are still on the head long after the casseroles stop; the third month and the first anniversary are often lonelier than the first week. Who do I know whose loss was six months to two years ago, past the point where most people stopped asking? What is one physical thing I could do for them this week, beyond a text that says "thinking of you"?

Watchmen who give God no rest

Chapter 62 commissions another kind of disciple: "I have set watchmen upon thy walls, O Jerusalem, which shall never hold their peace day nor night: ye that make mention of the LORD, keep not silence, And give him no rest" (62:6–7). The watchmen's job is to keep reminding God. That is permission for persistent, repetitive prayer for the same person or need, long after it feels redundant.

Chapters 63 and 64 show what such prayer can sound like, and it is not polite: "where is thy zeal and thy strength... are they restrained?" (63:15); "Oh that thou wouldest rend the heavens, that thou wouldest come down" (64:1); "wilt thou hold thy peace, and afflict us very sore?" (64:12). These are the prayers of tired people, and scripture keeps them. Someone who has prayed for years for a wayward child, a strained marriage, or a chronic illness does not need to tidy the prayer before offering it. In the middle of the complaint sits the anchor: "But now, O LORD, thou art our father; we are the clay, and thou our potter" (64:8). The clay verse is not spoken from serenity. It is the turn a tired person makes while still asking where God is.

A practice: choose one person or need you stopped praying about because it seemed pointless. Put it back on the wall. Pray about it every day for seven days, honestly, complaint included.

Building houses and living in them

The new-creation promises of 65:17–25 are cosmic, but their details are domestic: "They shall build houses, and inhabit them; and they shall plant vineyards, and eat the fruit of them. They shall not build, and another inhabit... They shall not labour in vain" (65:21–23). The curse being reversed is working hard while someone else enjoys the result. Isaiah stays realistic even here: "the child shall die an hundred years old" (65:20). The promise is not endless life but a full one, in which your work stays yours.

That speaks to anyone whose labor feels futile right now: the parent whose work at home is invisible, the employee whose project was cancelled. Isaiah does not call the futility imaginary. He says it is not the last word. And it turns a question back on us: whose labor in my own household do I enjoy without acknowledging it?

The one God looks at

Near the end the LORD waves off the idea that humans can house him: "The heaven is my throne, and the earth is my footstool: where is the house that ye build unto me?" (66:1). Then he names what he does look at: "to this man will I look, even to him that is poor and of a contrite spirit, and trembleth at my word" (66:2).

That is a description of how to read. To tremble at a word is to expect it to change something. These nine chapters hold many words that, taken seriously, would change Monday: what you give, what you say about people, what goes on the timesheet, whom you call, whom you pray for. The question at week's end is not how much of Isaiah you understood, but which verse you let make you tremble, and what you did about it.
