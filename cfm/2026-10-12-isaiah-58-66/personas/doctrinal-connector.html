One Verse, Three Fulfillments: Isaiah 58–66 Read Through the Restoration

Written by an AI persona connecting the block to Restoration scripture and modern teaching — not a real person.

Modern revelation does not just footnote the last nine chapters of Isaiah. It quotes some of them nearly whole, rewrites others, and assigns several to particular moments in the plan of salvation. That makes this block a good test of how Restoration scripture actually uses Isaiah: it divides his prophecies among different times and places, and sometimes it quietly changes what he says.

Two ground rules first. The Book of Mormon quotes Isaiah 2–14 and 48–54 at length, but it does not reproduce any of Isaiah 58–66 as a block quotation. The connections here run through the Doctrine and Covenants and the Pearl of Great Price. And the Joseph Smith Translation barely touches these chapters. The Church's footnotes record only two JST readings in the block. At 60:22, "I the LORD will hasten it in his time," the JST reads "my" for the pronoun. At 63:17, "why hast thou made us to err from thy ways, and hardened our heart," the JST reads "why hast thou suffered us to err from thy ways, and to harden our heart." God permits rather than causes, in line with the agency doctrine of 2 Nephi 2. That is a theological correction of the lament, not a recovered manuscript reading. Nothing in the JST softens the bloodied warrior of 63:1–6, the "filthy rags" of 64:6, death at a hundred in 65:20, or the undying worm of 66:24. Whatever modern revelation does with those passages, it does elsewhere.

The fast and the Sabbath: one chapter, one Sunday

Isaiah 58 holds two paragraphs that Latter-day Saints have folded into one monthly practice. The first (58:3–12) is God's answer to people who complain, "Wherefore have we fasted… and thou seest not?" (58:3). They really did abstain, but "in the day of your fast ye find pleasure, and exact all your labours" (58:3). The fast God chooses is social and economic: "to loose the bands of wickedness, to undo the heavy burdens… to deal thy bread to the hungry, and that thou bring the poor that are cast out to thy house" (58:6–7). The second paragraph (58:13–14) makes the Sabbath "a delight."

In April 2015, two General Conference talks took up these two paragraphs. President Henry B. Eyring's "Is Not This the Fast That I Have Chosen?" quoted 58:6–11 in full as "the Lord's description of the commandment and the blessing available to those of us in His Church," then gave it an institutional form: "In the Church today we are offered the opportunity to fast once a month and give a generous fast offering through our bishop or branch president for the benefit of the poor and the needy." That move matters. Nothing in Isaiah's complaint suggests the fasters' abstention fed anyone. The fast offering is built so that the skipped meals become bread "dealt" to the hungry, which answers 58:7 literally rather than as a metaphor. Eyring widened the circle beyond the ward: "Part of your fast offering and mine this month will be used to help someone, somewhere, whose relief the Lord will feel as if it were His own."

Elder Russell M. Nelson's "The Sabbath Is a Delight" quoted 58:13–14 and described how he gave up "lists of things to do and things not to do on the Sabbath" and asked instead, "What sign do I want to give to God?" That is a fair reading of Isaiah, which is short on rules. Isaiah's test is whose will governs the day: "not doing thine own ways, nor finding thine own pleasure, nor speaking thine own words" (58:13).

The Restoration text that joins the two paragraphs is D&C 59, given in Missouri in 1831, which Nelson also quoted. It sends the Saints "to the house of prayer" to "offer up thy sacraments upon my holy day" (D&C 59:9), and then redefines the vocabulary of fasting: "let thy food be prepared with singleness of heart that thy fasting may be perfect, or, in other words, that thy joy may be full. Verily, this is fasting and prayer, or in other words, rejoicing and prayer" (D&C 59:13–14). Isaiah set fasting, an affliction of soul, beside the Sabbath, a delight. D&C 59 erases the line between them: rightly done, fasting is rejoicing. Its promise, "the fulness of the earth is yours" (59:16), parallels 58:14, "I will cause thee to ride upon the high places of the earth." Fast Sunday is where both halves of Isaiah 58 land in Latter-day Saint practice.

Isaiah 61:1–2: one prophecy, divided three ways

The clearest case of Restoration scripture distributing Isaiah across the plan of salvation is 61:1–2: "The Spirit of the Lord GOD is upon me; because the LORD hath anointed me… to proclaim liberty to the captives, and the opening of the prison to them that are bound; to proclaim the acceptable year of the LORD, and the day of vengeance of our God."

Who is "me"? In its own setting the speaker may be the prophet, or the servant figure of earlier chapters. The New Testament gives the Christian answer: in the Nazareth synagogue Jesus read the passage and said, "This day is this scripture fulfilled in your ears" (Luke 4:21). But Luke's reading ends with "To preach the acceptable year of the Lord" (Luke 4:19), and then "he closed the book." The day of vengeance goes unread. Restoration scripture picks up both halves and gives each one a place.

Liberty to the captives goes to the spirit world. In his 1918 vision, President Joseph F. Smith saw the Son of God among the righteous dead, "declaring liberty to the captives who had been faithful" (D&C 138:18), then saw messengers sent out "to declare the acceptable day of the Lord and proclaim liberty to the captives who were bound, even unto all who would repent of their sins and receive the gospel" (D&C 138:31). Like Jesus at Nazareth, 138:31 stops at "the acceptable day." The vision even names its source. Among the assembled prophets was "Isaiah, who declared by prophecy that the Redeemer was anointed to bind up the broken-hearted, to proclaim liberty to the captives, and the opening of the prison to them that were bound" (D&C 138:42). Here "the prison" of 61:1 is the spirit prison, and opening it means preaching to the dead and performing ordinances for them, including "vicarious baptism for the remission of sins" (D&C 138:33). Joseph Smith had reached for the same language in 1842: "for the prisoners shall go free" (D&C 128:22).

The day of vengeance goes to the Second Coming. D&C 133, the 1831 "Appendix" revelation, reworks Isaiah 63:1–9, and there the returning Lord says, "I have trodden the wine-press alone… for this was the day of vengeance which was in my heart. And now the year of my redeemed is come" (D&C 133:50–52). The words Jesus left unread in Nazareth are finally spoken, by him.

Read through the Restoration, then, Isaiah 61:1–2 has three fulfillments: at Nazareth in mortality, in the spirit world after death, and at the Second Coming.

The red garments: what D&C 133 changes

D&C 133 is not a straight quotation of Isaiah 63, and the differences are worth reading side by side. Isaiah asks, "Who is this that cometh from Edom, with dyed garments from Bozrah?" (63:1). D&C 133:46 asks, "Who is this that cometh down from God in heaven with dyed garments; yea, from the regions which are not known." The geography is gone. Elsewhere the D&C decodes the name: the Lord "shall come down in judgment upon Idumea, or the world" (D&C 1:36). Edom becomes everyone.

The tense shifts too. Isaiah's warrior speaks of the future: "I will tread them in mine anger… their blood shall be sprinkled upon my garments" (63:3). In D&C 133 the deed is done: "I have trodden the wine-press alone, and have brought judgment upon all people… their blood have I sprinkled upon my garments" (133:50–51). And D&C 133 runs straight from the vengeance into Isaiah 63:7–9 without a break: "they shall mention the loving kindness of their Lord… In all their afflictions he was afflicted. And the angel of his presence saved them" (133:52–53). The bloodied warrior and the God who suffers with his people are one figure in one speech. That does not remove the violence of 63:1–6, and readers should not pretend it does. But it does not let the winepress stand without the compassion of 63:9.

The phrase "trodden the winepress alone" gets a second life in Restoration scripture, shifted toward the Atonement. D&C 76:107 and 88:106 describe the Lamb who "hath overcome and trodden the wine-press alone, even the wine-press of the fierceness of the wrath of Almighty God." Elder Jeffrey R. Holland built his April 2009 talk "None Were with Him" on 63:3 and 63:5, reading them as Gethsemane and Calvary. He spoke of the moment when "the Father briefly withdrew from Jesus the comfort of His Spirit, the support of His personal presence," and concluded: "because Jesus walked such a long, lonely path utterly alone, we do not have to do so." This is a Christological application, not a claim about Isaiah's original setting. In both Isaiah and D&C 133:51 the blood on the garments is the enemies'. But a reader following Holland will also hear Mosiah 3:7, "blood cometh from every pore," which the Church's lesson pairs with these chapters, and Alma 7:12, where Christ takes on his people's infirmities "that he may know according to the flesh how to succor his people." That is "in all their affliction he was afflicted" (63:9) worked out as doctrine.

Clothing, names, and the house

Isaiah 61:10 reads like a temple verse to Latter-day Saints, though no temple is mentioned: "he hath clothed me with the garments of salvation, he hath covered me with the robe of righteousness, as a bridegroom decketh himself with ornaments, and as a bride adorneth herself with her jewels." The Kirtland Temple dedicatory prayer of 1836 asks "that our garments may be pure, that we may be clothed upon with robes of righteousness" (D&C 109:76). In both places the point is being clothed by God rather than clothing oneself. In 59:17 God puts on "righteousness as a breastplate, and an helmet of salvation"; in 61:10 he dresses his people the same way, in wedding clothes.

Chapter 62 continues with names: "thou shalt be called by a new name, which the mouth of the LORD shall name" (62:2), namely "Hephzi-bah" and "Beulah," which the verse glosses itself: "for the LORD delighteth in thee, and thy land shall be married" (62:4). D&C 130:11 makes the new name individual: "a white stone is given to each of those who come into the celestial kingdom, whereon is a new name written, which no man knoweth save he that receiveth it." Isaiah renames a city; the Restoration renames persons. Add 61:6, "ye shall be named the Priests of the LORD," and 66:21, where God takes "priests and… Levites" from the gathered nations, and the block offers a priesthood not bound to a hereditary line.

Then comes the hard case for a temple-building church: "The heaven is my throne, and the earth is my footstool: where is the house that ye build unto me?… but to this man will I look, even to him that is poor and of a contrite spirit, and trembleth at my word" (66:1–2). Stephen used it against the Jerusalem establishment: "the most High dwelleth not in temples made with hands" (Acts 7:48–50). Modern revelation commands temples, "build a house to my name, for the Most High to dwell therein" (D&C 124:27), but it makes God's presence conditional in Isaiah's own terms: "all the pure in heart that shall come into it shall see God. But if it be defiled I will not come into it" (D&C 97:16–17), and "this is Zion—the pure in heart" (97:21). That is 66:2 in a new key. The house is commanded, but what God looks to is the contrite person inside it.

New heavens, new earth, and death at a hundred

Isaiah 65:17–25 is the block's millennial vision. The tenth Article of Faith states the doctrine in brief: "the earth will be renewed and receive its paradisiacal glory." D&C 101 echoes Isaiah closely: "the enmity of beasts… shall cease" (101:26; compare 65:25), and "they shall plant vineyards, and they shall eat the fruit thereof" (101:101; compare 65:21).

The telling move is on 65:20. Isaiah's renewed world still has death: "the child shall die an hundred years old." D&C 101:30 borrows Isaiah's own image from 65:22 ("as the days of a tree are the days of my people"): "an infant shall not die until he is old; and his life shall be as the age of a tree." Then it adds what Isaiah lacks: "when he dies he shall not sleep, that is to say in the earth, but shall be changed in the twinkling of an eye" (101:31). D&C 63:50–51 is plainer still: "it is appointed to him to die at the age of man… old men shall die; but they shall not sleep in the dust." The D&C keeps Isaiah's realism. There is still death in the Millennium; there is no burial.

Restoration teaching also separates this paradisiacal earth from a later celestial one, which "shall be crowned with glory, even with the presence of God the Father" (D&C 88:19) and made "like unto crystal" (D&C 130:9). On that reading Isaiah 60:19–20, "the sun shall be no more thy light by day… but the LORD shall be unto thee an everlasting light," looks past the Millennium to the earth's final state, while D&C 45:58 supplies the millennial social picture: "their children shall grow up without sin unto salvation."

The last verse

The book ends with corpses: "their worm shall not die, neither shall their fire be quenched" (66:24). Jesus uses the line of Gehenna (Mark 9:48), and so does D&C 76:44, "where their worm dieth not, and the fire is not quenched." But D&C 76 narrows its reach sharply. The Son "saves all the works of his hands, except those sons of perdition who deny the Son after the Father has revealed him" (76:43), and of their end "no man knows" (76:45). The Restoration does not erase Isaiah's last verse, but it confines it to a small group and declines to describe it further. Set beside D&C 138, the shape is clear: the prison of 61:1 opens, and the fire of 66:24 is kept for those who reject the light knowingly. Between them lies the long middle of the plan of salvation, which is where these chapters keep putting the reader: fasting for the poor, keeping the Sabbath as a delight, and waiting for the one who comes "mighty to save" (63:1).
