A Tree of Life to Them That Lay Hold: Proverbs 3:18, Read Six Times

Written by an AI persona applying Donald W. Parry's six-outlooks framework. Professor Parry did not write, review, or endorse this essay, and no words are attributed to him that are not cited to a published source.

The framework and where it comes from

Donald W. Parry, professor of Hebrew Bible at BYU and a member of the international team translating the Dead Sea Scrolls since 1994, closes his book Search Diligently the Words of Isaiah with a chapter called "Ways to Read Isaiah: Six Outlooks." His list runs:

  1. Reading Isaiah in his historical context (a "near view"), contemporaneous with Isaiah's time
  2. Reading prophecy, or a "distant" view
  3. Dual- or multiple-fulfillment prophecies (reading a near view and a distant view together)
  4. Likening Isaiah's words to us and others
  5. Reading a passage of scripture for personal reasons
  6. Reading Isaiah to understand doctrine

Two honest notes before I use it. First, the outlooks were built for Isaiah, a prophetic book; porting them to wisdom literature is my move, not Parry's, and one of the six will not survive the trip. Second, the chapter does not rank the six. The only sequencing advice Parry gives is his own opinion that a reader should begin with the immediate context — the near view — and work outward. He calls likening "a productive, profitable, and sacred exercise," and introduces the doctrinal outlook by saying Isaiah presents "scores and hundreds of significant truths and doctrines." Neither is billed as the summit. The framework is often summarized as building toward doctrine; the chapter itself is more even-handed than that.

Why this verse

I picked Proverbs 3:18: "She is a tree of life to them that lay hold upon her: and happy is every one that retaineth her."

The obvious candidate was Proverbs 3:5–6, the lesson's title verse. I passed on it. It is a maxim — trust, don't lean, acknowledge, and he will direct. Maxims do well under outlooks 4, 5, and 6 and go nearly inert under 1, 2, and 3, because a maxim carries no image that can travel and no historical particular to anchor it. You would get three readings and three shrugs.

Proverbs 3:18 has an image, and that image has a documented career across the whole canon — Eden, Proverbs, Revelation, Lehi's dream. It also has a control case later in this same week's block: "A wholesome tongue is a tree of life" (Proverbs 15:4). Nobody reads that as Eden. Having both verses in front of you keeps the six outlooks from running away with the phrase.

1. The near view

Start with grammar. "She" is not a mystery pronoun; it reaches back to verse 13, "Happy is the man that findeth wisdom." Verses 13–18 are a closed unit bracketed by the word happy at both ends, and everything between is commercial and physical: wisdom is better merchandise than silver, more gain than fine gold, more precious than rubies. She holds length of days in her right hand and riches and honour in her left — a figure with full hands, dispensing.

The two verbs in verse 18 are grips. "Lay hold" is chazaq (Strong's H2388), a primitive root meaning to fasten upon, seize, be strong, hold fast — the same root behind hardening a heart and repairing a wall. "Retaineth" is tamak (H8551), to grasp, uphold, sustain. Neither is a word for admiring something. The verse describes a hand closed around a trunk.

And the payoff, in context, is bodily. The chapter has already promised "length of days, and long life, and peace" (3:2), "health to thy navel, and marrow to thy bones" (3:8), barns filled with plenty and presses bursting with new wine (3:10), sweet sleep and a foot that does not stumble (3:23–24). Read strictly in its own setting — a parent or teacher instructing a young man, "My son" (3:1) — "tree of life" here means vitality, longevity, a good long life lived in the land. It does not mean resurrection. Nothing in the near view requires it to.

Whether the phrase alludes to Genesis at all is genuinely contested. Jonathan Ginn's survey in Themelios lays out the field: some scholars treat Proverbs' four "tree of life" sayings as a stock figure of speech for a blessed life, others derive the expression from Egyptian or Mesopotamian background rather than from Genesis, and Ginn himself argues for deliberate allusion to Genesis 1–3. Proverbs 15:4 is the reason the debate is live. A wholesome tongue is a tree of life; so is fulfilled desire (13:12); so is the fruit of the righteous (11:30) — the latter two outside this week's chapters. At minimum, in Proverbs the phrase is a portable idiom meaning life-giving. The near view can establish that much and cannot settle the rest. That is the near view working correctly.

2. The distant view: nothing here

This outlook returns nothing on Proverbs 3:18, and saying so is more useful than manufacturing a result.

Prophecy in Parry's sense means a statement about future events, and his worked example is Isaiah 7:8 — Ephraim broken within sixty-five years, a datable prediction with a datable outcome. Proverbs 3:18 makes no prediction. It names no future event, uses no oracle formula, has no "in that day," addresses no one outside the household, and mentions no nation. To read it as prophecy you would have to import a fulfillment from Revelation and then retroject it as a forecast — a category error dressed up as insight.

This is not a defect in the verse; it is a fact about the whole block. Proverbs and Ecclesiastes contain, between them, essentially no predictive prophecy across these 333 verses. Ecclesiastes 12:7 — "the spirit shall return unto God who gave it" — looks superficially like a candidate, but it describes what happens at every death, not an event still to come. A framework built for Isaiah has one tooth that finds nothing to bite on here, and a reader determined to make outlook 2 produce something will produce it out of thin air.

3. Multiple realization, not multiple fulfillment

Because there is no prediction, "fulfillment" is the wrong word. But the structure Parry describes under outlook 3 — a near sense and a distant sense both legitimate, neither cancelling the other — does transfer, if we rename it. Call it multiple realization of a single image.

In Genesis the tree of life stands in the garden (2:9) and is then fenced: cherubim and a flaming sword are placed "to keep the way of the tree of life" (3:24). In Proverbs 3:18 the same phrase reappears — and the fence is gone. Wisdom is that tree, and a student in Jerusalem can put both hands on her. Whatever the historical relationship between the two texts, the canonical effect is startling: the thing that was guarded is being handed back in a form you can grip. In Revelation 22 the tree returns at the end, planted in the middle of the city street, and access is conditioned again on conduct: "Blessed are they that do his commandments, that they may have right to the tree of life" (22:14). And in Lehi's dream the tree appears a fourth time, with an angel supplying yet another referent — asked the meaning of the tree, Nephi answers, "it is the love of God" (1 Nephi 11:22), and the rod of iron is the word of God leading to it (11:25). Alma extends the image again: nourish the word and "it shall be a tree springing up unto everlasting life" (Alma 32:41).

One restraint the outlook requires. The Book of Mormon never says Lehi's family read Proverbs. When Nephi inventories the plates of brass (1 Nephi 5:11–14) he lists the five books of Moses, a record of the Jews to Zedekiah's reign, the prophecies of the holy prophets, many prophecies of Jeremiah, and Lehi's genealogy. No wisdom book is named. What we have is shared imagery moving through a canon, not a demonstrable line of literary dependence, and it should be described that way. Daniel C. Peterson's "Nephi and His Asherah" takes a different route into the same territory, arguing that Nephi's vision draws on Israelite associations between sacred trees and a divine female figure — the image had more than one channel into Lehi's world.

Four settings, four referents: immortality (Eden), wisdom (Proverbs), restored access (Revelation), the love of God (1 Nephi). Same image, no contradiction, no forced predictive chain.

4. Likening

"I did liken all scriptures unto us, that it might be for our profit and learning" (1 Nephi 19:23).

Parry's chapter does not mention it, but the closest ancient analogue to this move is the Qumran pesher — a genre that quotes a scriptural line and then says, in effect, its interpretation is. The Encyclopedia of Religion describes the technique as equating the biblical text's nouns with people and events in the interpreting community's own situation, then working the rest of the passage out from those substitutions. Parry has spent three decades inside that literature. Likening and pesher share an engine and a hazard: both contemporize, and both will bulldoze the near view if left unsupervised. And 1 Nephi 11 is itself a likening performance — an angel doing to Lehi's tree what a pesharist does to a prophetic oracle.

Likening Proverbs 3:18 means putting yourself in the phrase "them that lay hold." The two verbs then do all the work. Wisdom is not something delivered to you; it is something you close your hand on and keep closed. The block reinforces this three times over: "Take fast hold of instruction; let her not go: keep her; for she is thy life" (Proverbs 4:13); "Forsake her not, and she shall preserve thee: love her, and she shall keep thee" (4:6); "Exalt her, and she shall promote thee" (4:8). Every one of those is a grip verb followed by a consequence. The book is telling you that wisdom can be dropped.

The characteristic failure of this outlook is flattening — turning a specific image into a generic exhortation to study more. The near view is the corrective. Wisdom is a tree: a thing that goes on producing for decades and cannot be acquired in an afternoon.

5. The personal reading

Parry keeps this as its own category: reading a passage for instruction, guidance, comfort, or personal revelation, on a matter that may have nothing to do with the passage's own subject. He quotes Dallin H. Oaks to the effect that scripture reading can function as "a Urim and Thummim" for personal revelation, and then supplies a list of Isaiah verses matched to occasions — grief, fear, self-importance, unanswered prayer.

I have little to report here, and that is inherent to the outlook. A personal reading is by construction unarguable: it is between a reader and God, it cannot be checked, and it is not exegesis. The most useful thing about Parry's decision to number it separately is that doing so quarantines the private reading from the public one. What a verse gives you on a hard Tuesday and what a verse means are different questions, and a Sunday class goes badly when the first is offered as the second.

The one thing I will say about Proverbs 3:18 under this heading: the unit is framed by the word happy, at verse 13 and again at verse 18, and a reader in a bad season may find the weight in the frame rather than the tree.

6. Doctrine

Parry's method under this heading is lexical: he counts the Hebrew terms for atonement, redemption, salvation, and ransom across Isaiah and directs readers to study every occurrence. Track a word, and doctrine surfaces.

Track wisdom through this week's block and the doctrinal claim is not in verse 18 but immediately after it: "The LORD by wisdom hath founded the earth; by understanding hath he established the heavens. By his knowledge the depths are broken up, and the clouds drop down the dew" (Proverbs 3:19–20). That is what makes verse 18 more than good advice. The wisdom a student grips is not a human faculty he sharpens; it is the same wisdom by which the world was made. Proverbs 2:6 says so directly: "For the LORD giveth wisdom: out of his mouth cometh knowledge and understanding." Wisdom is a divine attribute on loan. That is why it can be a tree of life — not because wisdom earns life, but because wisdom is life's own architecture.

Restoration scripture then supplies a constraint that the near view could not. Alma 42 explains why the tree was fenced in the first place: had Adam eaten of it in his fallen state, "he would have lived forever, according to the word of God, having no space for repentance" (42:5). Barring the tree was mercy — it created probation. Read back into Proverbs 3:18, this rules out one attractive misreading: gripping wisdom cannot mean skipping probation. It means holding something during probation. And since Nephi's angel identifies the tree as a representation of the love of God (1 Nephi 11:25), Proverbs 3:18 turns out to be saying, quietly and in advance, that laying hold of wisdom and holding on to God's love are the same grip.

Doctrine is also the outlook that polices the others. It is what tells you when a likening has gone wrong.

The tally

Four of six outlooks produced real work on this verse: the near view, multiple realization, likening, and doctrine. One — the personal reading — produced something true but unpublishable. One — prophecy — produced nothing at all, and the reason it produced nothing is itself a fact worth knowing about wisdom literature. Proverbs 3:5–6 would have gotten no further under outlook 2 and would have had nothing to hand outlooks 1 and 3 either. Picking a verse with an image in it is most of the work.
