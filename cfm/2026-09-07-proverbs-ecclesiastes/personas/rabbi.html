Know Him in All Your Ways: Mishlei and Kohelet as Jews Read Them

Written by an AI persona giving a Jewish reading in the voice of a rabbi — not a real rabbi.

If you want to know what Proverbs 3 means in Jewish life, don't start with a commentary. Start with a Shabbat morning: the reading finished, the scroll dressed and lifted, the congregation on its feet as it is carried back to the ark. What they are singing is Mishlei 3:18 and 3:17.

Etz ḥayyim hi la-maḥazikim bah, v'tomkheha me'ushar. D'rakheha darkhei no'am, v'khol n'tivoteha shalom. "She is a tree of life to those who hold fast to her, and those who support her are happy. Her ways are ways of pleasantness, and all her paths are peace."

The hymn built on 3:18 is sung in every Ashkenazi rite, with 3:17 chanted after it. Two verses of your block are, by a wide margin, the most frequently recited words in this lesson — heard every week by people who could not tell you the chapter number. And here is what a Christian reader almost never registers: the "her" being sung is not Lady Wisdom in the abstract. It is the Torah scroll in the cantor's arms. The liturgy performs an interpretation. Ḥokhmah — wisdom — is Torah.

Wisdom is the blueprint

That identification is not a pious flourish; it is load-bearing. Mishlei 3:19 says "YHWH b'ḥokhmah yasad aretz" — the LORD by wisdom founded the earth. Read that alongside the opening of Bereshit Rabbah and you get the rabbinic cosmology in one move. There, R. Hoshaya expounds Proverbs 8:30, where Wisdom says she was beside God as amon, and after running the word through several meanings lands on uman, artisan: the Torah is the architect's plan, and "the Holy One, blessed be He, looked into the Torah and created the world" (Bereshit Rabbah 1:1). Torah is prior to creation, and the world is built to its specifications.

Once you hold that, Mishlei stops being a collection of shrewd sayings and becomes a manual for living inside a structure you did not design. And darkhei no'am stops being a sentiment and becomes law. On Sukkah 32b, the Talmud is sorting out which plant qualifies for the four species, and the hirduf — oleander — is on the table. Abaye rules it out: "Its ways are ways of pleasantness" (Proverbs 3:17) — and this is not the case with a plant whose sharp leaves pierce the hand that holds it. Halakhah cannot require you to hold something that cuts you. Christian readers meet Proverbs 3:17 as poetry; in the Talmud it is a legal principle with teeth.

The fear that keeps wisdom from rotting

Mishlei 1:7: "Yir'at YHWH reshit da'at" — the fear of the LORD is the reshit of knowledge. Reshit is not merely "beginning." It is the word that opens the Torah — bereshit — and it carries the sense of first-fruits, the choice portion, the principle of the thing.

The rabbinic gloss on how this works is one of the sharpest images in the Talmud. On Shabbat 31a, Rava lists the questions a person is asked in the world to come — did you conduct your business faithfully, did you fix times for Torah — and then adds a condition: even so, "if the fear of the LORD is his treasure, yes; if not, no." The Gemara explains with a parable about a man who tells his agent to bring wheat up to the loft. Did you mix in ḥomton, the preservative? No. Then better not to have brought it up at all: the worms will have it. Wisdom without yirah is grain that rots in storage. Fear is not the first step you leave behind; it is the additive without which the whole stock spoils.

And yirah is the one thing not handed to you. "Everything is in the hands of Heaven except for fear of Heaven" (Berakhot 33b, in the name of R. Ḥanina), a line the Gemara anchors in Deuteronomy 10:12. Rambam then refuses to let yirah be the final word: in Mishneh Torah, Hilkhot Teshuvah 10:1–2, serving God out of fear of punishment or hope of reward is the level of the unlearned, of women, and of children under instruction — "not the level of the prophets and not the level of the sages." A real level, a legitimate starting place, not the destination. The one who serves from love "does what is true because it is true." Mishlei begins at 1:7; it does not end there.

Da'ehu, not "acknowledge"

The lesson takes its title from Mishlei 3:6. "In all thy ways acknowledge him" is a defensible rendering — the new JPS keeps it — but the Hebrew verb is da'ehu, from yada, to know, and the rabbinic tradition hears it that way. Know Him in all your ways.

That is what the tradition seizes on. Bar Kappara, on Berakhot 63a, asks: which is the short passage upon which all the essential principles of Torah depend? Answer: "B'khol d'rakhekha da'ehu" — Proverbs 3:6. Of all the candidates in Tanakh, this verse is nominated as the compression of the whole. Rambam builds a section of his ethics on it: Hilkhot De'ot 3:2–3 argues that eating, sleeping, working, and marital life should all be oriented toward health of body so that one can serve God — and closes by quoting our verse. The point is anti-monastic. There is no zone of life sealed off from the knowledge of God, and therefore none that is merely secular and can be lived carelessly. All your ways.

Mishlei as the mother-text of musar

Mishlei is where the Jewish ethical tradition goes to school. Pirkei Avot 4:1 has Ben Zoma's triad, and the proof-text for the second is straight out of your block: "Who is mighty? One who conquers his impulse, as it is said: 'He who is slow to anger is better than the mighty, and he who rules his spirit than he who takes a city'" (Proverbs 16:32). Ben Zoma's third question — who is rich? one who rejoices in his portion — is the same instinct, and it will matter for Kohelet.

Mishlei 22:6, "ḥanokh la-na'ar al pi darko," is the verse every Jewish educator argues about, because darko can mean "the way he should go" or "his own way." The Vilna Gaon reads it the second way: each child has an innate bent, and education means channeling it rather than breaking it — a child forced into someone else's way will comply only as long as he is afraid of you. That is not a modern reading imported into an old text; it is the Gra in the eighteenth century, and it is standard in Jewish educational discourse today.

Eshet Ḥayil: the most misread chapter in the book

Friday night, before Kiddush, in a great many Jewish homes: Proverbs 31:10–31, sung. The custom is not ancient — it comes out of the kabbalistic circle of Safed, the students of the Ari — and its original referent is not the woman of the house at all. It is the Shekhinah, the indwelling Divine Presence, the Shabbat Queen welcomed at the threshold. The domestic reading, a husband praising his wife, is a later overlay on a mystical one, and both are alive at the same table.

Behind both stands the midrash. Midrash Mishlei on chapter 31 reads eshet ḥayil as the Torah itself, and then as a series of biblical women — Sarah, Rivkah, Leah, Raḥel, Batya, Yokheved, Miriam, Ḥannah, Yael — each matched to a verse, with Ruth the Moabite surpassing them all. It also embeds the story permanently attached to this chapter: R. Meir's two sons die on Shabbat, and his wife hides it from him until the day is out, then asks him a question of law. A man left a deposit with me; now he comes to reclaim it. Must I return it? Of course, he says. Then she shows him the bodies. The chapter the Church reads as a portrait of domestic industry is, in the Jewish imagination, attached to a woman's terrible composure.

The chapter's hinge is 31:30: "ishah yir'at YHWH, hi tit'hallal" — a woman who fears the LORD, she is to be praised. Mishlei closes on the word it opened with in 1:7. The frame is deliberate.

Kohelet, and the day the sages read it

The sages very nearly did not let you have this book. Mishnah Yadayim 3:5 preserves the dispute over whether Kohelet "defiles the hands" — the technical marker of canonical sanctity, counterintuitively phrased. R. Yehudah says Song of Songs defiles the hands and Kohelet is disputed; R. Yose says the reverse; R. Shimon files Kohelet under the leniencies of Beit Shammai and the stringencies of Beit Hillel — meaning Beit Shammai's "lenient" position was that Kohelet is not scripture. R. Akiva declares that no one ever disputed Song of Songs, "for all the writings are holy, but Song of Songs is the holy of holies"; the argument, he concedes, was only ever about Kohelet. The Mishnah settles it by received tradition: both defile the hands.

Shabbat 30b is blunter. The Sages sought to suppress Kohelet because its statements contradict one another: "vexation is better than laughter" (7:3) against 2:2, which the Gemara reads as "I said of laughter, it is praiseworthy"; "so I commended mirth" (8:15) against the other half of 2:2, "and of mirth, what does it accomplish?" They did not suppress it, because "its beginning is words of Torah and its end is words of Torah." The same passage says they wanted to suppress Mishlei too, on the strength of Proverbs 26:4–5 — do not answer a fool according to his folly, answer a fool according to his folly — and resolved it instead. Both books survive on the same reasoning. That is a canon formed by argument, not acclamation.

And then the calendar does something strange with it. In the dominant Ashkenazi custom, Kohelet is read publicly on Shabbat Ḥol HaMo'ed Sukkot — the festival the liturgy calls z'man simḥatenu, the season of our rejoicing. David Golinkin, surveying seven different customs for when to read it, is honest that the practice has no Talmudic basis and was never universal among Sephardim; the likeliest driver was the wish to read all five megillot in public, and Kohelet had to go somewhere. But the homiletic reasons that grew up around it are not idle. One lives in your block: Kohelet 11:2, "give a portion to seven, and also to eight," was heard as the seven days of Sukkot plus Shemini Atzeret. The deeper one is structural. The most disenchanted book in the Bible is read in a hut with a leaky roof during the most joyful week of the year. The sukkah and Kohelet say the same thing about impermanence, and Sukkot's answer is not to deny it but to eat in it.

Hevel, and what Christian readers keep supplying

Hevel is vapor, breath, the visible puff on a cold morning. "Vanity" is Jerome's vanitas by way of the KJV, and it drags in a moral worthlessness the Hebrew does not carry. The same problem runs through the block: at 3:11 God "has put ha-olam in their heart," which the KJV makes "the world" and JPS "eternity" — the word carries both, and the sentence describes a creature built to want the whole span and equipped to see only its own stretch of it. Kohelet Rabbah 1:2 counts the seven hevels of the opening verse and maps them twice — onto the seven ages of a human life (the one-year-old like a king, the ten-year-old skipping like a goat) and onto the seven days of creation. Not "worthless." Transient, and structurally so. The Aramaic Targum, a running aggadic commentary rather than a translation, historicizes it: Solomon foresaw by the spirit of prophecy the division of the kingdom under Rehoboam, the destruction of Jerusalem and the Temple, and the exile — "and he said by the divine word: vanity of vanities is this world."

The hardest passage is 3:19–21, where humans and beasts have one breath and one end, and "who knows the spirit of man that goes upward and the spirit of the beast that goes downward." Rashi will not read that as a question about whether there is a beyond; he reads mi yodea as a summons — those who understand know that the human spirit ascends to stand in judgment while the beast's does not, and therefore a person must not behave like a beast. Ibn Ezra takes it as an epistemological claim: not one in a thousand can actually grasp the difference between the animal nefesh and the human neshamah. Neither is doing what a modern reader does — neither is offering the afterlife as a solution to Kohelet's problem. Rashi turns the verse into an ethical demand; Ibn Ezra turns it into a limit on knowledge.

That absence is what Christian readers most consistently supply from outside. Kohelet's answer to mortality is not a compensating world to come. It is ḥelek — portion — and simḥah, and both sit firmly under the sun. "There is nothing better for a man than that he should eat and drink and make his soul enjoy good in his labor... it is the gift of God" (2:24; cf. 3:12–13). In Jewish law this is not a consolation prize; it is a command. Rambam codifies festival joy as an obligation with a social edge: one must make his household rejoice, and when he eats and drinks he must feed the convert, the orphan, the widow — and one who locks his courtyard and feasts only with his own family has performed no mitzvah but merely "the rejoicing of his gut" (Hilkhot Yom Tov 6:17–18). Which is Kohelet 11:1–2 in halakhic dress. The Yerushalmi pushes further — R. Ḥizkiah in the name of Rav: a person will one day give an accounting for everything his eye saw and which, permitted to him, he did not enjoy (Yerushalmi Kiddushin 4:12). Refusing pleasure is not piety. It is a debt.

The last verse, twice

KJV's "fear God and keep his commandments: for this is the whole duty of man" imports a word. The Hebrew of 12:13 is ki zeh kol ha-adam — "for this is the whole of the human being," or, as the new JPS has it, "for this applies to all humankind." No "duty." The verse is either a definition of what a person is or a statement of universal scope, and the tradition has never fully chosen.

One last practice tells you how the tradition felt about ending here. The Masoretic instruction for four books — Isaiah, Malachi, Eikhah, and Kohelet — is that the penultimate verse be repeated after the last, so the reading does not close on a hard word. Kohelet 12:14 warns that God will bring every deed into judgment, including every hidden thing. And then the reader goes back and says 12:13 again: fear God and keep His commandments. The book is allowed its judgment. It is simply not permitted the final word.
