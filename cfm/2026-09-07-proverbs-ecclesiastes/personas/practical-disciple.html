A Just Weight, a Soft Answer, and the Next Right Thing

Written by an AI persona on living the week's text — not a real person.

Thirteen chapters this week, and almost none of them ask you to believe anything. They ask you to do things: answer softly, pay on time, keep an honest scale, stop cosigning, stop waiting for perfect weather, eat your dinner. The risk with material this quotable is that it slides past as decoration. Take it the other way — as assignments with due dates.

Before you hit send

Proverbs 15 is a chapter about your mouth, and the manual asks directly whether its counsel applies to "today's digital communication" (Come, Follow Me, September 7–13). It does, and one verse carries most of the weight: "The heart of the righteous studieth to answer: but the mouth of the wicked poureth out evil things" (15:28).

Notice the verbs. Studieth and poureth out. One is deliberate, effortful, slow; the other is what a container does when you tip it. A reply box is built to make you pour. The whole verse lives in the gap between reading something and responding to it, and the practical form of righteousness here is unglamorous: you write the reply, you do not send it, you come back in an hour. Most of what you would have sent will not survive the hour. That is the verse working.

Proverbs 15:1 is quoted more than it is obeyed, mostly because people misremember whose job it describes. "A soft answer turneth away wrath: but grievous words stir up anger." The wrath is already there — it belongs to the other person, and it is not being called unjustified. The softness is yours, supplied to someone who has not earned it, which is why it costs something. What it costs is the pleasure of being right out loud. Elder Neil L. Andersen's counsel on peacemaking runs the same direction: conviction without anger or malice ("Following Jesus: Being a Peacemaker," April 2022 general conference). You are allowed to be right. You are not allowed to be right the way you want to be.

Two more are specific enough to act on. Proverbs 15:23: "a word spoken in due season, how good is it!" — a true and kind word at the wrong moment is still a mistake, and most of us have a standing habit of raising the hard subject in the car on the way somewhere, or at 10:40 at night. And Proverbs 16:28: "a whisperer separateth chief friends." Note what the whisperer destroys — not a reputation, a friendship. The mild, accurate observation you made about someone in your ward to a mutual friend did not damage him; it damaged what those two people have. Elder Ronald A. Rasband's "Words Matter" (April 2024 general conference) presses the same point about ordinary speech. Proverbs 15:2 sets the boundary: "the tongue of the wise useth knowledge aright." Knowing something true about a person does not authorize saying it.

The weights in the bag

The money material here is not about generosity as a warm feeling. It is about timing and accuracy. Proverbs 3:9 sets the order — "the firstfruits of all thine increase," not the remainder — and 3:27 sets the deadline.

"Withhold not good from them to whom it is due, when it is in the power of thine hand to do it. Say not unto thy neighbour, Go, and come again, and to morrow I will give; when thou hast it by thee" (3:27–28). Read them as one instruction, because the second closes the loophole in the first. The sin named is not refusing — it is delaying while solvent. The money is in the account. You are going to pay. You just haven't, because the friction is small on your end and enormous on the end of the person waiting. The contractor's invoice. The friend who fronted the tickets. The teenager who mowed the lawn Saturday and has been paid in "remind me." Proverbs 3:28 is about the spiritual condition of meaning to.

Then Proverbs 16:11: "A just weight and balance are the LORD's: all the weights of the bag are his work." An ancient merchant carried his weights in a pouch, and the crooked ones carried two sets — a heavy stone for buying, a light one for selling. The claim is startling: the calibration itself belongs to God. Your bag of weights is the timesheet, the expense report, the mileage log, the description of the car you are selling, the line on the résumé that is technically defensible. The dishonesty available to most of us is never theft — it is a second set of weights, one for measuring ourselves and one for everyone else.

Proverbs 22:7 — "the borrower is servant to the lender" — does not condemn borrowing; it describes what borrowing does to your freedom. Then the bluntest counsel in the block: "Be not thou one of them that strike hands, or of them that are sureties for debts. If thou hast nothing to pay, why should he take away thy bed from under thee?" (22:26–27). Cosigning. The verse assumes you will do it out of love, and reminds you that love does not make you liquid.

And then the hard one: "Rob not the poor, because he is poor: neither oppress the afflicted in the gate" (22:22). Sit with that because. It is not a reason against robbing him; it is the reason he gets robbed — poverty is what makes a person robbable, because he cannot afford the lawyer, cannot leave the job, cannot dispute the fee. Proverbs 22:16 puts "he that oppresseth the poor to increase his riches" and "he that giveth to the rich" in one sentence, headed for one outcome. If you supervise anyone, set any price, or approve any policy, this verse has your name on it. King Lemuel's mother gives the active form: "Open thy mouth, judge righteously, and plead the cause of the poor and needy" (31:9).

Against all of this stand the "better" sayings — 15:16, 16:8, 22:1, and best of them, "Better is a dinner of herbs where love is, than a stalled ox and hatred therewith" (15:17). That is, among other things, a verse about what a family dinner is for. The menu is not the variable.

Two hard verses about children

Proverbs 22:6 has done real damage in the hands of people who read it as a guarantee, and it is worth knowing that the Hebrew does not say what the KJV says it says. The phrase rendered "in the way he should go" is ʿal-pî darkô — literally "according to his way." There is no word for should in the Hebrew; translators supplied it (see the word-by-word analysis at Biblehub). The line is a proverb, a statement of how formation generally works, and the genre trades in generalizations, not warranties.

This matters enormously for the parent whose adult child has walked away. Read as a promise, 22:6 becomes an accusation with your name in it: he departed, therefore you trained him wrong. But this same week's reading contains Ecclesiastes 2:19 — "and who knoweth whether he shall be a wise man or a fool?" — a man in agony precisely because he cannot control who comes after him. If you are going to hold 22:6 over yourself, you have to let 2:19 stand next to it.

Proverbs 22:15 — "Foolishness is bound in the heart of a child; but the rod of correction shall drive it far from him" — needs its first clause read as carefully as its second. The claim is that children are foolish, not wicked; what needs driving out is immaturity, not the child. The Church's own study helps for this week call the rod language figurative, stress loving and consistent discipline, and say plainly that the verse must not be used to justify physical abuse (Scripture Helps for this block). But the harder self-examination is narrower than the doctrinal question: when you corrected your child this week, were you removing foolishness, or discharging your own day? Proverbs 15:18 does not exempt parents: "A wrathful man stirreth up strife."

Notice how the teacher in Proverbs 4 introduces himself: "I was my father's son, tender and only beloved ... He taught me also" (4:3–4). Beloved first, taught second, in that order.

The city nobody sees you take

"He that is slow to anger is better than the mighty; and he that ruleth his spirit than he that taketh a city" (16:32). The comparison is military on purpose. Taking a city is the most public achievement that world offers — gates, spoils, songs. Ruling your spirit has no audience; nobody will congratulate you for the sentence you didn't say. That asymmetry is exactly why we don't do it, which is why the verse exists.

Proverbs 16:18 — "Pride goeth before destruction" — is famous, but 16:2 has the mechanism: "All the ways of a man are clean in his own eyes; but the LORD weigheth the spirits." You cannot audit your own pride, because pride is the instrument you'd audit it with. Which is why 15:22 is not filler: "Without counsel purposes are disappointed." Before the job change, the move, the big purchase — name three people you will actually let talk you out of it, not three people you will inform.

And 22:24–25 governs your inputs: "Make no friendship with an angry man ... lest thou learn his ways, and get a snare to thy soul." Anger is transmissible, and the accounts you follow are furious friends you have chosen and can unchoose.

"A man's heart deviseth his way: but the LORD directeth his steps" (16:9) is not an argument against planning — the devising is assumed. It is an argument against gripping. Make the plan; hold the steps loosely; "commit thy works unto the LORD" (16:3).

Ecclesiastes: work you will hand to someone else

Then the floor gives way. "Yea, I hated all my labour which I had taken under the sun: because I should leave it unto the man that shall be after me. And who knoweth whether he shall be a wise man or a fool?" (2:18–19). This is not abstract. It is the calling you will be released from and someone else will run differently, the business you sell, the house you remodeled that the next owner will gut. Qohelet's vertigo is the dread of having built something whose future you do not control.

His answer arrives three times (2:24; 3:12–13; 3:22), which in this book means he means it: "every man should eat and drink, and enjoy the good of all his labour, it is the gift of God." That is not a consolation prize; it is phrased as a duty, and refusing to enjoy your work is refusing a gift. If you cannot take a day off, cannot sit at the meal you cooked because you are already clearing the counter, cannot finish a project without itemizing its flaws — Ecclesiastes has an assignment for you and it is stop it.

Ecclesiastes 3:1–8 gets read at funerals as comfort. Read as a schedule it is severe: "a time to keep silence, and a time to speak" means both are wrong at the wrong time, and the whole skill of wisdom is knowing which season you are in. What are you still doing because it was right two years ago?

Sowing without a forecast

Chapter 11 is the practical center of the book. "Cast thy bread upon the waters ... Give a portion to seven, and also to eight; for thou knowest not what evil shall be upon the earth" (11:1–2). Then the diagnosis of paralysis: "He that observeth the wind shall not sow; and he that regardeth the clouds shall not reap" (11:4). If you wait for conditions, you will not plant. Then the instruction: "In the morning sow thy seed, and in the evening withhold not thine hand: for thou knowest not whether shall prosper, either this or that" (11:6).

That is the whole discipline. Ecclesiastes refuses to promise outcomes and then tells you to act anyway — twice a day, without a forecast. Send the text to the person who may not want it. Make the ministering visit that has never once gone well. Keep showing up for the child who is not returning your calls. The not-knowing is given as the reason to do both, not as a reason to do neither. For anyone in a hard season, this is the most usable thing in thirteen chapters.

Then: "Remember now thy Creator in the days of thy youth, while the evil days come not" (12:1), followed by an unflinching inventory of a body shutting down — trembling hands, failing teeth, dimming windows, the grasshopper that has become a burden (12:3–5). The load-bearing word is now. The passage is not saying old age is too late; it is saying the person you have been planning to become is someone you become by Thursday or not at all.

Questions worth being uncomfortable about

  • What did I send this week that would not have survived one hour in a drafts folder (15:28)?
  • Who is currently waiting on money I already have (3:28)?
  • Where do I keep two sets of weights — one standard for my own conduct, one for everyone else's (16:11, 16:2)?
  • Is there anyone I have leverage over whose situation I am quietly pricing into what I pay them or how I treat them (22:16, 22:22)?
  • The last time I corrected my child, was it aimed at foolishness or was it my bad day arriving on schedule (22:15, 15:18)?
  • Name the three people who could actually talk me out of my current plan. Have I told any of them what it is (15:22)?
  • When did I last enjoy my work without immediately itemizing what was wrong with it — and do I understand that as disobedience (3:13)?
  • What am I refusing to start because the conditions aren't right (11:4)?
  • Whose anger have I been marinating in by choice (22:24–25)?

"Let us hear the conclusion of the whole matter: Fear God, and keep his commandments: for this is the whole duty of man" (12:13). After thirteen chapters about invoices, tempers, and the weather, that verse is not an abstraction. It is a summary of the receipts.
