Wisdom Is a Tree, and the Spirit Goes Home

Written by an AI persona connecting the block to Restoration scripture and modern teaching — not a real person.

Two books, two temperaments, one week. Proverbs teaches that wisdom can be gotten and that getting it pays. Ecclesiastes reports that the man who got more of it than anyone in Jerusalem still concluded, "in much wisdom is much grief" (Ecclesiastes 1:18). The Restoration does not resolve that tension by picking a side. It does something more interesting: it gives the spirit that "returns unto God who gave it" a before and an after that Qohelet could not see — and then, through modern prophets, borrows Proverbs' most famous couplet as a discipline for everything that is still unknown.

The verse the week is named for

"Trust in the Lord with all thine heart; and lean not unto thine own understanding. In all thy ways acknowledge him, and he shall direct thy paths" (Proverbs 3:5–6).

The most instructive modern use of this passage is not a sermon on obedience. In October 2019, President Dallin H. Oaks devoted a general conference address — titled, simply, "Trust in the Lord" — to the spirit world, and specifically to how much of it we do not know. He acknowledged that "our canon of scripture contains very little about the spirit world that follows death and precedes the Final Judgment," warned against answering such questions "on the basis of something you heard from another person sometime in the past," and then offered "two answers": that God loves His children and will do what is best for each of us, and Proverbs 3:5–6, which he called "most helpful to me on a multitude of unanswered questions."

Proverbs 3:5 is usually preached as courage under trial. Oaks preaches it as epistemic restraint — the antidote to confident speculation, including confident Latter-day Saint speculation. Read that way, the verse stops being a rebuke of doubt and becomes a rebuke of overconfidence, which is precisely how Proverbs itself uses it: verse 7 continues, "Be not wise in thine own eyes."

Sister Bonnie H. Cordon pressed on the physics of the verb in the general women's session of April 2017: "In English the word lean has a connotation of physically listing or moving to one side. … When we spiritually lean to our own understanding, we lean away from our Savior. If we lean, we are not centered; we are not balanced; we are not focused on Christ." Elder Stanley G. Ellis built "Do We Trust Him? Hard Is Good" (October 2017) on verses 5–7, reaching them by way of President Thomas S. Monson's use of the passage. And the Church made Proverbs 3:5–6 the 2022 youth theme. Few Old Testament verses carry that much modern freight.

Watch, too, how Proverbs itself glosses the promise. "In all thy ways acknowledge him, and he shall direct thy paths" (3:6) is restated thirteen chapters later without the conditional softening: "A man's heart deviseth his way: but the Lord directeth his steps" (16:9). The direction is not a reward for trusting; it is the standing situation. Trust is how you stop fighting it.

Learned, if

Proverbs' warning about self-assessment is relentless and specific. "All the ways of a man are clean in his own eyes; but the Lord weigheth the spirits" (16:2). "There is a way that seemeth right unto a man, but the end thereof are the ways of death" (16:25). The problem is not ignorance; it is that the internal gauge reads clean.

Jacob makes the identical move in almost the same grammar: "When they are learned they think they are wise, and they hearken not unto the counsel of God, for they set it aside, supposing they know of themselves. … But to be learned is good if they hearken unto the counsels of God" (2 Nephi 9:28–29). Elder Boyd K. Packer took that phrase as the title of a general conference address in October 1992, "'To Be Learned Is Good If …'," and paired it with the Lord's own command to "seek ye out of the best books words of wisdom; seek learning, even by study and also by faith" (Doctrine and Covenants 88:118). Note that neither Jacob nor Proverbs is anti-intellectual. Proverbs 4:7 says get it: "Wisdom is the principal thing; therefore get wisdom: and with all thy getting get understanding." The conditional is about hearkening, not about how much you know.

Ecclesiastes 1:9 — "there is no new thing under the sun" — sounds like the opposite of a Restoration claim, until you notice what the Restoration actually claims. Not novelty: restoration, a "welding together of dispensations, and keys, and powers, and glories … from the days of Adam even to the present time" (Doctrine and Covenants 128:18). Qohelet is nearly right. What he misses appears two verses later: "There is no remembrance of former things" (1:11). Amnesia is exactly the condition the Restoration addresses — records, genealogies, and the sealing that makes fathers and children mutually necessary, "we without them cannot be made perfect; neither can they without us be made perfect" (128:18).

The tree

Proverbs 3:13–18 is the passage a Latter-day Saint reader should slow down over. Wisdom is a woman; she is "more precious than rubies"; "length of days is in her right hand; and in her left hand riches and honour"; "her ways are ways of pleasantness, and all her paths are peace." Then: "She is a tree of life to them that lay hold upon her: and happy is every one that retaineth her" (3:18).

The verbs are Lehi's verbs. In 1 Nephi 8, the multitudes "caught hold of the end of the rod of iron," "did press forward through the mist of darkness, clinging to the rod of iron," and came forth to "partake of the fruit of the tree" (8:24); those who are lost afterward are lost not because the tree moved but because, having tasted, they were ashamed and let go (8:28). Proverbs compresses the whole sequence into two verbs: lay hold, and retain. Nephi's interpreting angel identifies the tree as "a representation of the love of God" (1 Nephi 11:25), and Alma extends the image forward in time — nourish the word and "it shall be a tree springing up unto everlasting life," and "ye shall feast upon this fruit even until ye are filled, that ye hunger not, neither shall ye thirst" (Alma 32:41–42). Proverbs 3:18 is not a stretched proof-text; it is the same Eden image doing the same work — grasp it, hold it, live.

The context reinforces the temple reading rather than diluting it. The very next verse turns to creation: "The Lord by wisdom hath founded the earth; by understanding hath he established the heavens" (3:19). Tree, garden, creation, and a guarded way back — the sequence a Latter-day Saint meets in the temple before meeting it in Proverbs. And 15:4 puts the tree in an unexpected place: "A wholesome tongue is a tree of life." Life-giving speech is itself the tree, which is the whole burden of Proverbs 15–16.

One more path text belongs beside modern revelation: "the path of the just is as the shining light, that shineth more and more unto the perfect day" (Proverbs 4:18), against "that which is of God is light; and he that receiveth light, and continueth in God, receiveth more light; and that light groweth brighter and brighter until the perfect day" (Doctrine and Covenants 50:24). The revelation answers the question Proverbs leaves open — why does the path brighten?

Fear that ends fear

"The fear of the Lord is the beginning of knowledge" (Proverbs 1:7). The phrase recurs across the block — 2:5; 3:7; 15:33; 16:6; 22:4; 31:30 — and Ecclesiastes ends on it: "Fear God, and keep his commandments: for this is the whole duty of man" (12:13).

Elder David A. Bednar addressed the term directly in "Therefore They Hushed Their Fears" (April 2015), the talk the Come, Follow Me manual itself points to. Godly fear, he taught, includes "a deep feeling of reverence, respect, and awe for the Lord Jesus Christ, obedience to His commandments, and anticipation of the Final Judgment and justice at His hand," and — the line that matters here — "Unlike worldly fear that creates alarm and anxiety, godly fear is a source of peace, assurance, and confidence."

Proverbs makes that argument on its own terms. Chapter 1 ends with the promise that whoever hearkens to wisdom "shall dwell safely, and shall be quiet from fear of evil" (1:33); chapter 3 continues, "When thou liest down, thou shalt not be afraid: yea, thou shalt lie down, and thy sleep shall be sweet. Be not afraid of sudden fear" (3:24–25). In Proverbs, the fear of the Lord is the thing that subtracts fear. Bednar's distinction is not imposed on the text; it is read out of it. Note also the company the phrase keeps: "The fear of the Lord is the instruction of wisdom; and before honour is humility" (15:33); "By humility and the fear of the Lord are riches, and honour, and life" (22:4). It is paired with humility, not with dread.

"The spirit shall return unto God who gave it"

Ecclesiastes 12:7 is the closest the Old Testament comes to stating the outer frame of the plan of salvation in one sentence: "Then shall the dust return to the earth as it was: and the spirit shall return unto God who gave it."

Alma 40:11 reads like a commentary on it: "the spirits of all men, as soon as they are departed from this mortal body, yea, the spirits of all men, whether they be good or evil, are taken home to that God who gave them life" — followed by what Qohelet could not supply, the division into paradise and outer darkness "until the time of their resurrection" (40:12–14). Abraham 3:22–23 supplies the other end, "the intelligences that were organized before the world was," and Doctrine and Covenants 138 supplies the middle: Joseph F. Smith, pondering on 3 October 1918, "the eyes of my understanding were opened … and I saw the hosts of the dead, both small and great" (138:11).

Set that against Qohelet's own question — "Who knoweth the spirit of man that goeth upward, and the spirit of the beast that goeth downward to the earth?" (3:21) — and his admission, "thou knowest not what is the way of the spirit" (11:5). The Restoration answers the who and the where. It does not answer much else, which is why Oaks reached for Proverbs 3:5–6 on exactly this subject. Ecclesiastes is not refuted by the Restoration; it is bounded by it.

Ecclesiastes 3:11 sits in the same territory: "He hath made every thing beautiful in his time: also he hath set the world in their heart." The Hebrew behind KJV "world" is olam, which most modern translations render "eternity" — a sense of duration planted in a creature of whom the same verse says "no man can find out the work that God maketh from the beginning to the end." Elder Russell M. Nelson quoted the opening of that chapter — "To every thing there is a season … A time to be born, and a time to die" — in "Doors of Death" (April 1992), a talk that sets physical death inside a plan beginning before birth: "Prior to our birth, we dwelled as spirit children with our Father in Heaven."

Proverbs 31, read as written

The chapter is not anonymous advice about wives. Verse 1 attributes it to "king Lemuel, the prophecy that his mother taught him" — a woman's teaching, preserved as scripture, and the first nine verses of it are about sobriety in leadership and pleading "the cause of the poor and needy" (31:9).

The acrostic that follows (verses 10–31) is startlingly economic and public. She "considereth a field, and buyeth it" (16); she manufactures and sells, "maketh fine linen, and selleth it; and delivereth girdles unto the merchant" (24); she "stretcheth out her hand to the poor" (20). And the chapter ends where her husband sits: "let her own works praise her in the gates" (31; compare 23). The 2026 Come, Follow Me outline calls her "a woman of great spiritual strength, capability, and influence," which is the plain sense of the poem.

The literary shape is worth naming. Proverbs opens with Wisdom as a woman who "crieth without … in the openings of the gates" (1:20–21) and closes with a woman who "openeth her mouth with wisdom" and is praised in the gates (31:26, 31). And the last thing said of her is the first thing said in the book: "a woman that feareth the Lord, she shall be praised" (31:30), answering 1:7. Note what the poem declines to praise — "Favour is deceitful, and beauty is vain" — and what it never mentions: her silence.

The Joseph Smith Translation: an honest null

There is almost nothing here. The Joseph Smith Translation manuscript (Old Testament Revision 2, page 97) records only terse scribal notations for Proverbs — reference points for Proverbs 16:29, 18:22, and 22:12 — from the stage of the project when Joseph Smith marked his own Bible and his scribe noted the locations rather than copying out full text. Only two of those three fall inside this week's chapters, and neither carries doctrinal weight. The same manuscript page bears the note "The Songs of Solomon are not Inspired writings." Ecclesiastes is untouched.

A reader hoping the JST will settle the Proverbs–Ecclesiastes tension will not find help there. That is worth saying plainly rather than manufacturing a connection. The tension stands, and the Restoration's contribution to it comes from elsewhere: from Alma 40, from Doctrine and Covenants 138, and from a sitting member of the First Presidency saying, about the spirit world, that he does not know — and quoting Proverbs 3:5–6 anyway.
