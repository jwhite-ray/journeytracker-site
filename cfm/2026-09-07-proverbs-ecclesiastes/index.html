The Argument the Canon Refused to Settle

≈30 min · listen or read · the week's introduction — not one of the five sessions

The manual sets Proverbs and Ecclesiastes side by side and admits the tension itself: Proverbs says seek wisdom and life will go well, and Ecclesiastes “seems to say, ‘It’s not that simple.’” Our hosts bring in the panel’s rabbi — because this week is Mishlei and Kohelet, sung at the Shabbat table and read in the sukkah, and because the Talmud records the sages nearly suppressing both of the books in this lesson. Context, then content, then the promises the text makes and does not always keep.

Two books sit next to each other in the canon and do not agree, and this week you read both. Proverbs teaches that a life shaped by discipline, restraint, and the fear of the Lord runs on rails toward long days and full barns; it makes specific promises — length of days, filled barns, sweet sleep, a child who will not depart. Ecclesiastes, whose narrator says he ran exactly that program at royal scale, reports that the returns are unpredictable, the accounting is closed to us, and everyone dies anyway. The Come, Follow Me manual notices this and says the two books "look at life from different perspectives" but "teach similar truths." Another way to say it is that the canon preserved an argument and declined to settle it.

That is not a defect to be managed. It is the week's whole opportunity. Proverbs is not one book but a library: its own superscriptions break it into collections credited to Solomon, to anonymous "wise men," to a foreign king named Lemuel, and to scribes working under Hezekiah two and a half centuries after Solomon died. Ecclesiastes is a late Hebrew composition in which a narrator wearing a Solomonic mask audits the wisdom tradition from inside it and then hands the last word to a second voice who tells you what to make of him. Reading them in one week means holding a confident tradition and its own most searching critic in the same hand.

The five sessions below move through the block in order. Sessions 1 and 2 build Proverbs' world — wisdom crying in the street, the two paths, the tree you can put both hands on. Session 3 works the sentence collections, where the theology gets small and practical: your mouth, your temper, the weights in your bag. Session 4 is the week's hinge and its hardest sitting: Proverbs 22's promises about children and money, and then Ecclesiastes 1–2, which is the Bible's own rebuttal. Session 5 gathers the work of your hands and the time you are given — the capable woman whose works praise her in the gates, the poem of seasons, sowing without a forecast, and the body that gives out while the spirit goes home.

A note on how to read the questions at the end of each session. They are not review. Several of them have no settled answer, and a few of them are questions the text itself refuses to close. Sit in them for a minute before you answer.

25–30 minSession 1: Wisdom Crying in the Street

Reading assignment: Proverbs 1, 2

The book tells you what it is

Proverbs 1:1 — "The proverbs of Solomon the son of David, king of Israel" — reads like a title page, but it is the heading of the first of several collections, and the book supplies the rest of the headings itself: a second Solomonic heading at 10:1, "the words of the wise" at 22:17 in this week's block, another at 24:23, Agur at 30:1, Lemuel at 31:1, and most decisively 25:1, which credits further Solomonic material to "the men of Hezekiah king of Judah" who "copied out" — an internal claim that the book was still being assembled centuries after Solomon. This is not a concession wrung from the text; it is the text's own account of itself, and the Church's own study helps for this week describe Proverbs as a library of Israelite wisdom collected over several centuries. The critical consensus says the same thing: an anthology assembled over centuries, individual sayings that may well be old, a final shape that is late.

The book also holds two different literary forms, and this week you read both. Chapters 1–9 are instruction — long parental speeches with vocatives, motive clauses, extended metaphor, and a sustained argument about two paths. That is Proverbs 1–4, your reading for the first two sessions. Proverbs 15, 16, and 22 are something else entirely, and session 3 will take them on their own terms.

She cries in the street, and her idiom is prophetic

At 1:20 something happens that the surrounding material does not prepare you for. Wisdom stops being an object and becomes a speaker. She "crieth without," takes her stand "in the openings of the gates," and delivers a first-person oracle in a voice that is not merely wise but divine: "I will pour out my spirit unto you, I will make known my words unto you" (1:23), followed by the classic prophetic rejection formula — "Because I have called, and ye refused" (1:24).

Who is she? The explanations divide. Most scholars read her as literary personification, an abstraction given a voice. Some read her against Egyptian maat, the personified cosmic order that is also a goddess. A minority reads her as a real goddess figure demoted and redeployed, pointing to eighth-century inscriptions from Kuntillet 'Ajrud that bless by "YHWH and his asherah." And some take the prophetic register of 1:20–33 as the key: her speech has the shape of an indictment, in a voice Israel already had. Latter-day Saint scholarship has engaged this seriously — Daniel Peterson's work on Nephi's tree and Samuel Zinner's on Lady Wisdom traditions — and the engagement is live because Latter-day Saints have a doctrinal Heavenly Mother. Two cautions are earned, though, and the second is the sharp one: Proverbs itself does not present Wisdom as anyone's consort. She is God's instrument and, in chapter 8, his delight. Reading a Restoration doctrine backward into Proverbs is a category error even when the Restoration doctrine is true.

Fed by your own way

Proverbs' confidence rests on a structure worth naming, because once you see it you cannot unsee it in these chapters. Klaus Koch argued that the Hebrew Bible does not generally think in terms of a judge assigning penalties but in terms of a deed that carries its own outcome inside it — later scholarship calls it the deed-consequence nexus. Proverbs 1 states it without hedging. The ambushers "lay wait for their own blood" (1:18). The fools "shall eat of the fruit of their own way, and be filled with their own devices" (1:31). Not punished for their way — fed by it. Hold onto this: in session 4, Qohelet will run the same instrument over the same data and get a different reading.

The fear that keeps wisdom from rotting

"The fear of the Lord is the beginning of knowledge" (1:7). The Hebrew word behind "beginning" is reshit, and it is not merely "beginning" — it is the word that opens the Torah, and it carries the sense of first-fruits, the choice portion, the principle of the thing. The Talmud's image for how this works is sharper than any paraphrase: a man is told to carry wheat up to the loft and neglects the preservative, so the whole stock is eaten by worms. Wisdom without yirah is grain that rots in storage. Fear is not a first step you leave behind; it is the additive without which everything else spoils.

And yet the tradition refuses to let fear be the destination. Maimonides ranks service from fear of punishment as a real level and a legitimate starting place, not the summit — the one who serves from love "does what is true because it is true." Mishlei begins at 1:7; it does not end there.

Two women in the same street

Chapter 2 introduces Wisdom's structural opposite: the "strange woman" of 2:16, who "forsaketh the guide of her youth, and forgetteth the covenant of her God," whose "house inclineth unto death." What social reality she names is disputed — another man's wife, apostasy, the intermarriage controversies of the Persian period. But the literary function is not in doubt. Wisdom calls in the street, the strange woman calls in the street, and the young man has to tell them apart.

Notice what that arrangement assumes about you. The instruction chapters address a son, over and over — 1:8, 1:10, 2:1, 3:1, 3:11, 3:21, 4:10, 4:20 — and the women in them are figures the son must sort. Both poles are female; the reader is assumed male. You can find that historically intelligible and still notice it, and you will need to notice it again in session 5.

Think deeply. Work these before you move on; several of them will come back later in the week.

  • Proverbs 1:31 says the fool will "eat of the fruit of his own way." Is that a description of how consequences actually work in your experience, or a claim about how God arranges them — and does the difference change how you pray about outcomes?
  • Wisdom's speech in 1:24–28 ends with her laughing at calamity and refusing to answer those who call. What do you do with a canonical passage in which the personified voice of divine wisdom is merciless? Is that hyperbole, pedagogy, or a description of consequence you would rather not accept?
  • If Proverbs is an anthology assembled over centuries by editors who credited material to Solomon, Agur, Lemuel, and Hezekiah's scribes, what work is the word "inspired" doing? Can it describe an editor's judgment about what to keep, and not only a prophet's mouth?
  • The chapters address "my son" and set two women in his path. What is lost, and what remains, when a daughter reads Proverbs 2 as written to her? Is the answer "nothing changes," and are you sure?
  • Rava's parable makes the fear of the Lord a preservative rather than a first step. Name something you learned early in your discipleship that has since rotted for lack of it. What was the missing additive, concretely?

25–30 minSession 2: In All Thy Ways, Know Him

Reading assignment: Proverbs 3, 4

The verse the week is named for

"Trust in the Lord with all thine heart; and lean not unto thine own understanding. In all thy ways acknowledge him, and he shall direct thy paths" (3:5–6). Start with the verb. The Hebrew in 3:6 is da'ehu, from yada, to know — "know Him in all your ways." The rabbinic tradition hears it that way and builds on it. Bar Kappara asks which short passage carries all the essential principles of Torah, and answers with this verse: of all the candidates in Tanakh, this one is nominated as the compression of the whole. Maimonides then runs an entire section of his ethics off it — eating, sleeping, working, marital life, all oriented toward being able to serve God. The point is anti-monastic: there is no zone of life sealed off from the knowledge of God, and therefore none that is merely secular and can be lived carelessly.

The most instructive modern Latter-day Saint use of the passage runs in a direction most readers do not expect. In October 2019 President Dallin H. Oaks devoted a general conference address to the spirit world, and specifically to how much of it we do not know — warning against answering such questions from "something you heard from another person sometime in the past" — and then offered Proverbs 3:5–6 as what has been "most helpful to me on a multitude of unanswered questions." Oaks preaches it as epistemic restraint — the antidote to confident speculation, including confident Latter-day Saint speculation. Which is exactly how Proverbs itself uses it, since verse 7 continues, "Be not wise in thine own eyes."

Then watch Proverbs gloss its own promise. "He shall direct thy paths" (3:6) gets restated thirteen chapters on, without the conditional: "A man's heart deviseth his way: but the Lord directeth his steps" (16:9). The direction is not a reward for trusting; it is the standing situation. Trust is how you stop fighting it.

Count the guarantees before you go further

Read chapter 3 straight through with a pen. "Length of days, and long life, and peace, shall they add to thee" (3:2). Honour the Lord with your substance "so shall thy barns be filled with plenty" (3:10). "When thou liest down, thou shalt not be afraid" (3:24). This is prosperity theology with a Hebrew accent. The standard faithful defense — a proverb is a genre, a compressed generalization about how the world usually runs, not a contract — is almost certainly right as literary description, and it is also expensive, because it leaves you deciding verse by verse which promises are load-bearing and which are proverbial exaggeration, on criteria the book never supplies. Do not resolve this yet. Session 4 gives you Ecclesiastes' version of the same complaint.

Two verses in this chapter, though, are not promises at all but deadlines. "Withhold not good from them to whom it is due, when it is in the power of thine hand to do it. Say not unto thy neighbour, Go, and come again, and to morrow I will give; when thou hast it by thee" (3:27–28). Read them as one instruction, because the second closes the loophole in the first. The sin named is not refusing — it is delaying while solvent. The contractor's invoice. The friend who fronted the tickets. The teenager who mowed the lawn Saturday and has been paid in "remind me."

One verse, six ways: Proverbs 3:18

Donald W. Parry's "six outlooks" framework — historical near view, prophecy, dual or multiple fulfillment, likening, personal reading, and reading for doctrine — was built for Isaiah. Porting it to wisdom literature is instructive precisely because one of the six will not survive the trip. Take 3:18: "She is a tree of life to them that lay hold upon her: and happy is every one that retaineth her."

The near view. "She" reaches back to verse 13, "Happy is the man that findeth wisdom," and verses 13–18 are a closed unit bracketed by the word happy at both ends. The verbs in 18 are grips: chazaq, to fasten upon, seize, hold fast, and tamak, to grasp and sustain. Neither is a word for admiring something. The verse describes a hand closed around a trunk. And the payoff in context is bodily — health to the navel, marrow to the bones, barns, sweet sleep, a foot that does not stumble. It does not mean resurrection. Nothing in the near view requires it to.

Prophecy. This outlook returns nothing, and saying so is more useful than manufacturing a result. Proverbs 3:18 predicts nothing, names no future event, uses no oracle formula. That is a fact about the whole block: across these thirteen chapters there is essentially no predictive prophecy at all. A framework built for Isaiah has one tooth that finds nothing here to bite.

Multiple realization. Because there is no prediction, "fulfillment" is the wrong word, but the structure transfers. In Genesis the tree of life is fenced by cherubim and a flaming sword. In Proverbs 3:18 the same phrase returns and the fence is gone — a student in Jerusalem can put both hands on her. In Revelation 22 the tree stands in the city street with access conditioned again on conduct. In Lehi's dream it appears a fourth time, and the angel identifies it as the love of God. Four settings, four referents: immortality, wisdom, restored access, the love of God. One restraint is required here, and it is worth saying in a class: the Book of Mormon never says Lehi's family read Proverbs. Nephi's inventory of the plates of brass names no wisdom book. What we have is shared imagery moving through a canon, not a demonstrable line of dependence.

Likening. Put yourself in the phrase "them that lay hold," and the grip verbs do the work. The block reinforces it three times over in chapter 4 — "Take fast hold of instruction; let her not go" (4:13), "Forsake her not, and she shall preserve thee" (4:6), "Exalt her, and she shall promote thee" (4:8). Every one is a grip verb followed by a consequence, which means the book is telling you that wisdom can be dropped. Lehi's crowds use the same verbs: they caught hold of the rod, clung to it, and those who were lost afterward were lost because, having tasted, they let go.

The personal reading. Parry keeps this separate on purpose, and the separation is the point. A personal reading cannot be checked and is not exegesis. What a verse gives you on a hard Tuesday and what a verse means are different questions, and a class goes badly when the first is offered as the second.

Doctrine. The doctrinal claim is not in verse 18 but immediately after it: "The Lord by wisdom hath founded the earth; by understanding hath he established the heavens" (3:19). Wisdom is a divine attribute on loan — that is why it can be a tree of life, not because wisdom earns life but because wisdom is life's own architecture. And Alma 42 supplies a constraint the near view could not: the tree was barred because eating it in a fallen state would have left "no space for repentance." Barring the tree was mercy — it created probation, which rules out one attractive misreading. Gripping wisdom is not a way to skip probation. It is what you hold during it.

One more thing you cannot see from an English page. In every Ashkenazi synagogue, as the Torah scroll is carried back to the ark, the congregation sings this verse and the one before it. The "her" being sung is not Lady Wisdom in the abstract — it is the Torah scroll in the cantor's arms. Two verses of this week's reading are, by a wide margin, the most frequently recited words in it — heard every week by people who could not tell you the chapter number.

Ponder the path of thy feet

Chapter 4 is the path chapter, and it is also the chapter where the teacher tells you where he got this. "I was my father's son, tender and only beloved in the sight of my mother. He taught me also" (4:3–4). Beloved first, taught second, in that order. Then 4:18: "the path of the just is as the shining light, that shineth more and more unto the perfect day," which Doctrine and Covenants 50:24 picks up almost verbatim and then explains — he that receiveth light and continueth in God receiveth more light. The revelation answers the question Proverbs leaves open: why does the path brighten?

Think deeply. Take the last two questions slowly; they are the ones with teeth.

  • Oaks reads "lean not unto thine own understanding" as a rebuke of overconfidence rather than of doubt. Where in your religious life are you most confidently speculating — and would you recognize it as speculation if someone else said it?
  • Proverbs 3 promises long life, full barns, and unafraid sleep. Name a specific promise in this chapter you have watched go unhonored in someone's life. What did you conclude about the promise, and what would it cost you to conclude something else?
  • The six outlooks produced nothing at all under "prophecy." What does it mean that a whole mode of reading scripture comes up empty on 333 verses of the Bible — and where else might you be applying a tool the text was never built for?
  • If wisdom is a tree you grip rather than a gift you receive, what have you already dropped? Not "what should you study more" — what did you have hold of and let go?
  • Proverbs 3:28 forbids delaying while solvent. Who is currently waiting on something you already have — money, an answer, an apology — and what is the actual reason for the delay?
  • The near view says "tree of life" here means a good long life in the land, not resurrection. Does the near view diminish the verse for you, or steady it? What is at stake for you in the answer?

20–25 minSession 3: A Soft Answer and a Just Weight

Reading assignment: Proverbs 15, 16

A different kind of book inside the same book

You are now out of the instruction speeches and into something structurally different. Chapters 10–29 are sentence literature: freestanding two-line sayings, mostly antithetical, arranged with only loose thematic clustering. There is no argument to follow here and no plot. There is a pile of observations, and the work of reading them is the work of noticing which ones are about you.

There is a live scholarly disagreement about what kind of material this is, and it matters more than it sounds. William McKane's 1970 commentary sorted the sayings into a secular, pragmatic "old wisdom" and a later Yahwistic class representing post-exilic religious reinterpretation; Katharine Dell argues that the theological interest was there from the start. If McKane is right, verses like 16:9 are a religious frame set later around practical folk observation. If Dell is right, the observation and the theology were never separable. Read 15:3, 15:11, 16:2, 16:9, and 16:33 with both options in mind and see which reading the text supports.

Your mouth

Proverbs 15 is a chapter about your mouth, and the manual asks directly whether this counsel applies to digital communication. One verse carries most of the weight: "The heart of the righteous studieth to answer: but the mouth of the wicked poureth out evil things" (15:28). One verb is deliberate, effortful, slow; the other is what a container does when you tip it. A reply box is built to make you pour. The practical form of righteousness here is unglamorous: write the reply, do not send it, come back in an hour.

Proverbs 15:1 is quoted more than it is obeyed, mostly because people misremember whose job it describes. "A soft answer turneth away wrath." The wrath already exists and belongs to the other person, and the text does not call it unjustified. The softness is yours, supplied to someone who has not earned it, which is why it costs something — and what it costs is the pleasure of being right out loud. Then 16:28: "a whisperer separateth chief friends." Note what the whisperer destroys. Not a reputation, a friendship. The mild, accurate observation you made about someone to a mutual friend did not damage him; it damaged what those two people have.

There is a tree in this chapter, too, and it is not where you expect: "A wholesome tongue is a tree of life" (15:4). This is the control case that keeps the tree-of-life image honest — nobody reads that as Eden. In Proverbs the phrase is a portable idiom meaning life-giving. Life-giving speech is itself the tree, which is very nearly the whole burden of Proverbs 15–16.

The weights in the bag

"A just weight and balance are the Lord's: all the weights of the bag are his work" (16:11). An ancient merchant carried his weights in a pouch, and a crooked one carried two sets — a heavy stone for buying, a light one for selling. The claim is startling: the calibration itself belongs to God. Your bag of weights is the timesheet, the expense report, the mileage log, the description of the car you are selling. The dishonesty available to most of us is never theft — it is a second set of weights, one for measuring ourselves and one for everyone else.

Which runs directly into 16:2: "All the ways of a man are clean in his own eyes; but the Lord weigheth the spirits." The problem is not ignorance; it is that the internal gauge reads clean. Or, put practically: you cannot audit your own pride, because pride is the instrument you would audit it with. This is why 15:22 is not filler — "Without counsel purposes are disappointed" — and why the real test of it is whether you can name three people you would actually let talk you out of your current plan, rather than three people you intend to inform.

The city nobody sees you take

"He that is slow to anger is better than the mighty; and he that ruleth his spirit than he that taketh a city" (16:32). The comparison is military on purpose. Taking a city is the most public achievement that world offers — gates, spoils, songs. Ruling your spirit has no audience; nobody will congratulate you for the sentence you did not say.

Mishlei is where the Jewish ethical tradition goes to school, and this verse is one of its schoolrooms: Pirkei Avot answers "Who is mighty?" with "one who conquers his impulse," and cites 16:32 for it. Note also the company the fear of the Lord keeps in these chapters — "The fear of the Lord is the instruction of wisdom; and before honour is humility" (15:33), and "By humility and the fear of the Lord are riches, and honour, and life" (22:4). It is paired with humility, not with dread.

Think deeply. These chapters are easy to admire and hard to obey; aim the questions at the second thing.

  • Proverbs 16:2 says your own eyes report clean. What is the mechanism by which you would ever discover a sin you are currently committing — and has that mechanism produced anything in the last year?
  • If a "soft answer" is something you supply to someone whose anger is real and possibly justified, when does softness become dishonesty? Where is the line between a soft answer and refusing to say the true hard thing?
  • Take the McKane–Dell disagreement seriously for ten minutes: read 16:1, 16:9, and 16:33 as if the theological line were added later to a secular saying. Does the verse mean something different? Does that change what you think inspiration is?
  • Name your second set of weights — the standard you apply to yourself but not to others, or the reverse. Be specific enough that the example would embarrass you if it were read aloud.
  • 16:32 says ruling your own spirit beats taking a city, and the whole point is that nobody watches you do it. What have you done well this month that no one will ever know about — and if the honest answer is "nothing," what does that tell you about what you are optimizing for?

25–30 minSession 4: The Promise and the Rebuttal

Reading assignment: Proverbs 22 · Ecclesiastes 1, 2

The Egyptian text in the room

Read Proverbs 22 straight through and you will feel a seam open at verse 17: "Bow down thine ear, and hear the words of the wise." That is a new collection's heading, and what follows it, running to 24:22, reads in stretches like an adaptation of the Egyptian Instruction of Amenemope, a Ramesside-period text published from a British Museum papyrus in 1923. The parallels in this chapter are dense — 22:22 on robbing the poor, 22:28 on removing the ancient landmark, 22:29 on the diligent man standing before kings. And there is a famous crux at 22:20, where the KJV's "excellent things" renders a genuinely difficult Hebrew word that Adolf Erman proposed reading as sheloshim, "thirty," matching Amenemope's explicit thirty chapters.

Get the state of the argument right. The mainstream position is that a real literary relationship exists, that Egyptian priority is likelier than the reverse, and that the Hebrew is an adaptation rather than a translation — and that anyone who tells you the case is closed is overstating. R. N. Whybray grew skeptical; John Ruffle argued the parallels are common property of advice literature; Emerton and Shupak answered the skeptics. For a Latter-day Saint this should land more softly than for almost anyone else: Restoration scripture is explicit that God grants to all nations to teach his word and rebukes the assumption that revelation belongs to one people. Whether inspiration can run through an editor who selects and reframes a neighbor's proverbs is the real question, and it is theological, not archaeological.

"Train up a child" — the verse that indicts parents

Proverbs 22:6: "Train up a child in the way he should go: and when he is old, he will not depart from it." For Latter-day Saints this verse is not merely biblical — Lehi quotes it nearly word for word in his dying blessing (2 Nephi 4:5), and then immediately hedges it, contemplating grandchildren cursed anyway. The verse arrives in Restoration scripture already under strain.

Two things sit under the English. There is no word for "should" in the Hebrew; the phrase is ʿal-pî darkô, "according to his way," and translators supplied the rest. Jewish educational tradition has argued about that for centuries: the Vilna Gaon reads it as the child's own way — each child has an innate bent, and education means channeling it rather than breaking it, since a child forced into someone else's way will comply only as long as he is afraid of you. And the genre matters: a proverb trades in generalizations, not warranties. Read as a promise, 22:6 becomes an accusation with your name in it — he departed, therefore you trained him wrong.

Elder David A. Bednar took this up directly in "Faithful Parents and Wayward Children," and his answer declines the easy comfort: popular interpretations of the statements about sealed parents securing their posterity have "contributed to a measure of doctrinal misunderstanding," and righteous parental influence "does not overrule the consequences of the unrighteous exercise of moral agency." That is honest. It also leaves Proverbs 22:6 standing there, unqualified, in the King James text the manual assigns. Keep that in mind for the second half of this session, where Qohelet asks about his own heir, "who knoweth whether he shall be a wise man or a fool?" (2:19).

The rod

Proverbs 22:15: "Foolishness is bound in the heart of a child; but the rod of correction shall drive it far from him." The Church's own Scripture Helps for this week flag the verse and answer it directly — these passages are best understood as figurative expressions about loving and consistent discipline, and must not be used to justify physical abuse. The empirical picture is not ambiguous either: Gershoff and Grogan-Kaylor's 2016 meta-analysis found spanking associated with detrimental outcomes on thirteen of seventeen measures and none in the beneficial direction.

The institutional and empirical answers are clear. The interpretive problem is narrower and remains — whether "the rod of correction" was originally a metaphor is a historical question, not a devotional one, and the companion material in Proverbs 23 does not read metaphorically. Readers land in three different places on that, and it is worth knowing that the manual's answer is a reading, not a translation. Then there is a narrower self-examination that no institutional statement can do for you: when you corrected your child this week, were you removing foolishness, or discharging your own day? Note that 15:18 does not exempt parents.

The rebuttal begins

Now turn the page into Ecclesiastes, and read chapters 1 and 2 in one sitting. The narrator adopts a Solomonic persona in 1:12 — "I the Preacher was king over Israel in Jerusalem," a past tense no living king would use — runs the experiment of chapter 2 at royal scale, and reports that wisdom's advantage over folly is real but bounded, because "one event happeneth to them all" (2:14). Solomonic authorship here is not merely doubted; it is linguistically impossible. The Hebrew is late, and one of the exhibits sits inside your reading: 2:5, "I made me gardens and orchards," where "orchards" is pardes, a Persian loanword and the ancestor of English paradise. Persia was not a lending culture for Hebrew in the tenth century BCE.

Everything in the book turns on one word. Hebel appears thirty-eight times, and the KJV renders it "vanity" — but literally it is vapor or breath, the visible puff on a cold morning, the same word that names Abel. Fox translates it "absurd," meaning the mismatch between what ought to follow and what does; Seow takes it as what exceeds human grasp; others prefer "fleeting" or "enigma." The manual's gloss, "temporary and often unimportant," gets the first half right and adds a second half the Hebrew will not carry. Qohelet does not say the world is unimportant. He says it will not hold still.

Read 2:18–21 as the counterweight to Proverbs 22:6. "I hated all my labour which I had taken under the sun: because I should leave it unto the man that shall be after me. And who knoweth whether he shall be a wise man or a fool?" Qohelet's vertigo is the dread of having built something whose future you do not control — the calling you will be released from, the business you sell, the child who is his own agent. Against it, the book's first carpe diem refrain: eat, drink, make your soul enjoy good in your labor, "This also I saw, that it was from the hand of God" (2:24). That is not a consolation prize; it is phrased as a duty, and refusing to enjoy your work is refusing a gift.

One more line deserves a Restoration footnote before you close the session. Ecclesiastes 1:9 — "there is no new thing under the sun" — sounds like the opposite of a Restoration claim until you notice what the Restoration actually claims: not novelty but restoration, a welding together of dispensations. What Qohelet misses shows up two verses later — "There is no remembrance of former things" (1:11). Amnesia, not repetition, is the condition the Restoration addresses.

Think deeply. This is the week's hardest sitting. Do not resolve it too quickly.

  • Proverbs 22:6 and Ecclesiastes 2:19 are in the same week's reading and give opposite answers about whether you control who comes after you. If you had to preach one of them to a grieving parent, which — and what would you do with the other one?
  • The Hebrew has no word for "should" in 22:6. If the verse means "according to his own way," does it become a promise about formation or an instruction about attention? Which reading is harder on you as a parent, teacher, or leader?
  • The rod sayings in Proverbs 23 do not read metaphorically. Steel-man the position that the text is simply wrong here and that saying so costs a believer nothing. Then steel-man the position that it does cost something. Which argument do you find yourself resisting, and why?
  • If a Judahite scribe adapted Egyptian instruction into Proverbs 22, what does that do to your working definition of revelation? Can inspiration operate through selection and reframing rather than dictation — and if it can there, where else?
  • Qohelet ran the experiment with more resources than you will ever have and concluded that the wise man and the fool meet the same end. What, specifically, is your answer to him? Not the Sunday School answer — the one you actually live by.
  • Ecclesiastes 2:24 frames enjoyment as a gift you can refuse. When did you last enjoy your work without immediately itemizing what was wrong with it, and are you willing to read your refusal as disobedience?

25–30 minSession 5: The Work of Your Hands and the Time You Are Given

Reading assignment: Proverbs 31 · Ecclesiastes 3 · Ecclesiastes 11, 12

The most misread chapter in Proverbs

Proverbs 31 is two things stitched together. Verses 1–9 are a queen mother's instruction to a king — sobriety in leadership, and "Open thy mouth, judge righteously, and plead the cause of the poor and needy" (31:9). Verses 10–31 are an acrostic: twenty-two lines, each beginning with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet. That is a formal, composed, self-consciously artificial poem — a completeness device, an A-to-Z — not observational advice and not a list.

Start with the word. The KJV's "virtuous woman" translates 'ēšet ḥayil, and ḥayil is a martial and economic word: force, valor, capability, wealth. A gibbôr ḥayil is a warrior. Read as a heroic hymn, what the poem praises snaps into focus. She evaluates a field and buys it (31:16). She runs a textile operation and sells to merchants (31:24). She provisions her household like trading ships (31:14). Her husband's standing "in the gates" is presented as downstream of her competence. And placed at the end of a book that opened with Wisdom crying in the streets, she is that figure brought down to earth — the last line has her praised in the gates, the public square where Wisdom first spoke. Note also what the poem declines to praise and what it never mentions: her silence.

Now the honest part. For many women this is the passage that has cost them the most. Read as a job description — real estate, a business, a household fed before dawn, a candle that does not go out at night — it is a machine for manufacturing inadequacy. The acrostic reframing helps, but it does not dissolve the harder problem: a woman reading Proverbs 31 is reading a description of herself written for someone else's use. And the poem's own closing instruction is not that she be emulated but that she be paid and publicly credited — "Give her of the fruit of her hands." The lesson's prompt converts a hymn into a performance review. The genre will not bear it.

Jewish practice has kept the poem in a different key entirely. It is sung on Friday night before Kiddush in a great many homes, and the custom comes out of the kabbalistic circle of Safed, where its original referent was not the woman of the house at all but the Shekhinah, the Shabbat Queen welcomed at the threshold. Midrash Mishlei reads eshet ḥayil as the Torah itself, then as a sequence of biblical women, and it embeds the story permanently attached to this chapter: R. Meir's two sons die on Shabbat, and his wife hides it from him until the day is out, then asks him a question of law about a deposit that must be returned — and then shows him the bodies. The chapter the Church reads as a portrait of domestic industry is, in the Jewish imagination, attached to a woman's terrible composure. Either way, praise offered, not a standard imposed.

A time to every purpose

Ecclesiastes 3:1–8 is read at funerals as comfort, and it is not comfort. Its point is stated in 3:11: God "hath made every thing beautiful in his time," and has also made it so "that no man can find out the work that God maketh from the beginning to the end." There are right times, and we cannot see them. The Hebrew behind KJV "world" in that verse is ha-olam, which most modern versions render "eternity" — a sense of the whole span planted in a creature equipped to see only its own stretch of it. Read as a schedule rather than a poem, 3:1–8 is severe: "a time to keep silence, and a time to speak" means both are wrong at the wrong time. What are you still doing because it was right two years ago?

Then 3:19–21, the passage that stops Latter-day Saint readers cold: humans and beasts have one breath and one end, and "who knoweth the spirit of man that goeth upward?" The Bible Dictionary's answer is the "under the sun" frame — these are observations about how things appear from a worldly point of view — and it is a coherent reading, and it is worth being clear that it is an interpretive frame imposed on the text rather than something the text announces about itself. Jewish commentators do something different with the same verse: Rashi reads mi yodea not as a question about the beyond but as a summons, and Ibn Ezra as a limit on human knowledge. Neither offers the afterlife as a solution to Qohelet's problem, which is what Christian readers most consistently supply from outside. Qohelet's own answer is ḥelek — portion — and simḥah, and both sit firmly under the sun.

Sow in the morning

Chapter 11 is the practical center of the book, and for anyone in a hard season it is the most usable thing in these thirteen chapters. "Cast thy bread upon the waters." "Give a portion to seven, and also to eight; for thou knowest not what evil shall be upon the earth." Then the diagnosis of paralysis: "He that observeth the wind shall not sow; and he that regardeth the clouds shall not reap" (11:4). Then the instruction: "In the morning sow thy seed, and in the evening withhold not thine hand: for thou knowest not whether shall prosper, either this or that" (11:6). Ecclesiastes refuses to promise outcomes and then tells you to act anyway — twice a day, without a forecast. The not-knowing is given as the reason to do both, not as a reason to do neither.

Jewish practice puts the whole book in that key. In the dominant Ashkenazi custom Kohelet is read publicly on Sukkot, the festival the liturgy calls the season of our rejoicing. The most disenchanted book in the Bible is read in a hut with a leaky roof during the most joyful week of the year — the sukkah and Kohelet say the same thing about impermanence, and Sukkot's answer is not to deny it but to eat in it. That is also why "vanity" misleads: hevel is vapor, breath — not worthless but transient, and structurally so.

The body, the spirit, and the last word

Ecclesiastes 12:1–7 is an allegory of aging — trembling keepers of the house, grinders ceasing because they are few, darkened windows, the almond tree white, the grasshopper a burden — ending with "the dust return to the earth as it was: and the spirit shall return unto God who gave it" (12:7). The load-bearing word in verse 1 is now. The passage is not saying old age is too late; it is saying that the person you have been planning to become is someone you become by Thursday or not at all.

Latter-day Saints regularly cite 12:7 for premortal existence. It is worth knowing that Dana M. Pike, writing for BYU's Religious Studies Center, argues the Hebrew rûaḥ here means life-breath rather than an individual spirit personage, with the book's own usage behind him — 3:19 says humans and animals have "all one breath," the same word. Pike is a believing Latter-day Saint making a philological argument that cuts against a beloved proof-text, which is what serious scholarship inside a faith tradition looks like. What the Restoration does supply is a before and an after: Alma 40:11 answers 3:21's question in the vocabulary of 12:7 and grounds it in something Qohelet never claimed — an angel. Ecclesiastes is not refuted by the Restoration; it is bounded by it. And a reader hoping the Joseph Smith Translation will settle the Proverbs–Ecclesiastes tension should know in advance that they will not find help there: the manuscript records only terse scribal notations for Proverbs and leaves Ecclesiastes untouched.

Finally, the ending the manual leans on. At 12:8 the opening motto returns and Qohelet's first-person voice stops; at 12:9 someone else speaks about him in the third person, and that voice delivers "Fear God, and keep his commandments." Whether the epilogue is a later hand domesticating a dangerous book or an authorial frame is a standing question, and the Church's own Scripture Helps note that many scholars regard 12:9–14 as a later addition. Either way, the verse the manual leans on is spoken by a voice that is not Qohelet's — a reader's verdict on the book, embedded in the book. And the Hebrew of 12:13 has no word for "duty": ki zeh kol ha-adam, "this is the whole of the human being," or "this applies to all humankind." The verse is either a definition of what a person is or a statement of universal scope, and the tradition has never fully chosen.

The sages nearly did not let you have this book — Mishnah Yadayim preserves the dispute over whether Kohelet "defiles the hands," and Shabbat 30b says the sages sought to suppress both Kohelet and Mishlei and resolved to keep them. Both books survive on the same reasoning. That is a canon formed by argument, not acclamation. And one last practice tells you how the tradition felt about ending on judgment: in the public reading of Kohelet, the penultimate verse is repeated after the last, so the book does not close on a hard word. The book is allowed its judgment. It is simply not permitted the final word.

Think deeply. End the week with these; two of them are the week's real exam.

  • The Proverbs 31 poem ends by telling the community to pay her and credit her publicly, not by telling women to match her. What would it change in your ward, home, or workplace if you took that as the assignment instead?
  • Jewish tradition sings this chapter to the Shekhinah, the Torah, and a wife on the same Friday night without deciding among them. What is gained by a reading that refuses to pick one referent — and what is lost?
  • Ecclesiastes 3:1–8 read as a schedule says both silence and speech are wrong at the wrong time, and gives you no way to tell which season you are in. How do you actually decide? Name the last time you got the season wrong.
  • Chapter 11 tells you to sow morning and evening precisely because you cannot know which will prosper. Where has not-knowing become your reason to do nothing — the ministering visit that never goes well, the call that is not returned?
  • Pike argues that 12:7 is about life-breath, not a premortal spirit. If a favorite proof-text turns out not to prove what you have always cited it for, what happens to the doctrine — and what does your answer reveal about how your belief is actually held together?
  • The tradition will not let Ecclesiastes end on judgment, and reads 12:13 again after 12:14. Having read both books this week, which one do you want to have the last word — and is that a judgment about the texts or about yourself?
